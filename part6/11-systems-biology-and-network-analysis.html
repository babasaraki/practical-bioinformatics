11  Biological networks and pathway analysis

After working through this chapter you will be able to

  • distinguish protein–protein interaction, regulatory, metabolic and co-expression networks by the evidence that supports an edge in each
  • compute degree, betweenness, clustering coefficient, shortest paths and components on a real interaction network with igraph
  • judge what a degree distribution does and does not establish about network architecture
  • detect modules with two community-detection algorithms and compare their answers honestly
  • draw a network so that it can be read, and recognise when a drawing has stopped carrying information
  • implement over-representation analysis with the hypergeometric test and control the false discovery rate
  • explain why a significant enrichment term is a hypothesis rather than a finding

A differential expression analysis ends with a list: four hundred genes, each with a fold change and an adjusted p-value computed by a tool such as DESeq2 (Love, Huber, and Anders 2014). The list is a measurement, not an explanation. Nothing in it says which of those genes act together, which are upstream of which, or whether the four hundred represent one disturbed process or nine unrelated ones. The work of this chapter is to take that kind of list and place it in a structure — a network, a pathway, an annotation hierarchy — that can support an interpretation, while being candid about how much of that structure is measurement and how much is inference from the literature.

Two bundled datasets carry the chapter. The first, data/networks/p53_string_network.tsv, is a high-confidence slice of the STRING database (Szklarczyk et al. 2022) covering 30 human cell-cycle and DNA-damage genes, with each interaction broken down into the separate evidence channels that produced it. The second, data/annotation/human_go_annotations.tsv, is a UniProtKB export (Bateman et al. 2022) of 20,431 reviewed human proteins and their Gene Ontology terms, which serves as the background universe for enrichment testing.

By the end you will have measured the topology of a real network, partitioned it into modules with two algorithms that disagree, written an over-representation test from first principles rather than calling a package, corrected it for multiple testing, implemented the running-sum statistic that underlies gene set enrichment analysis, and then taken the resulting table apart to see which of its 511 significant terms survive contact with a more careful background choice.

11.2 What a network adds to a gene list

A gene list is a set. A network is a set plus a relation, and almost every question worth asking about a group of genes is a question about the relation. Are these genes connected to each other more than chance would predict? Do they cluster into separable groups? Is there a small number of genes through which most of the connections run? Which genes, not in the list, sit between the ones that are?

That last question is the one that most often changes an experiment. If 20 genes from a 400-gene list all connect to a single transcription factor that is itself not differentially expressed, the factor becomes a candidate even though no measurement flagged it. This is the characteristic move of network biology: using known relations to reach the entities a measurement did not see, because their activity changed without their messenger RNA (mRNA) abundance changing.

The move has a cost, and it is worth stating before the first line of code. A measurement — a read count, a peak height — is an observation of the system under study. An edge in a public interaction network is an observation about a different system, usually a cell line, under different conditions, reported by somebody else, sometimes inferred rather than observed at all. When you put your measurement into a network, you are mixing two kinds of knowledge with very different error structures, and the network’s errors are mostly invisible to you. Everything else in this chapter follows from taking that seriously.

Systems biology, understood narrowly, is the attempt to explain a phenotype by the behaviour of an interacting set of components rather than by any one component. Understood broadly, it is simply the admission that genes do not act alone. The narrow version demands quantitative models — rate constants, differential equations, perturbation data — and is out of scope here. The broad version is where most practical bioinformatics lives, and its tools are graphs and annotation databases.

11.3 Kinds of network and the evidence behind an edge

Four classes of network cover most of what you will meet. They look identical once loaded into igraph (Csardi and Nepusz 2006), which is exactly the problem, because their edges mean entirely different things.

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network has proteins as nodes and physical association as edges. “Physical association” conceals a range of evidence: a solved co-crystal structure, a yeast two-hybrid positive, an affinity-purification mass spectrometry (AP-MS) co-precipitation, or a sentence in an abstract saying two proteins “interact”. These have wildly different false positive rates, and high-throughput screens are known to be noisy in both directions.

A gene regulatory network has directed edges: transcription factor to target. Evidence ranges from a ChIP-seq peak near a promoter — which shows binding, not regulation — to a reporter assay with mutated binding sites, which shows regulation in one construct in one cell type. Direction is the valuable part and the part most often asserted from correlation.

A metabolic network is the best-grounded of the four. Nodes are metabolites and edges are enzyme-catalysed reactions with a stoichiometry established by biochemistry, often decades ago. Its uncertainty lies not in whether the reaction exists but in whether a given organism runs it under given conditions.

A co-expression network has an edge wherever two genes’ expression profiles correlate above a threshold across samples. Nothing physical is claimed. The edges are a property of the expression dataset, and change when you change the samples, the normalisation or the correlation cut-off.

A PPI network is not a measurement of your system

Interaction databases are aggregations of published results, and publication is not a random sample of biology. A protein studied by two hundred laboratories will have hundreds of recorded partners; an equally important protein studied by two will have three. Degree in a PPI network is therefore partly a measurement of scientific attention. You will quantify this bias later in the chapter.

The STRING subnetwork bundled with the book makes this visible, because STRING keeps its evidence channels separate instead of reporting only a combined score.

string_edges <- read_tsv(book_data("networks", "p53_string_network.tsv"),
                         show_col_types = FALSE)

cat("edges:", nrow(string_edges),
    " combined score range:", min(string_edges$score), "-",
    max(string_edges$score), "\n")
#> edges: 210  combined score range: 0.701 - 0.999
names(string_edges)
#>  [1] "stringId_A"      "stringId_B"      "preferredName_A" "preferredName_B"
#>  [5] "ncbiTaxonId"     "score"           "nscore"          "fscore"         
#>  [9] "pscore"          "ascore"          "escore"          "dscore"         
#> [13] "tscore"

The columns from nscore to tscore are the individual channels: genomic neighbourhood, gene fusion, phylogenetic co-occurrence, co-expression, experiments, curated databases and text mining. Each is a probability-like score between 0 and 1, and the combined score integrates them. Counting how many edges each channel supports tells you what this network is actually made of.

channels <- c(neighbourhood = "nscore", fusion = "fscore",
              cooccurrence = "pscore", coexpression = "ascore",
              experiments = "escore", database = "dscore",
              textmining = "tscore")

support <- sapply(channels, function(col) sum(string_edges[[col]] > 0))
print(support)
#> neighbourhood        fusion  cooccurrence  coexpression   experiments 
#>             0             0             4           123           176 
#>      database    textmining 
#>            85           210

# Edges with no experimental and no curated-database support at all.
cat("edges resting on inference alone:",
    sum(string_edges$escore == 0 & string_edges$dscore == 0), "\n")
#> edges resting on inference alone: 26

Every one of the 210 edges has a text-mining score above zero, 176 have experimental support and only 85 appear in a curated database. Twenty-six edges rest on no experimental or curated evidence whatsoever: they exist because two gene names co-occur in the literature and because the genes are co-expressed. Those 26 edges are not wrong, but they are a different kind of claim from a co-crystal structure, and a result that depends on them is correspondingly weaker. Filtering by individual channel rather than by combined score is often the right first move, and is one line of dplyr: filter(escore >= 0.4) keeps the experimentally supported subnetwork.

Record the confidence threshold with the result

STRING’s default web threshold is 0.400 (medium confidence); this bundle used 0.700 (high). The number of edges roughly doubles between the two, and so does every topology statistic computed from them. A network figure without a stated score threshold cannot be reproduced or compared.

11.4 Graph vocabulary and the shape of a real network

A graph is a set of nodes (also called vertices) and a set of edges joining pairs of them. An edge may be directed or undirected, and may carry a weight. The degree of a node is the number of edges touching it. A path is a sequence of edges leading from one node to another, and the shortest path between two nodes is the one with fewest edges; its length is the distance. A component is a maximal set of nodes all reachable from each other. The density of a graph is the fraction of possible edges that are present. These seven terms carry most of the chapter.

p53_net <- string_edges |>
  select(preferredName_A, preferredName_B) |>
  graph_from_data_frame(directed = FALSE) |>
  simplify()  # drop duplicate edges and any self-loops

cat("nodes:", vcount(p53_net), " edges:", ecount(p53_net), "\n")
#> nodes: 30  edges: 210
cat("density:", round(edge_density(p53_net), 3),
    " mean degree:", mean(degree(p53_net)), "\n")
#> density: 0.483  mean degree: 14
cat("components:", components(p53_net)$no,
    " largest:", max(components(p53_net)$csize), "\n")
#> components: 1  largest: 30

Thirty nodes, 210 edges, a single connected component and a density of 0.483: nearly half of all possible pairs are joined. That is extraordinarily dense for a biological network, and the reason is the sampling, not the biology. These 30 genes were chosen as a coherent functional group and then queried against a database that already knows they belong together. A query-driven subnetwork is not a random sample of the interactome and must never be described as one.

Distances in such a graph are short by construction.

cat("diameter:", diameter(p53_net), "\n")
#> diameter: 2
cat("mean shortest path:", round(mean_distance(p53_net), 3), "\n")
#> mean shortest path: 1.517
cat("global clustering coefficient:",
    round(transitivity(p53_net, type = "global"), 3), "\n")
#> global clustering coefficient: 0.646
cat("mean local clustering coefficient:",
    round(transitivity(p53_net, type = "average"), 3), "\n")
#> mean local clustering coefficient: 0.726

shortest_paths(p53_net, from = "LZTR1", to = "BAX", output = "vpath")$vpath[[1]]
#> + 3/30 vertices, named, from d94d007:
#> [1] LZTR1 TP53  BAX

The diameter is two: every gene reaches every other in at most two steps. The clustering coefficient measures how often a node’s neighbours are themselves neighbours — formally, the fraction of connected triples that close into triangles. At 0.646 globally this network is highly cliquish, which again reflects the curated selection of genes. A genome-scale PPI network typically has a mean distance near four and a clustering coefficient around 0.1.

The degree distribution is where the most common over-claim in network biology lives. Figure 11.1 shows it two ways: as a histogram, and as the complementary cumulative distribution on logarithmic axes, which is the plot people use when arguing that a network is scale-free.

node_degree <- degree(p53_net)

hist_panel <- ggplot(data.frame(k = node_degree), aes(k)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 2, fill = book_palette[1], colour = "white") +
  labs(x = "Degree k", y = "Number of genes", title = "Degree distribution")

ccdf <- data.frame(k = sort(unique(node_degree))) |>
  mutate(p_ge_k = sapply(k, function(x) mean(node_degree >= x)))

ccdf_panel <- ggplot(ccdf, aes(k, p_ge_k)) +
  geom_point(colour = book_palette[4], size = 2) +
  scale_x_log10() + scale_y_log10() +
  labs(x = "Degree k (log scale)", y = "P(K >= k)", title = "Cumulative tail")

patchwork::wrap_plots(hist_panel, ccdf_panel)

Read the right-hand panel carefully. It contains 17 points. A straight line through 17 points on log–log axes is not evidence of a power law, and the claim that biological networks are scale-free — that the probability of degree k falls as k raised to some negative power, producing a few enormous hubs — has been tested far more loosely than its popularity suggests (Broido and Clauset 2019). Fitting a power law requires hundreds of nodes, a maximum-likelihood estimate of the exponent, a lower cut-off chosen by the data rather than by eye, and a goodness-of-fit test against alternatives such as the log-normal. None of that is possible here, and saying “the network is scale-free” on the strength of a plot like this one is a claim your reader cannot check.

What survives is weaker and more useful: degree is unevenly distributed, with a handful of genes far above the mean of 14 and several at the bottom. That unevenness is worth investigating regardless of what functional form generates it.

11.5 Centrality, hubs and study bias

Centrality is any measure of how important a node is to the structure of a graph, and the choice of measure encodes what you mean by important. Degree counts connections. Betweenness counts how many shortest paths between other pairs of nodes pass through a node, identifying bridges between otherwise separate regions. Closeness measures how near a node is to all others on average.

centrality <- data.frame(
  gene        = names(node_degree),
  degree      = as.integer(node_degree),
  betweenness = round(betweenness(p53_net, normalized = TRUE), 3),
  closeness   = round(closeness(p53_net, normalized = TRUE), 3)
) |> arrange(desc(degree))

head(centrality, 8)
#>          gene degree betweenness closeness
#> TP53     TP53     29       0.170     1.000
#> BRCA1   BRCA1     22       0.037     0.806
#> MDM2     MDM2     21       0.034     0.784
#> PTEN     PTEN     21       0.034     0.784
#> MYC       MYC     21       0.034     0.784
#> CDKN2A CDKN2A     20       0.033     0.763
#> CDKN1A CDKN1A     19       0.020     0.744
#> AKT1     AKT1     19       0.025     0.744

TP53 has degree 29 in a 30-node graph: it is joined to everything. Its normalised betweenness, 0.170, is five times that of the next gene, so it also lies on most shortest paths. In a network assembled by querying a database for p53-associated genes, this is not a discovery. It is the query, returned.

The deeper problem is that hub status in any literature-derived network partly measures how much a gene has been studied. The bundled GO annotation file gives a direct proxy for study intensity — the number of GO terms a gene carries — and the relationship is strong.

go_annotations <- read_tsv(book_data("annotation", "human_go_annotations.tsv"),
                           show_col_types = FALSE)

gene_to_go <- go_annotations |>
  filter(!is.na(`Gene Ontology IDs`), !is.na(`Gene Names (primary)`)) |>
  transmute(gene = `Gene Names (primary)`, go = `Gene Ontology IDs`) |>
  separate_longer_delim(go, delim = "; ") |>
  distinct()

terms_per_gene <- count(gene_to_go, gene, name = "n_go")
bias <- inner_join(centrality, terms_per_gene, by = "gene")

cat("Spearman correlation of degree with GO-term count:",
    round(cor(bias$degree, bias$n_go, method = "spearman"), 3), "\n")
#> Spearman correlation of degree with GO-term count: 0.746
cat("median GO terms, all human proteins:", median(terms_per_gene$n_go),
    " in this network:", median(bias$n_go), "\n")
#> median GO terms, all human proteins: 9  in this network: 54.5

The rank correlation between degree and annotation count is 0.746. Genes in this network carry a median of 54.5 GO terms against a genome-wide median of nine. Two explanations compete: important genes attract both interactions and annotations, or well-studied genes accumulate both. Both are partly true, and no analysis of the network alone can separate them.

Report hubs as candidates, never as conclusions

“We identified TP53 as a hub and therefore a key regulator” is a sentence that cannot be falsified by the data that produced it. The defensible version names the hub, states the evidence channels behind its edges, acknowledges the literature bias, and proposes the experiment that would settle it — a knockdown, a degron, a dose–response.

11.6 Finding modules

A community or module is a group of nodes with more edges inside the group than to the rest of the network. Modularity, written Q, scores a partition by comparing its within-group edge count to the count expected if edges were rewired at random; Q above roughly 0.3 is usually taken as meaningful structure. Community detection algorithms search for partitions with high Q.

Two popular algorithms take different routes. The Louvain method (Blondel et al. 2008) moves nodes between communities greedily and then collapses communities into nodes, repeating until Q stops improving. Fast greedy modularity optimisation instead merges the pair of communities that most increases Q, building a dendrogram and cutting it at the maximum. Running both on the same graph is the minimum honest practice.

set.seed(20250101)
louvain  <- cluster_louvain(p53_net)
greedy   <- cluster_fast_greedy(p53_net)
walktrap <- cluster_walktrap(p53_net)

partitions <- list(Louvain = louvain, FastGreedy = greedy, Walktrap = walktrap)
for (method in names(partitions)) {
  result <- partitions[[method]]
  cat(method, ": k =", length(result),
      " Q =", round(modularity(result), 3),
      " sizes =", paste(sizes(result), collapse = ","), "\n")
}
#> Louvain : k = 3  Q = 0.137  sizes = 11,13,6 
#> FastGreedy : k = 3  Q = 0.135  sizes = 12,7,11 
#> Walktrap : k = 4  Q = 0.109  sizes = 15,4,10,1

Louvain finds three communities with Q = 0.137; fast greedy also finds three, with Q = 0.135; walktrap finds four with Q = 0.109. All three values are far below 0.3. The correct conclusion is that this network has no strong modular structure — which is what you should expect from a dense, hand-picked set of genes that all participate in one broad process. The algorithms still return a partition, because they always do. An algorithm that cannot say “no structure here” will never say it.

How much do the two best partitions agree? The adjusted Rand index compares two clusterings, scoring 1 for identical partitions and about 0 for the agreement expected by chance.

cat("adjusted Rand index, Louvain vs fast greedy:",
    round(compare(louvain, greedy, method = "adjusted.rand"), 3), "\n")
#> adjusted Rand index, Louvain vs fast greedy: 0.7
table(Louvain = membership(louvain), FastGreedy = membership(greedy))
#>        FastGreedy
#> Louvain  1  2  3
#>       1 10  1  0
#>       2  2  0 11
#>       3  0  6  0

An adjusted Rand index of 0.700 means substantial but incomplete agreement: the cross-tabulation shows one 10-gene core assigned together by both methods, an 11-gene group likewise, and three genes that move. Those three genes are the interesting ones — they are the genes whose module membership is an artefact of the algorithm, and no biological story should be built on them.

A third algorithm illustrates how badly the choice can go. The Girvan–Newman method (Girvan and Newman 2002), available as cluster_edge_betweenness(), removes the highest-betweenness edge repeatedly and is elegant but quadratic in the number of edges. On this dense graph it returns 20 communities, 19 of them singletons, with Q = 0.009. Dense graphs defeat it, and the method suits sparse networks with genuine bottlenecks instead.

11.7 Drawing a network that can be read

Network figures fail in a characteristic way. Past a few hundred edges, a force-directed layout produces an undifferentiated mass — a “hairball” — in which every node is adjacent to a dark region and nothing can be traced. The failure is not aesthetic. A hairball transmits no information, so a figure that is one is a figure that should have been a table.

Figure 11.2 makes the comparison directly. The left panel places the same 30 nodes at random; the right panel uses the Fruchterman–Reingold force-directed layout, sizes nodes by degree and colours them by Louvain community.

module_colour <- book_palette[membership(louvain)]

par(mfrow = c(1, 2), mar = c(0.5, 0.5, 2, 0.5))

set.seed(20250101)
plot(p53_net, layout = layout_randomly, vertex.label = NA,
     vertex.size = 6, vertex.color = "grey70",
     edge.color = "grey75", main = "Random layout")

set.seed(20250101)
plot(p53_net, layout = layout_with_fr,
     vertex.size = 4 + 0.6 * node_degree,
     vertex.color = module_colour, vertex.frame.color = "white",
     vertex.label.cex = 0.6, vertex.label.color = "black",
     vertex.label.family = "sans",
     edge.color = adjustcolor("grey60", alpha.f = 0.5),
     main = "Force-directed, sized and coloured")

The right-hand panel is legible only because there are 30 nodes. Three habits keep larger networks readable. First, filter before drawing: raise the confidence threshold, keep the largest component, or show only the subgraph induced by the genes you will discuss. Second, encode at most two variables visually — size for one quantity, colour for another — and put everything else in a table. Third, label selectively; a label on every node is a label on none. When filtering cannot rescue the figure, present the topology as numbers and show only the subnetwork around the genes under discussion.

11.8 Pathway resources and over-representation analysis

Three resources dominate functional interpretation. The Gene Ontology (GO) is a structured vocabulary of terms in three namespaces — biological process, molecular function and cellular component — arranged as a directed acyclic graph in which a term implies all its ancestors (Ashburner et al. 2000; Aleksander et al. 2023). KEGG curates metabolic and signalling pathway maps with defined reactions and topology (Kanehisa 2000). Reactome curates reactions at finer granularity, with explicit inputs, outputs, catalysts and literature evidence for each step (Milacic et al. 2023). GO annotates genes with terms; KEGG and Reactome place gene products inside a described mechanism. GO has the widest coverage, Reactome the most explicit evidence trail.

Over-representation analysis (ORA) asks a single question: given a background universe of genes, a gene set defined by an annotation term, and a query list, are more query genes annotated to that term than you would expect by chance? The exact test is the hypergeometric distribution — the distribution of the number of white balls drawn when sampling n balls without replacement from an urn holding K white and N − K black. Here N is the universe, K the term’s size, n the query list size and k the observed overlap.

The annotation table must first be reshaped from one row per protein to one row per gene–term pair — the form every enrichment tool uses internally. The separate_longer_delim() call in the previous section did exactly this, and the tidyverse idiom generalises to any semicolon-delimited annotation column (Wickham et al. 2019).

universe <- unique(gene_to_go$gene)
term_sizes <- count(gene_to_go, go, name = "K")

cat("universe:", length(universe), "genes;",
    nrow(term_sizes), "distinct GO terms;",
    nrow(gene_to_go), "gene-term pairs\n")
#> universe: 19248 genes; 17553 distinct GO terms; 247239 gene-term pairs

The query list is the 30 network genes, all of which map to the universe. The test itself is one call to phyper(). Note the k - 1 and lower.tail = FALSE: you want P(X ≥ k), and phyper(q, ...) with the upper tail returns P(X > q).

query_genes <- V(p53_net)$name
n_query <- length(query_genes)
N <- length(universe)

enrichment <- gene_to_go |>
  filter(gene %in% query_genes) |>
  count(go, name = "k") |>
  inner_join(term_sizes, by = "go") |>
  filter(K >= 5, K <= 2000) |>          # drop terms too small or too vague to test
  mutate(expected = round(n_query * K / N, 2),
         p_value  = phyper(k - 1, K, N - K, n_query, lower.tail = FALSE),
         p_adj    = p.adjust(p_value, method = "BH")) |>
  arrange(p_value)

cat("terms tested:", nrow(enrichment),
    " significant at BH < 0.05:", sum(enrichment$p_adj < 0.05), "\n")
#> terms tested: 830  significant at BH < 0.05: 511
head(enrichment, 6)
#> # A tibble: 6 x 6
#>   go             k     K expected  p_value    p_adj
#>   <chr>      <int> <int>    <dbl>    <dbl>    <dbl>
#> 1 GO:0042802    21  1744     2.72 7.23e-16 6.00e-13
#> 2 GO:0006974    12   303     0.47 1.25e-14 5.19e-12
#> 3 GO:0008630     7    39     0.06 1.56e-13 4.32e-11
#> 4 GO:0032991    13   641     1    3.91e-12 8.11e-10
#> 5 GO:0090399     5    13     0.02 8.26e-12 1.37e- 9
#> 6 GO:0051726     9   195     0.3  1.11e-11 1.54e- 9

That is over-representation analysis in its entirety. A package such as clusterProfiler (Yu et al. 2012) adds term-name lookup, ontology-aware filtering, plotting and support for many organisms, but the statistic underneath is the call you just made. Knowing this matters when a tool and your own calculation disagree: the difference will be in the universe, the term-size filter or the ontology propagation, never in the distribution.

Two filtering decisions were made above and both are conventional rather than principled. Terms with fewer than five annotated genes produce unstable estimates and dominate the tail of small p-values for trivial reasons. Terms with more than 2,000 genes — “protein binding”, “cytoplasm” — are too vague to interpret even when significant. State both bounds in your methods; they change the answer.

Correcting for multiple testing

Testing 830 terms at a 5% threshold would be expected to yield about 42 false positives by chance alone. The Bonferroni correction divides the threshold by the number of tests, controlling the probability of even one false positive but discarding real signal. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure instead controls the false discovery rate (FDR): the expected proportion of false positives among the terms you call significant (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). For exploratory enrichment, where the output is a list of hypotheses rather than a single decision, FDR control is the right trade-off; p.adjust(method = "BH") applies it.

cat("raw p < 0.05:        ", sum(enrichment$p_value < 0.05), "\n")
#> raw p < 0.05:         617
cat("Benjamini-Hochberg:  ", sum(enrichment$p_adj < 0.05), "\n")
#> Benjamini-Hochberg:   511
cat("Bonferroni:          ",
    sum(p.adjust(enrichment$p_value, "bonferroni") < 0.05), "\n")
#> Bonferroni:           82

617 terms pass uncorrected, 511 survive FDR control and 82 survive Bonferroni. 11.9 Rank-based enrichment

ORA requires a cut-off. Genes are in the list or out of it, and a gene just below the threshold contributes nothing while its neighbour just above contributes fully. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) removes the cut-off by using the whole ranked list (Subramanian et al. 2005). Walk down the ranking from the top; add to a running sum when you meet a member of the gene set, subtract when you meet a non-member. If the set’s members are concentrated near the top, the running sum climbs to a large positive peak before falling back. That peak is the enrichment score (ES).

The function is short enough to write out, which is the point of writing it out.

running_enrichment <- function(ranked_stat, set_members) {
  is_hit <- names(ranked_stat) %in% set_members
  weights <- abs(ranked_stat)
  hit_sum  <- cumsum(ifelse(is_hit, weights, 0)) / sum(weights[is_hit])
  miss_sum <- cumsum(ifelse(is_hit, 0, 1)) / sum(!is_hit)
  hit_sum - miss_sum
}

A ranking is needed. In a real analysis it would be a differential expression statistic across every measured gene. Here, to keep every number in the chapter computable from bundled data, the 30 network genes are ranked by degree and tested against the p53-binding gene set. This is a demonstration of mechanics on a deliberately small list, not an analysis anyone would publish.

ranked_by_degree <- sort(node_degree, decreasing = TRUE)

Two differences from ORA matter. GSEA uses every gene, so a set whose members are all modestly shifted in the same direction can be significant even though no single member passes a differential expression threshold — a pattern ORA cannot see. And the null distribution is built by permutation rather than from a closed form, which means the result depends on what you permute. Shuffling gene labels, as above, asks whether this set is more concentrated at the top than a random set of the same size — a competitive null. Shuffling sample labels and recomputing the statistic asks whether the set responds to the treatment — a self-contained null, and the one GSEA uses when sample numbers allow. The two answer different questions and frequently disagree.

Ranking statistic is part of the method

GSEA results change with the ranking statistic. Ranking by p-value discards direction; by raw fold change over-weights low-expression genes with unstable ratios; by the signed test statistic or shrunken log fold change is usually the defensible choice. Report which you used.

11.10 Reading an enrichment table honestly

The enrichment table computed above declares 511 of 830 terms significant. No set of 30 genes participates in 511 distinct biological processes. Four problems explain the number, and all four are present in published analyses.

The background universe. The hypergeometric test compares your query to a specific population, and changing that population changes every p-value. If your experiment measured 12,000 expressed genes, the universe is those 12,000, not the 20,431 proteins in the proteome — testing against the proteome credits you for an enrichment that is merely the enrichment of expressed genes. Study bias makes this sharper. Replacing the universe with a background matched for study intensity — genes carrying at least 20 GO terms, a proxy for how heavily investigated a gene is — changes the answer substantially.

well_studied <- terms_per_gene$gene[terms_per_gene$n_go >= 20]
N_matched <- length(well_studied)

matched <- gene_to_go |>
  filter(gene %in% well_studied) |>
  count(go, name = "K_matched") |>
  inner_join(count(filter(gene_to_go, gene %in% query_genes), go, name = "k"),
             by = "go") |>
  filter(K_matched >= 5, K_matched <= 2000) |>
  mutate(p_value = phyper(k - 1, K_matched, N_matched - K_matched,
                          n_query, lower.tail = FALSE),
         p_adj = p.adjust(p_value, method = "BH"))

cat("matched background size:", N_matched, "genes\n")
#> matched background size: 3433 genes

#> significant terms, proteome background: 511

    sum(matched$p_adj < 0.05), "\n")
#> significant terms, study-matched background: 121

The significant-term count falls from 511 to 121 — under a quarter of the original — with no change to the query list and no change to the test. The top terms survive and the long tail does not, which is exactly the diagnostic value of the comparison.

Annotation bias. Enrichment can only find processes that have been annotated. The median human gene carries nine GO terms and the genes in this network carry 54.5, so terms describing well-studied biology have more opportunity to be hit. A gene set enriched for cancer genes will appear enriched for apoptosis partly because apoptosis is what cancer genes get annotated for.

Redundancy. GO is a hierarchy, and a gene annotated to a term is annotated to all of that term’s ancestors. Significant terms therefore arrive in correlated families, and a list of twenty is rarely twenty independent findings.

top_ten <- head(enrichment$go, 10)
members <- lapply(top_ten, function(term)
  intersect(gene_to_go$gene[gene_to_go$go == term], query_genes))

jaccard <- outer(seq_along(top_ten), seq_along(top_ten),
  Vectorize(function(i, j)
    length(intersect(members[[i]], members[[j]])) /
    length(union(members[[i]], members[[j]]))))

cat("mean Jaccard overlap among the top ten terms:",
    round(mean(jaccard[upper.tri(jaccard)]), 3),
    " maximum:", round(max(jaccard[upper.tri(jaccard)]), 3), "\n")
#> mean Jaccard overlap among the top ten terms: 0.268  maximum: 0.571

The ten leading terms share a mean Jaccard overlap of 0.268 and a maximum of 0.571: the same handful of genes is driving many of them. Reporting them as ten results would be counting the same evidence ten times. Semantic-similarity clustering of terms, or reporting only the most specific significant term in each branch, is the usual remedy.

Circularity. The 30 genes in this network were selected because they are cell-cycle and DNA-damage genes. Finding that they are enriched for cell-cycle and DNA-damage terms confirms the selection, not a biological hypothesis. The same circularity appears whenever a query list is assembled using prior knowledge — genes from a pathway database, from a review, from a curated portal such as cBioPortal (Cerami et al. 2012) — and then tested against that knowledge. The protection is to fix the query list from the measurement alone, before any annotation is consulted.

A significant term is a hypothesis

An enrichment result says that an annotation category is over-represented in a list, given a background, under a test with stated filters. It does not say the process is active, that it is causal, or that it is the most important thing happening. The claim that a pathway is involved requires perturbation: inhibit it, knock it down, and measure whether the phenotype moves.

11.11 Summary

  • A network adds a relation to a gene list, and most useful network questions — connectivity, modules, bridging nodes — are questions about that relation.
  • Edges in different network classes carry different evidence. STRING’s channel scores make this explicit: every edge in the bundled subnetwork has text-mining support, 176 of 210 have experimental support and only 85 are in a curated database.
  • Topology statistics describe the network you were given, not the interactome. A query-driven subnetwork is dense, short-pathed and cliquish by construction.
  • Degree distributions from small networks cannot establish scale-free architecture; claiming it requires hundreds of nodes and a fitted, tested model.
  • Hub status in literature-derived networks partly measures study intensity: degree correlates with GO annotation count at Spearman 0.746 in this network.
  • Community detection always returns communities. Low modularity across several algorithms, and disagreement between them, is the signal that the partition is not real.
  • Over-representation analysis is one hypergeometric test per term, and knowing that is what lets you diagnose a tool that disagrees with you.
  • Multiple testing correction is mandatory; Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate and is the right choice for exploratory enrichment.
  • GSEA uses the whole ranked list and so detects coordinated small shifts that ORA misses, at the cost of depending on the ranking statistic and the permutation scheme.
  • The background universe is a scientific choice, not a default. Matching it for study intensity cut the significant-term count in this chapter from 511 to 121.

A differential expression analysis ends with a list: four hundred genes, each
with a fold change and an adjusted *p*-value computed by a tool such as DESeq2
[@love2014]. The list is a measurement, not an
explanation. Nothing in it says which of those genes act together, which are
upstream of which, or whether the four hundred represent one disturbed process or
nine unrelated ones. The work of this chapter is to take that kind of list and
place it in a structure — a network, a pathway, an annotation hierarchy — that can
support an interpretation, while being candid about how much of that structure is
measurement and how much is inference from the literature.

Two bundled datasets carry the chapter. The first,
`data/networks/p53_string_network.tsv`, is a high-confidence slice of the STRING
database [@szklarczyk2023] covering 30 human cell-cycle and DNA-damage genes,
with each interaction broken down into the separate evidence channels that
produced it. The second, `data/annotation/human_go_annotations.tsv`, is a

## What a network adds to a gene list {#sec-11-why-networks}

A gene list is a set. A network is a set plus a relation, and almost every
question worth asking about a group of genes is a question about the relation.
Are these genes connected to each other more than chance would predict? Do they
cluster into separable groups? Is there a small number of genes through which
most of the connections run? Which genes, not in the list, sit between the ones
that are?

That last question is the one that most often changes an experiment. If 20 genes
from a 400-gene list all connect to a single transcription factor that is itself
not differentially expressed, the factor becomes a candidate even though no
measurement flagged it. This is the characteristic move of network biology: using
known relations to reach the entities a measurement did not see, because their
activity changed without their messenger RNA (mRNA) abundance changing.

The move has a cost, and it is worth stating before the first line of code. A
measurement — a read count, a peak height — is an observation of the system under
study. An edge in a public interaction network is an observation about a
different system, usually a cell line, under different conditions, reported by
somebody else, sometimes inferred rather than observed at all. When you put your
measurement into a network, you are mixing two kinds of knowledge with very
different error structures, and the network's errors are mostly invisible to
you. Everything else in this chapter follows from taking that seriously.

Systems biology, understood narrowly, is the attempt to explain a phenotype by
the behaviour of an interacting set of components rather than by any one
component. Understood broadly, it is simply the admission that genes do not act
alone. The narrow version demands quantitative models — rate constants,
differential equations, perturbation data — and is out of scope here. The broad
version is where most practical bioinformatics lives, and its tools are graphs
and annotation databases.

## Kinds of network and the evidence behind an edge {#sec-11-network-types}

Four classes of network cover most of what you will meet. They look identical
once loaded into igraph [@csardi2006], which is exactly the problem, because
their edges mean entirely different things.

A **protein–protein interaction (PPI) network** has proteins as nodes and
physical association as edges. "Physical association" conceals a range of
evidence: a solved co-crystal structure, a yeast two-hybrid positive, an
affinity-purification mass spectrometry (AP-MS) co-precipitation, or a sentence
in an abstract saying two proteins "interact". These have wildly different false
positive rates, and high-throughput screens are known to be noisy in both
directions.

A **gene regulatory network** has directed edges: transcription factor to target.
Evidence ranges from a ChIP-seq peak near a promoter — which shows binding, not
regulation — to a reporter assay with mutated binding sites, which shows
regulation in one construct in one cell type. Direction is the valuable part and
the part most often asserted from correlation.

A **metabolic network** is the best-grounded of the four. Nodes are metabolites
and edges are enzyme-catalysed reactions with a stoichiometry established by
biochemistry, often decades ago. Its uncertainty lies not in whether the reaction
exists but in whether a given organism runs it under given conditions.

A **co-expression network** has an edge wherever two genes' expression profiles
correlate above a threshold across samples. Nothing physical is claimed. The
edges are a property of the expression dataset, and change when you change the
samples, the normalisation or the correlation cut-off.

::: {.callout-warning title="A PPI network is not a measurement of your system"}
Interaction databases are aggregations of published results, and publication is
not a random sample of biology. A protein studied by two hundred laboratories
will have hundreds of recorded partners; an equally important protein studied by
two will have three. Degree in a PPI network is therefore partly a measurement of
scientific attention. You will quantify this bias later in the chapter.
:::

The STRING subnetwork bundled with the book makes this visible, because STRING
keeps its evidence channels separate instead of reporting only a combined score.

The columns from `nscore` to `tscore` are the individual channels: genomic

between 0 and 1, and the combined `score` integrates them. Counting how many
edges each channel supports tells you what this network is actually made of.

## Graph vocabulary and the shape of a real network {#sec-11-graph-vocabulary}

A graph is a set of **nodes** (also called vertices) and a set of **edges**
joining pairs of them. An edge may be **directed** or **undirected**, and may
carry a **weight**. The **degree** of a node is the number of edges touching it.
A **path** is a sequence of edges leading from one node to another, and the
**shortest path** between two nodes is the one with fewest edges; its length is
the **distance**. A **component** is a maximal set of nodes all reachable from
each other. The **density** of a graph is the fraction of possible edges that are
present. These seven terms carry most of the chapter.

```{r}
#| label: build-graph
p53_net <- string_edges |>
  select(preferredName_A, preferredName_B) |>
  graph_from_data_frame(directed = FALSE) |>
  simplify()  # drop duplicate edges and any self-loops

cat("nodes:", vcount(p53_net), " edges:", ecount(p53_net), "\n")
cat("density:", round(edge_density(p53_net), 3),
    " mean degree:", mean(degree(p53_net)), "\n")
cat("components:", components(p53_net)$no,
    " largest:", max(components(p53_net)$csize), "\n")
```

Thirty nodes, 210 edges, a single connected component and a density of 0.483:
nearly half of all possible pairs are joined. That is extraordinarily dense for a
biological network, and the reason is the sampling, not the biology. These 30
genes were chosen as a coherent functional group and then queried against a
database that already knows they belong together. A query-driven subnetwork is
not a random sample of the interactome and must never be described as one.

Distances in such a graph are short by construction.

```{r}
#| label: distances
cat("diameter:", diameter(p53_net), "\n")
cat("mean shortest path:", round(mean_distance(p53_net), 3), "\n")
cat("global clustering coefficient:",
    round(transitivity(p53_net, type = "global"), 3), "\n")
cat("mean local clustering coefficient:",
    round(transitivity(p53_net, type = "average"), 3), "\n")

shortest_paths(p53_net, from = "LZTR1", to = "BAX", output = "vpath")$vpath[[1]]
```

The diameter is two: every gene reaches every other in at most two steps. The
**clustering coefficient** measures how often a node's neighbours are themselves
neighbours — formally, the fraction of connected triples that close into
triangles. At 0.646 globally this network is highly cliquish, which again
reflects the curated selection of genes. A genome-scale PPI network typically has
a mean distance near four and a clustering coefficient around 0.1.

The degree distribution is where the most common over-claim in network biology
lives. @fig-11-degree shows it two ways: as a histogram, and as the
complementary cumulative distribution on logarithmic axes, which is the plot
people use when arguing that a network is scale-free.

Read the right-hand panel carefully. It contains 17 points. A straight line
through 17 points on log–log axes is not evidence of a power law, and the
claim that biological networks are scale-free — that the probability of degree
*k* falls as *k* raised to some negative power, producing a few enormous hubs —
has been tested far more loosely than its popularity suggests [@broido2019].
Fitting a power law requires hundreds of nodes, a maximum-likelihood estimate of
the exponent, a lower cut-off chosen by the data rather than by eye, and a
goodness-of-fit test against alternatives such as the log-normal. None of that is possible here, and
saying "the network is scale-free" on the strength of a plot like this one is a
claim your reader cannot check.

What survives is weaker and more useful: degree is unevenly distributed, with a
handful of genes far above the mean of 14 and several at the bottom. That
unevenness is worth investigating regardless of what functional form generates
it.

## Centrality, hubs and study bias {#sec-11-centrality}

**Centrality** is any measure of how important a node is to the structure of a
graph, and the choice of measure encodes what you mean by important. Degree
counts connections. **Betweenness** counts how many shortest paths between other
pairs of nodes pass through a node, identifying bridges between otherwise
separate regions. **Closeness** measures how near a node is to all others on
average.

```{r}
#| label: centrality
centrality <- data.frame(
  gene        = names(node_degree),
  degree      = as.integer(node_degree),
  betweenness = round(betweenness(p53_net, normalized = TRUE), 3),
  closeness   = round(closeness(p53_net, normalized = TRUE), 3)
) |> arrange(desc(degree))

head(centrality, 8)
```

*TP53* has degree 29 in a 30-node graph: it is joined to everything. Its
normalised betweenness, 0.170, is five times that of the next gene, so it also
lies on most shortest paths. In a network assembled by querying a database for
p53-associated genes, this is not a discovery. It is the query, returned.

The deeper problem is that hub status in any literature-derived network partly
measures how much a gene has been studied. The bundled GO annotation file gives a
direct proxy for study intensity — the number of GO terms a gene carries — and
the relationship is strong.

```{r}
#| label: study-bias
go_annotations <- read_tsv(book_data("annotation", "human_go_annotations.tsv"),
                           show_col_types = FALSE)

gene_to_go <- go_annotations |>
  filter(!is.na(`Gene Ontology IDs`), !is.na(`Gene Names (primary)`)) |>
  transmute(gene = `Gene Names (primary)`, go = `Gene Ontology IDs`) |>
  separate_longer_delim(go, delim = "; ") |>
  distinct()

terms_per_gene <- count(gene_to_go, gene, name = "n_go")
bias <- inner_join(centrality, terms_per_gene, by = "gene")

cat("Spearman correlation of degree with GO-term count:",
    round(cor(bias$degree, bias$n_go, method = "spearman"), 3), "\n")
cat("median GO terms, all human proteins:", median(terms_per_gene$n_go),
    " in this network:", median(bias$n_go), "\n")
```

The rank correlation between degree and annotation count is 0.746. Genes in this
network carry a median of 54.5 GO terms against a genome-wide median of nine.
Two explanations compete: important genes attract both interactions and
annotations, or well-studied genes accumulate both. Both are partly true, and no
analysis of the network alone can separate them.

::: {.callout-warning title="Report hubs as candidates, never as conclusions"}
"We identified *TP53* as a hub and therefore a key regulator" is a sentence that
cannot be falsified by the data that produced it. The defensible version names
the hub, states the evidence channels behind its edges, acknowledges the
literature bias, and proposes the experiment that would settle it — a knockdown,
a degron, a dose–response.
:::

## Finding modules {#sec-11-communities}

A **community** or **module** is a group of nodes with more edges inside the
group than to the rest of the network. **Modularity**, written *Q*, scores a
partition by comparing its within-group edge count to the count expected if edges
were rewired at random; *Q* above roughly 0.3 is usually taken as meaningful
structure. Community detection algorithms search for partitions with high *Q*.

Two popular algorithms take different routes. The Louvain method [@blondel2008]
moves nodes between communities greedily and then collapses communities into
nodes, repeating until *Q* stops improving. Fast greedy modularity optimisation
instead merges the pair of communities that most increases *Q*, building a
dendrogram and
cutting it at the maximum. Running both on the same graph is the minimum honest
practice.

```{r}
#| label: communities
set.seed(20250101)
louvain  <- cluster_louvain(p53_net)
greedy   <- cluster_fast_greedy(p53_net)
walktrap <- cluster_walktrap(p53_net)

partitions <- list(Louvain = louvain, FastGreedy = greedy, Walktrap = walktrap)
for (method in names(partitions)) {
  result <- partitions[[method]]
  cat(method, ": k =", length(result),
      " Q =", round(modularity(result), 3),
      " sizes =", paste(sizes(result), collapse = ","), "\n")
}
```

Louvain finds three communities with *Q* = 0.137; fast greedy also finds three,
with *Q* = 0.135; walktrap finds four with *Q* = 0.109. All three values are far
below 0.3. The correct conclusion is that this network has no strong modular
structure — which is what you should expect from a dense, hand-picked set of
genes that all participate in one broad process. The algorithms still return a
partition, because they always do. An algorithm that cannot say "no structure
here" will never say it.

How much do the two best partitions agree? The adjusted Rand index compares two
clusterings, scoring 1 for identical partitions and about 0 for the agreement
expected by chance.

```{r}
#| label: community-agreement
cat("adjusted Rand index, Louvain vs fast greedy:",
    round(compare(louvain, greedy, method = "adjusted.rand"), 3), "\n")
table(Louvain = membership(louvain), FastGreedy = membership(greedy))
```

An adjusted Rand index of 0.700 means substantial but incomplete agreement: the
cross-tabulation shows one 10-gene core assigned together by both methods, an
11-gene group likewise, and three genes that move. Those three genes are the
interesting ones — they are the genes whose module membership is an artefact of
the algorithm, and no biological story should be built on them.

A third algorithm illustrates how badly the choice can go. The Girvan–Newman
method [@girvan2002], available as `cluster_edge_betweenness()`, removes the
highest-betweenness edge repeatedly and is elegant but quadratic in the number of
edges. On this dense graph it returns 20 communities, 19 of them singletons, with
*Q* = 0.009. Dense graphs defeat it, and the method suits sparse networks with
genuine bottlenecks instead.

figure that should have been a table.

subnetwork around the genes under discussion.

## Pathway resources and over-representation analysis {#sec-11-ora}

Three resources dominate functional interpretation. The **Gene Ontology (GO)** is
a structured vocabulary of terms in three namespaces — biological process,
molecular function and cellular component — arranged as a directed acyclic graph
in which a term implies all its ancestors [@ashburner2000; @go2023]. **KEGG**
curates metabolic and signalling pathway maps with defined reactions and
topology [@kanehisa2000]. **Reactome** curates reactions at finer granularity,
with explicit inputs, outputs, catalysts and literature evidence for each step
[@milacic2024]. GO annotates genes with terms; KEGG and Reactome place gene
products inside a described mechanism. GO has the widest coverage, Reactome the
most explicit evidence trail.

**Over-representation analysis (ORA)** asks a single question: given a background
universe of genes, a gene set defined by an annotation term, and a query list,
are more query genes annotated to that term than you would expect by chance? The
exact test is the hypergeometric distribution — the distribution of the number of
white balls drawn when sampling *n* balls without replacement from an urn holding
*K* white and *N* − *K* black. Here *N* is the universe, *K* the term's size, *n*
the query list size and *k* the observed overlap.

The annotation table must first be reshaped from one row per protein to one row
per gene–term pair — the form every enrichment tool uses internally. The
`separate_longer_delim()` call in the previous section did exactly this, and the
tidyverse idiom generalises to any semicolon-delimited annotation column
[@wickham2019].

```{r}
#| label: go-universe
universe <- unique(gene_to_go$gene)
term_sizes <- count(gene_to_go, go, name = "K")

cat("universe:", length(universe), "genes;",
    nrow(term_sizes), "distinct GO terms;",
    nrow(gene_to_go), "gene-term pairs\n")
```

The query list is the 30 network genes, all of which map to the universe. The
test itself is one call to `phyper()`. Note the `k - 1` and `lower.tail = FALSE`:
you want P(X ≥ k), and `phyper(q, ...)` with the upper tail returns P(X > q).

```{r}
#| label: ora
query_genes <- V(p53_net)$name
n_query <- length(query_genes)
N <- length(universe)

enrichment <- gene_to_go |>
  filter(gene %in% query_genes) |>
  count(go, name = "k") |>
  inner_join(term_sizes, by = "go") |>
  filter(K >= 5, K <= 2000) |>          # drop terms too small or too vague to test
  mutate(expected = round(n_query * K / N, 2),
         p_value  = phyper(k - 1, K, N - K, n_query, lower.tail = FALSE),
         p_adj    = p.adjust(p_value, method = "BH")) |>
  arrange(p_value)

cat("terms tested:", nrow(enrichment),
    " significant at BH < 0.05:", sum(enrichment$p_adj < 0.05), "\n")
head(enrichment, 6)
```

That is over-representation analysis in its entirety. A package such as
clusterProfiler [@yu2012] adds term-name lookup, ontology-aware filtering,
plotting and support for many organisms, but the statistic underneath is the call
you just made. Knowing this matters when a tool and your own calculation
disagree: the difference will be in the universe, the term-size filter or the
ontology propagation, never in the distribution.

Two filtering decisions were made above and both are conventional rather than
principled. Terms with fewer than five annotated genes produce unstable estimates
and dominate the tail of small *p*-values for trivial reasons. Terms with more
than 2,000 genes — "protein binding", "cytoplasm" — are too vague to interpret
even when significant. State both bounds in your methods; they change the answer.

### Correcting for multiple testing

Testing 830 terms at a 5% threshold would be expected to yield about 42 false
positives by chance alone. The **Bonferroni** correction divides the threshold by
the number of tests, controlling the probability of even one false positive but
discarding real signal. The **Benjamini–Hochberg** procedure instead controls the
**false discovery rate (FDR)**: the expected proportion of false positives among
the terms you call significant [@benjamini1995]. For exploratory enrichment,
where the output is a list of hypotheses rather than a single decision, FDR
control is the right trade-off; `p.adjust(method = "BH")` applies it.

```{r}
#| label: multiple-testing
cat("raw p < 0.05:        ", sum(enrichment$p_value < 0.05), "\n")
cat("Benjamini-Hochberg:  ", sum(enrichment$p_adj < 0.05), "\n")
cat("Bonferroni:          ",
    sum(p.adjust(enrichment$p_value, "bonferroni") < 0.05), "\n")
```

617 terms pass uncorrected, 511 survive FDR control and 82 survive Bonferroni.
The gap between 511 and 82 is the price of the two error rates, and the fact that
511 of 830 tested terms are "significant" is itself a warning that the chapter
returns to below. An alternative to BH is Storey's *q*-value, which estimates the
proportion of true null hypotheses rather than assuming it is one, and typically
calls more terms significant [@storey2003].

show fold enrichments under 14 across many genes. Both kinds are significant;
only the specific ones say anything.

## Rank-based enrichment {#sec-11-gsea}

ORA requires a cut-off. Genes are in the list or out of it, and a gene just below
the threshold contributes nothing while its neighbour just above contributes
fully. **Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)** removes the cut-off by using the
whole ranked list [@subramanian2005]. Walk down the ranking from the top; add to
a running sum when you meet a member of the gene set, subtract when you meet a
non-member. If the set's members are concentrated near the top, the running sum
climbs to a large positive peak before falling back. That peak is the
**enrichment score (ES)**.

The function is short enough to write out, which is the point of writing it out.

```{r}
#| label: gsea-function
running_enrichment <- function(ranked_stat, set_members) {
  is_hit <- names(ranked_stat) %in% set_members
  weights <- abs(ranked_stat)
  hit_sum  <- cumsum(ifelse(is_hit, weights, 0)) / sum(weights[is_hit])
  miss_sum <- cumsum(ifelse(is_hit, 0, 1)) / sum(!is_hit)
  hit_sum - miss_sum
}
```

A ranking is needed. In a real analysis it would be a differential expression
statistic across every measured gene. Here, to keep every number in the chapter
computable from bundled data, the 30 network genes are ranked by degree and
tested against the p53-binding gene set. This is a demonstration of mechanics on
a deliberately small list, not an analysis anyone would publish.

```{r}
#| label: gsea-run
ranked_by_degree <- sort(node_degree, decreasing = TRUE)
p53_binders <- gene_to_go |>
  filter(go == "GO:0002039", gene %in% names(ranked_by_degree)) |>
  pull(gene)

walk <- running_enrichment(ranked_by_degree, p53_binders)
observed_es <- walk[which.max(abs(walk))]

set.seed(20250101)
null_es <- replicate(2000, {
  shuffled <- setNames(ranked_by_degree, sample(names(ranked_by_degree)))
  w <- running_enrichment(sort(shuffled, decreasing = TRUE), p53_binders)
  w[which.max(abs(w))]
})

cat("set size:", length(p53_binders),
    " ES:", round(observed_es, 3),
    " permutation p:", round((1 + sum(null_es >= observed_es)) / 2001, 4), "\n")
```

The enrichment score is 0.661, reached after six steps, with a permutation
*p*-value of 0.0195 against 2,000 gene-label shuffles. @fig-11-gsea shows the
walk.

Two differences from ORA matter. GSEA uses every gene, so a set whose members are
all modestly shifted in the same direction can be significant even though no
single member passes a differential expression threshold — a pattern ORA cannot
see. And the null distribution is built by permutation rather than from a closed
form, which means the result depends on what you permute. Shuffling gene labels,
as above, asks whether this set is more concentrated at the top than a random set
of the same size — a *competitive* null. Shuffling sample labels and recomputing
the statistic asks whether the set responds to the treatment — a *self-contained*
null, and the one GSEA uses when sample numbers allow. The two answer different
questions and frequently disagree.

::: {.callout-note title="Ranking statistic is part of the method"}
GSEA results change with the ranking statistic. Ranking by *p*-value discards
direction; by raw fold change over-weights low-expression genes with unstable
ratios; by the signed test statistic or shrunken log fold change is usually the
defensible choice. Report which you used.
:::

## Reading an enrichment table honestly {#sec-11-enrichment-pitfalls}

The enrichment table computed above declares 511 of 830 terms significant. No set
of 30 genes participates in 511 distinct biological processes. Four problems
explain the number, and all four are present in published analyses.

**The background universe.** The hypergeometric test compares your query to a
specific population, and changing that population changes every *p*-value. If
your experiment measured 12,000 expressed genes, the universe is those 12,000,
not the 20,431 proteins in the proteome — testing against the proteome credits
you for an enrichment that is merely the enrichment of expressed genes. Study
bias makes this sharper. Replacing the universe with a background matched for
study intensity — genes carrying at least 20 GO terms, a proxy for how heavily
investigated a gene is — changes the answer substantially.

```{r}
#| label: background-choice
well_studied <- terms_per_gene$gene[terms_per_gene$n_go >= 20]
N_matched <- length(well_studied)

matched <- gene_to_go |>
  filter(gene %in% well_studied) |>
  count(go, name = "K_matched") |>
  inner_join(count(filter(gene_to_go, gene %in% query_genes), go, name = "k"),
             by = "go") |>
  filter(K_matched >= 5, K_matched <= 2000) |>
  mutate(p_value = phyper(k - 1, K_matched, N_matched - K_matched,
                          n_query, lower.tail = FALSE),
         p_adj = p.adjust(p_value, method = "BH"))

cat("matched background size:", N_matched, "genes\n")
cat("significant terms, proteome background:", sum(enrichment$p_adj < 0.05), "\n")
cat("significant terms, study-matched background:",
    sum(matched$p_adj < 0.05), "\n")
```

The significant-term count falls from 511 to 121 — under a quarter of the
original —
with no change to the query list and no change to the test. The top terms survive
and the long tail does not, which is exactly the diagnostic value of the
comparison.

**Annotation bias.** Enrichment can only find processes that have been annotated.
The median human gene carries nine GO terms and the genes in this network carry
54.5, so terms describing well-studied biology have more opportunity to be hit.
A gene set enriched for cancer genes will appear enriched for apoptosis partly
because apoptosis is what cancer genes get annotated for.

**Redundancy.** GO is a hierarchy, and a gene annotated to a term is annotated to
all of that term's ancestors. Significant terms therefore arrive in correlated
families, and a list of twenty is rarely twenty independent findings.

```{r}
#| label: term-redundancy
top_ten <- head(enrichment$go, 10)
members <- lapply(top_ten, function(term)
  intersect(gene_to_go$gene[gene_to_go$go == term], query_genes))

jaccard <- outer(seq_along(top_ten), seq_along(top_ten),
  Vectorize(function(i, j)
    length(intersect(members[[i]], members[[j]])) /
    length(union(members[[i]], members[[j]]))))

cat("mean Jaccard overlap among the top ten terms:",
    round(mean(jaccard[upper.tri(jaccard)]), 3),
    " maximum:", round(max(jaccard[upper.tri(jaccard)]), 3), "\n")
```

The ten leading terms share a mean Jaccard overlap of 0.268 and a maximum of
0.571: the same handful of genes is driving many of them. Reporting them as ten
results would be counting the same evidence ten times. Semantic-similarity
clustering of terms, or reporting only the most specific significant term in each
branch, is the usual remedy.

**Circularity.** The 30 genes in this network were selected because they are
cell-cycle and DNA-damage genes. Finding that they are enriched for cell-cycle
and DNA-damage terms confirms the selection, not a biological hypothesis. The
same circularity appears whenever a query list is assembled using prior knowledge
— genes from a pathway database, from a review, from a curated portal such as
cBioPortal [@cerami2012] — and then tested against that knowledge. The protection
is to fix the query list from the measurement alone, before any annotation is
consulted.

::: {.callout-important title="A significant term is a hypothesis"}
An enrichment result says that an annotation category is over-represented in a
list, given a background, under a test with stated filters. It does not say the
process is active, that it is causal, or that it is the most important thing
happening. The claim that a pathway is involved requires perturbation: inhibit
it, knock it down, and measure whether the phenotype moves.
:::

## Summary {#sec-11-summary}

- A network adds a relation to a gene list, and most useful network questions —
  connectivity, modules, bridging nodes — are questions about that relation.
- Edges in different network classes carry different evidence. STRING's channel
  scores make this explicit: every edge in the bundled subnetwork has text-mining
  support, 176 of 210 have experimental support and only 85 are in a curated
  database.
- Topology statistics describe the network you were given, not the interactome.
  A query-driven subnetwork is dense, short-pathed and cliquish by construction.
- Degree distributions from small networks cannot establish scale-free
  architecture; claiming it requires hundreds of nodes and a fitted, tested model.
- Hub status in literature-derived networks partly measures study intensity:
  degree correlates with GO annotation count at Spearman 0.746 in this network.
- Community detection always returns communities. Low modularity across several
  algorithms, and disagreement between them, is the signal that the partition is
  not real.
- Over-representation analysis is one hypergeometric test per term, and knowing
  that is what lets you diagnose a tool that disagrees with you.
- Multiple testing correction is mandatory; Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false
  discovery rate and is the right choice for exploratory enrichment.
- GSEA uses the whole ranked list and so detects coordinated small shifts that
  ORA misses, at the cost of depending on the ranking statistic and the
  permutation scheme.
- The background universe is a scientific choice, not a default. Matching it for
  study intensity cut the significant-term count in this chapter from 511 to 121.

**3.** The lowest local clustering coefficient belongs to *TP53*, the
highest-degree node. Local clustering asks what fraction of a node's neighbour
pairs are themselves joined; because *TP53* is adjacent to all 29 other genes its
neighbourhood is the whole network, including every sparsely connected gene in
it. High degree therefore pushes local clustering down, which is why degree and
clustering must never be read as interchangeable measures of importance.


**4.** The enrichment score rises from 0.661 to 0.874. Degree and betweenness are
correlated but not identical, and reordering even a few genes near the top of a
30-element list moves the running sum substantially. The score should change,
because it is a statistic of a particular ranking. What must not change between
rankings is the conclusion drawn from it, and a conclusion that flips when you
swap centrality measures was never supported in the first place.
