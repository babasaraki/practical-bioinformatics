8  From assembled sequence to annotated genome

After working through this chapter you will be able to

  • distinguish contigs, scaffolds and chromosomes, and explain why N50 alone is a poor summary of assembly quality
  • compute length, base composition and coding density for an assembled genome before committing to an annotation
  • run a prokaryotic gene finder and justify the genetic code and model options you chose
  • compare two annotations of the same genome and classify their disagreements as gene-level or boundary-level
  • explain why eukaryotic gene prediction needs external evidence that prokaryotic prediction does not
  • parse GFF3 column by column, including the attributes column and parent/child relationships, without mis-handling coordinates
  • judge whether a functional assignment is supported by evidence or inherited from a previous guess

An assembler hands you a FASTA file. That file is a hypothesis about the order of bases in an organism’s genome, and nothing more: it contains no genes, no operons, no promoters and no names. Annotation is the process of turning that string of letters into a set of claims about biology — this region is transcribed, that one encodes a protein, this protein probably hydrolyses ATP — each of which can be right, wrong, or right for the wrong reason. The claims are what everyone downstream will actually use. A differential expression analysis counts reads against annotated gene models; a variant effect predictor decides whether a substitution is synonymous by consulting annotated reading frames; a pathway enrichment test inherits every functional label the annotator assigned. Errors introduced here do not stay here.

This chapter works through annotation on a genome small enough to hold in your head and real enough to behave badly: Mycoplasmoides genitalium G37 (Fraser et al. 1995), bundled as data/sequences/mycoplasma-genitalium-NC_000908.2.fasta. At 580 kb it is among the smallest genomes of any free-living organism, which means it can be annotated completely in under a second on a laptop and its annotation can be checked by hand. Alongside it sits the current RefSeq annotation, data/annotation/mycoplasma-genitalium.gff, produced by a curated pipeline. You will measure the genome, predict its genes yourself with Prodigal, and then compare your predictions with RefSeq’s — not to decide who is right, but to learn what the disagreements between two competent annotations look like and where they come from.

By the end you will have produced a gene set, quantified its agreement with a reference annotation at both the gene level and the start-codon level, measured coding density along the chromosome, and dissected the GFF3 file format closely enough that you can debug one when a downstream tool refuses to read it. The habits that matter are smaller than any of that: check the genome before you annotate it, record which evidence supports which claim, and never let an inherited label masquerade as a measurement.

8.2 Judging an assembly before you annotate it

An assembler takes reads and reports contigs: contiguous stretches of sequence that the evidence supports without interruption. If paired-end or long-range linking information is available, contigs can be ordered and oriented into scaffolds, in which the gaps of unknown sequence between contigs are represented by runs of N. A scaffold is therefore a weaker claim than a contig — it asserts adjacency and approximate distance, not sequence. Only when scaffolds are placed on a genetic or physical map, or assembled telomere to telomere, do they become chromosomes (Nurk et al. 2022). Short-read assemblers such as SPAdes (Bankevich et al. 2012) fragment at repeats longer than the read pairs can span; long-read assemblers such as Canu (Koren et al. 2016) and Flye (Kolmogorov et al. 2018) resolve most of those repeats but inherit the higher per-base error of the underlying chemistry.

The statistic you will see quoted first is almost always N50: the length of the contig such that contigs of that length or longer contain at least half of the assembly’s total bases. It is a weighted median, and that is exactly its weakness.

def n50(lengths):
    """Length of the contig at which half the assembly's bases are reached."""
    ordered = sorted(lengths, reverse=True)
    half = sum(ordered) / 2
    running = 0
    for length in ordered:
        running += length
        if running >= half:
            return length
    raise ValueError("empty assembly")

careful = [900_000, 800_000, 700_000, 600_000, 500_000] + [1_000] * 500
reckless = [2_500_000] + [1_000] * 1_500
print(f"careful : {len(careful):4d} contigs, {sum(careful):,} bp, N50 = {n50(careful):,}")
#> careful :  505 contigs, 4,000,000 bp, N50 = 700,000
print(f"reckless: {len(reckless):4d} contigs, {sum(reckless):,} bp, N50 = {n50(reckless):,}")
#> reckless: 1501 contigs, 4,000,000 bp, N50 = 2,500,000

Both assemblies have the same total length. The second has a much larger N50 and is much worse: it was produced by an assembler that resolved an ambiguity aggressively and fused regions that the data did not justify joining. N50 rewards exactly that behaviour, because it measures contiguity and says nothing about correctness. It is also not comparable between assemblies of different total size, which is why NG50, computed against an expected genome size rather than the assembled total, is preferred when a reference size is known. QUAST (Gurevich et al. 2013) reports both, along with misassembly counts when a reference is available.

Two further checks matter more than contiguity. Completeness is usually estimated with BUSCO (Manni et al. 2021), which searches the assembly for a lineage-specific set of genes present in single copy in nearly all members of that lineage and reports each as complete, duplicated, fragmented or missing. A high fragmented count points at an under-assembled or error-rich draft; a high duplicated count points at uncollapsed haplotypes or genuine polyploidy. Contamination is the other check: Kraken 2 (Wood, Lu, and Langmead 2019) classifies contigs taxonomically, and contigs whose taxonomy disagrees with the rest of the assembly are candidates for removal. Annotating a contaminated assembly produces genes that are real but belong to the wrong organism, and they propagate into every database that mirrors your submission.

Three numbers before you annotate

Record total length, number of contigs and BUSCO completeness in your laboratory notebook before the annotation step, and again after. If the gene count surprises you later, these three numbers tell you immediately whether to suspect the assembly or the gene finder.

8.3 Measuring the genome in hand

The bundled M. genitalium assembly is a finished, circular, single-contig genome, so contiguity statistics are trivial: N50 equals the genome length. What is worth measuring is composition, because composition constrains everything that follows.

from collections import Counter
from Bio import SeqIO

record = SeqIO.read(book_data("sequences", "mycoplasma-genitalium-NC_000908.2.fasta"),
                    "fasta")
genome = str(record.seq).upper()
counts = Counter(genome)
gc_percent = 100 * (counts["G"] + counts["C"]) / len(genome)

print(f"{record.id}: {len(genome):,} bp")
#> NC_000908.2: 580,076 bp
print(f"A={counts['A']:,}  C={counts['C']:,}  G={counts['G']:,}  T={counts['T']:,}")
#> A=200,544  C=91,515  G=92,306  T=195,711
print(f"ambiguous bases: {len(genome) - sum(counts[b] for b in 'ACGT')}")
#> ambiguous bases: 0
print(f"GC content: {gc_percent:.2f}%")
#> GC content: 31.69%

Three things follow from that output. There are no ambiguous bases, so this is a finished sequence rather than a draft with gaps. The GC content of 31.69% is low, which matters for gene finding because codon usage and start-codon preference both shift with composition, and because a naive open-reading-frame scan in an AT-rich genome produces more spurious long ORFs by chance. And at 580,076 bp the genome is small enough that an exhaustive six-frame analysis costs nothing.

M. genitalium belongs to the Mollicutes, which use genetic code 4: the codon TGA encodes tryptophan rather than termination. Getting this wrong is not a subtlety. Under the bacterial default, code 11, every internal TGA is read as a stop, and genes are chopped into fragments.

The genetic code is a parameter, not a default

Translation tables differ between bacterial clades, between nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, and between some ciliates and everything else. A gene finder run with the wrong table does not fail — it returns a plausible-looking answer with roughly twice as many, roughly half as long, genes. You will see this quantified in Section 8.4. Always look up the translation table for your organism before the first run, and record it in your methods.

Repeats and why masking comes first

A repeat is a sequence that occurs in multiple copies in a genome: tandem satellite arrays, transposable elements, segmental duplications, and the dispersed families such as Alu and LINE-1 that between them cover roughly half of the human genome (Lander et al. 2001). Repeats cause two separate problems. They break assemblies, as described above. And they wreck gene prediction, because a transposon carries its own open reading frames, its own splice-site-like motifs and its own promoters; an unmasked eukaryotic genome yields thousands of predicted “genes” that are transposase fragments.

Masking marks repetitive regions so that downstream tools can ignore or down-weight them. Hard masking replaces the bases with N, soft masking lower-cases them, and soft masking is almost always the right choice because it preserves the sequence for any tool that wants it while letting repeat-aware tools skip it. Prokaryotic genomes are repeat-poor enough — M. genitalium has no large repeat families — that masking is usually skipped, which is one of several reasons bacterial annotation is easier.

8.4 Finding genes in a compact bacterial genome

Bacterial protein-coding genes are, with rare exceptions, uninterrupted: one start codon, one reading frame, one stop codon. That makes the search space finite. A gene finder enumerates every open reading frame (ORF) — every stretch from a potential start codon to the next in-frame stop — and then has to decide which ORFs are real and, for each real one, which of several possible start codons the ribosome actually uses.

Prodigal (Hyatt et al. 2010) makes both decisions with models it trains on the genome itself, which is why it needs no reference database. It first builds a coding model from the hexamer composition of long, unambiguous ORFs, so that a candidate’s coding score reflects how closely its codon statistics resemble the genome’s own genes. It then examines the region immediately upstream of each candidate start and learns the translation initiation signal: in most bacteria a Shine–Dalgarno ribosome-binding site, a purine-rich motif such as AGGAGG positioned 5–10 bp before the start codon and complementary to the 3’ end of the 16S rRNA. Where no Shine–Dalgarno signal is detectable, Prodigal switches to a generic upstream motif model. The final start call combines coding score, upstream motif score and start-codon type.

Prodigal is an external binary, but it is pinned in the book’s environment.yml and runs on this genome in well under a second, so the chapter runs it for real rather than quoting a stored result. The command-line form is the one you would type:

prodigal -i data/sequences/mycoplasma-genitalium-NC_000908.2.fasta \
         -o mgen_prodigal.gff -f gff -a mgen_prodigal.faa -g 4

-f gff asks for GFF output rather than Prodigal’s default GenBank-like format, -a writes the translated proteins, and -g 4 selects the genetic code. The same command is issued below from Python so that its output feeds the rest of the chapter.

import subprocess, tempfile, shutil, os

if shutil.which("prodigal") is None:
    raise RuntimeError("prodigal is not on PATH; install the book environment first")

workdir = tempfile.mkdtemp(prefix="mgen_annotation_")
fasta = book_data("sequences", "mycoplasma-genitalium-NC_000908.2.fasta")

def run_prodigal(table):
    """Predict genes with the given translation table; return the GFF path."""
    out = os.path.join(workdir, f"prodigal_table{table}.gff")
    subprocess.run(["prodigal", "-i", fasta, "-o", out, "-f", "gff",
                    "-g", str(table), "-q"], check=True)
    return out

gff_code4 = run_prodigal(4)
gff_code11 = run_prodigal(11)

The two runs differ only in the translation table, and that single parameter changes the answer completely.

def count_cds(path):
    with open(path) as handle:
        return sum(1 for line in handle if not line.startswith("#") and line.strip())

print(f"genetic code 4  (Mollicutes): {count_cds(gff_code4):4d} predicted CDS")
#> genetic code 4  (Mollicutes):  527 predicted CDS
print(f"genetic code 11 (bacterial default): {count_cds(gff_code11):4d} predicted CDS")
#> genetic code 11 (bacterial default):  995 predicted CDS

Nearly twice as many genes, because every internal TGA tryptophan codon terminated a reading frame prematurely and the true gene was reported as two or three fragments. Nothing in the output flags the problem; the GFF is well-formed and the proteins are translatable. Only the biology is wrong. The rest of the chapter uses the code-4 predictions.

def parse_gff(path):
    """Yield one dictionary per GFF3 feature line, attributes already split."""
    features = []
    with open(path) as handle:
        for line in handle:
            if line.startswith("#") or not line.strip():
                continue
            fields = line.rstrip("\n").split("\t")
            if len(fields) != 9:
                continue
            attributes = dict(pair.split("=", 1)
                              for pair in fields[8].rstrip(";").split(";")
                              if "=" in pair)
            features.append({"seqid": fields[0], "source": fields[1],
                             "type": fields[2], "start": int(fields[3]),
                             "end": int(fields[4]), "score": fields[5],
                             "strand": fields[6], "phase": fields[7],
                             "attributes": attributes})
    return features

predicted = parse_gff(gff_code4)
print(f"{len(predicted)} predicted CDS features")
#> 527 predicted CDS features
print(Counter(feature["attributes"]["start_type"] for feature in predicted))
#> Counter({'ATG': 458, 'GTG': 50, 'TTG': 19})

The start-codon distribution is itself informative: ATG dominates, but GTG and TTG together account for a substantial minority. A gene finder that only considered ATG would truncate every one of those genes at the first internal ATG, shortening the protein and shifting the annotated promoter region.

8.5 Comparing two annotations of the same genome

The RefSeq annotation of this genome was produced by the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (Tatusova et al. 2016), which combines ab initio prediction with protein homology evidence and curated rules. It is a useful comparator precisely because it was built differently from the Prodigal run.

reference = parse_gff(book_data("annotation", "mycoplasma-genitalium.gff"))
print(Counter(feature["type"] for feature in reference).most_common())
#> [('gene', 546), ('CDS', 524), ('exon', 42), ('tRNA', 36), ('pseudogene', 20), ('rRNA', 3), ('region', 1), ('SRP_RNA', 1), ('RNase_P_RNA', 1), ('tmRNA', 1)]

Count the feature types before anything else. There are 546 gene features but only 524 CDS features, and 20 pseudogene features; the difference is made up of the tRNA, rRNA and other structural RNA genes, which have a gene parent but no CDS child. Conflating “number of gene lines” with “number of proteins” is the single most common arithmetic error in annotation summaries.

The right way to ask whether two annotations agree is to match genes by their stop codon, not their start. The stop is determined by the reading frame and is rarely ambiguous; the start is a prediction in its own right. On the plus strand the stop is at the feature’s end coordinate, on the minus strand at its start.

reference_cds = [f for f in reference if f["type"] == "CDS"]

def stop_key(feature):
    """Strand plus the coordinate of the 3' end: a frame-anchored gene identity."""
    return (feature["strand"],
            feature["end"] if feature["strand"] == "+" else feature["start"])

reference_by_stop = {stop_key(f): f for f in reference_cds}
predicted_by_stop = {stop_key(f): f for f in predicted}
shared = set(reference_by_stop) & set(predicted_by_stop)

print(f"RefSeq CDS        : {len(reference_cds)}")
#> RefSeq CDS        : 524
print(f"Prodigal CDS      : {len(predicted)}")
#> Prodigal CDS      : 527
print(f"shared 3' ends    : {len(shared)}")
#> shared 3' ends    : 501
print(f"RefSeq only       : {len(set(reference_by_stop) - set(predicted_by_stop))}")
#> RefSeq only       : 23
print(f"Prodigal only     : {len(set(predicted_by_stop) - set(reference_by_stop))}")
#> Prodigal only     : 26

Of 524 RefSeq coding sequences and 527 Prodigal predictions, 501 share a 3’ end. That is roughly 95% gene-level agreement between an evidence-informed curated pipeline and a database-free ab initio run, which is the usual state of affairs for a compact bacterial genome and should not be extrapolated to anything larger or more repetitive.

The 23 RefSeq-only features are the more interesting side of the disagreement.

reference_only = set(reference_by_stop) - set(predicted_by_stop)
pseudo = [key for key in reference_only
          if reference_by_stop[key]["attributes"].get("pseudo") == "true"]
print(f"{len(pseudo)} of {len(reference_only)} RefSeq-only features are pseudogenes")
#> 19 of 23 RefSeq-only features are pseudogenes
for key in sorted(pseudo)[:3]:
    attributes = reference_by_stop[key]["attributes"]
    print(" -", attributes.get("product"), "|", attributes.get("Note"))
#>  - hypothetical protein | incomplete%3B partial in the middle of a contig%3B missing N-terminus and C-terminus
#>  - adhesin P1 | incomplete%3B partial in the middle of a contig%3B missing N-terminus and C-terminus
#>  - adhesin | incomplete%3B partial in the middle of a contig%3B missing N-terminus and C-terminus

Nineteen of the 23 are annotated pseudogenes: genes that homology evidence shows were once functional but that now carry a frameshift or an internal stop. RefSeq records them because a reader needs to know the locus exists and is broken. Prodigal does not report them because they are not open reading frames. Neither behaviour is a bug; they answer different questions. Five of these RefSeq CDS features have a length that is not a multiple of three, which is a deliberate signal that the feature spans a frameshift and must not be naively translated.

Boundary disagreements are the other, larger, class.

def start_offset(reference_feature, predicted_feature):
    """Signed distance from the RefSeq start to the Prodigal start, in bp."""
    if reference_feature["strand"] == "+":
        return predicted_feature["start"] - reference_feature["start"]
    return reference_feature["end"] - predicted_feature["end"]

offsets = {key: start_offset(reference_by_stop[key], predicted_by_stop[key])
           for key in shared}
identical = sum(1 for value in offsets.values() if value == 0)
print(f"identical at both ends: {identical} of {len(shared)}")
#> identical at both ends: 464 of 501
print(f"start-codon disagreements: {len(shared) - identical}")
#> start-codon disagreements: 37
in_frame = sum(1 for value in offsets.values() if value and value % 3 == 0)
print(f"of which in-frame (a different start in the same frame): {in_frame}")
#> of which in-frame (a different start in the same frame): 36

Of the 501 genes both methods find, 464 agree on both coordinates exactly. Of the 37 that differ, 36 differ by a multiple of three: the two methods chose different start codons within the same reading frame. The single exception is a RefSeq pseudogene annotated across a frameshift, whose CDS length is deliberately not a multiple of three. This is the classic boundary disagreement and it has a biological cause. Many bacterial genes have several in-frame candidate starts within the first 60 bp, the ribosome-binding signal is weak or absent, and the choice between them rests on a probabilistic model rather than on evidence. Two models trained on different data choose differently. The consequence is a protein that is a handful of residues longer or shorter at the N-terminus — usually harmless, occasionally fatal if the difference removes a signal peptide or a start-proximal catalytic residue.

The distributions are close to identical, with a median near 855 bp in both cases, which corresponds to a protein of about 285 residues. Short ab initio predictions in an AT-rich genome are the category that most often turns out to be noise, and also the category where genuine small proteins are most often missed. Neither annotation can settle the question without proteomic or ribosome-profiling evidence.

The single conspicuous feature is the window beginning at 170 kb, where coding density collapses to 0.5%: that window holds the 16S, 23S and 5S ribosomal RNA genes, which are transcribed but not translated and therefore contribute no CDS. Across the whole chromosome, 94.64% of bases fall inside an annotated CDS, and 96.05% fall inside some annotated feature once the structural RNAs are included. That is extreme even for a bacterium, and it is a direct consequence of genome reduction: this organism has discarded almost everything that is not a gene. Two practical lessons follow. First, intergenic distance is a usable diagnostic here — 197 adjacent CDS pairs actually overlap, most commonly by 1, 8 or 11 bp, which is the signature of translationally coupled operons in which a stop codon and the next start codon share or nearly share bases. Second, a window of low coding density in a genome like this is worth investigating rather than ignoring; in a eukaryote it would be unremarkable.

The left panel is the summary a reviewer wants: 464 genes identical, 37 differing only in their start, 23 found only by RefSeq and 26 only by Prodigal. The right panel shows that the start disagreements are not a smooth distribution around zero but a set of discrete in-frame offsets, and that they are close to balanced in direction: Prodigal places its start upstream of RefSeq’s in 17 genes and downstream in 20. The disagreement is therefore not a systematic bias in one method but a genuine ambiguity in the data, resolved differently by a motif model and by an alignment to a homologous protein. Homology evidence is the more defensible tie-breaker, which is the single best argument for running an evidence-based pipeline such as Prokka (Seemann 2014) or PGAP rather than a bare gene finder.

8.6 Gene prediction in eukaryotes

Everything above depended on the assumption that a gene is one uninterrupted reading frame. In eukaryotes it is not, and every part of the problem becomes harder.

A eukaryotic protein-coding gene is transcribed as a pre-mRNA containing exons separated by introns that are spliced out. The spliceosome recognises short, degenerate signals: a GT at the intron’s 5’ end, an AG at its 3’ end, a branch point and a polypyrimidine tract. Those motifs are far too short to locate reliably on their own — a genome contains a GT every 16 bp by chance — so a predictor must combine them with coding-potential models on both sides and with constraints on intron length. Human introns range from under 100 bp to over a megabase, so the search space is enormous. Alternative splicing then means that the correct answer is not one gene model but several: the same locus can produce transcripts with different exon content, different reading frames and different protein products. A tool asked to report a single model per locus is being asked the wrong question.

Ab initio eukaryotic predictors such as AUGUSTUS (Stanke et al. 2006) use generalised hidden Markov models whose states correspond to the parts of a gene — intergenic, 5’ UTR, initial exon, intron, internal exon, terminal exon — and whose parameters must be trained on a set of known genes from a related species. Run on a novel genome with mis-matched training parameters, they produce gene sets whose counts can be wrong by a factor of two.

Modern practice is therefore evidence-based annotation: predictions are made in the presence of data that directly observes transcription. RNA-seq reads aligned with a spliced aligner give exon boundaries and splice junctions empirically — a junction supported by 200 reads is not a hypothesis — and full-length long-read cDNA gives complete models. Proteins from related species, aligned to the genome, confirm coding regions and reading frames. A combiner reconciles all three into a consensus gene set, so the annotation’s quality is bounded by the tissue coverage of the evidence: a gene expressed only in a developmental stage you did not sample will be annotated badly or not at all.

Annotation versions are not interchangeable

Ensembl (Cunningham et al. 2021) and RefSeq (Sayers et al. 2021) annotate the same human genome and do not produce identical gene models, and each changes between releases. Gene counts, exon boundaries and even the set of genes considered protein-coding move. Record the exact annotation release used in any analysis — Ensembl 110, RefSeq GCF_000001405.40-RS_2023_10 — because a count computed against one release cannot be compared with a count computed against another.

8.7 From a predicted protein to a biological function

Structural annotation says where the genes are. Functional annotation says what they do, and it is almost entirely an exercise in inference by similarity.

The simplest form is homology transfer: search the predicted protein against a curated database with BLAST (Altschul et al. 1990) or a more sensitive profile method, and copy the description of the best hit. This works when the hit is strong, nearly full length and to a protein whose function was experimentally determined. It fails in characteristic ways. Similarity over a shared domain does not imply shared overall function; a protein kinase domain tells you the protein phosphorylates something, not what. Enzymes in the same superfamily can differ in substrate. And best-hit transfer confuses orthologues — genes in different species that descend from a single ancestral gene via speciation, and usually do keep their function — with paralogues, which arose by duplication within a lineage and frequently diverge in function. Orthology-aware resources exist because the best BLAST hit is often a paralogue.

Domain annotation is more conservative and more durable. Pfam (Mistry et al. 2020) models protein families and domains as profile hidden Markov models built from curated seed alignments, and searching a protein against them with HMMER (Eddy 2011) reports which domains are present, in what order, and over which residues. InterPro (Paysan-Lafosse et al. 2022) integrates Pfam with a dozen other signature databases and maps the result onto Gene Ontology terms. A domain architecture is a statement about the protein’s parts and is far less likely to be wrong than a transferred description, which is why curated resources show domains and evidence rather than a single functional label.

Annotation errors propagate and compound

When a protein is annotated by copying the description of its best database hit, and that description was itself copied from an earlier best hit, the chain of inference can be many steps long and no step is recorded. A single mis-annotation in a well-populated family can be re-copied into hundreds of genomes within a few years, and the resulting consensus looks like independent corroboration. The defences are specific: prefer domain-level and orthology-based assignments to best-hit transfer; demand alignment coverage of at least 70–80% of both query and subject before transferring a description; treat a hit whose own annotation carries an electronic evidence code as a hypothesis, not a fact; and propagate the evidence alongside the label so the next person can re-evaluate it.

It is worth measuring how much of a well-studied proteome is still unexplained.

products = [f["attributes"].get("product", "") for f in reference_cds]

duf = sum(1 for p in products if "DUF" in p)

#> hypothetical protein : 27

#> domain of unknown function (DUF): 27

Just over 10% of the proteome of one of the most intensively studied minimal organisms is still unannotated after three decades of work on it. That is a useful calibration for how much of any new genome’s functional annotation is genuinely known.

Controlled vocabularies and evidence codes

Free-text product descriptions cannot be computed on: DNA polymerase III subunit beta, DNA pol III beta and beta clamp are the same thing to a reader and three different strings to a program. Controlled vocabularies solve this by assigning stable identifiers to concepts.

The Gene Ontology (Ashburner et al. 2000; Aleksander et al. 2023) is the most widely used. It provides three independent hierarchies — molecular function, biological process and cellular component — organised as a directed acyclic graph in which a term may have several parents and in which an annotation to a term implies annotation to all its ancestors. A gene annotated to GO:0003887 (DNA-directed DNA polymerase activity) is thereby also annotated to the broader nucleotidyltransferase and catalytic activity terms, which is what makes enrichment analysis possible. EC numbers provide a complementary four-level classification of enzymatic reactions (2.7.7.7 is DNA-directed DNA polymerase), and KEGG (Kanehisa 2000) maps gene products onto metabolic and signalling pathways.

Every GO annotation carries an evidence code stating how it was arrived at. EXP and its children mark experimental evidence; IDA is a direct assay; IMP is inference from a mutant phenotype; ISS is inference from sequence similarity, assigned by a curator; IEA is inferred from electronic annotation, assigned automatically with no curator review. The RefSeq GFF carries these in its go_function, go_process and go_component attributes.

evidence = Counter()
with_go = 0
for feature in reference_cds:
    has_go = False
    for key in ("go_function", "go_process", "go_component"):
        if key in feature["attributes"]:
            has_go = True
            for item in feature["attributes"][key].split(","):
                parts = item.split("|")
                if len(parts) >= 4:
                    evidence[parts[3]] += 1
    with_go += has_go

print(f"CDS with at least one GO term: {with_go} of {len(reference_cds)}")
#> CDS with at least one GO term: 331 of 524
print("evidence codes:", dict(evidence))
#> evidence codes: {'IEA': 985}

Every one of the 985 GO annotations in this file carries the code IEA. Not one was reviewed by a curator for this organism. This is entirely normal — the overwhelming majority of GO annotations in any genome are IEA — but it changes how you should read an enrichment result. An enrichment of a GO term among your differentially expressed genes, computed against an IEA-only background, is a statement about which sequence features your gene set shares, filtered through a transfer pipeline. It is a hypothesis-generating observation, not evidence that the process is active.

Read the evidence code before the term

When a functional enrichment result surprises you, retrieve the evidence codes for the genes driving it. A term driven entirely by IEA annotations on hypothetical proteins is a different kind of finding from one driven by IDA annotations on characterised enzymes, and only the second justifies a confident claim in a discussion section.

8.8 The anatomy of GFF3 and GTF

Annotation is exchanged as tab-delimited feature tables, and almost every frustrating bug in a genomics pipeline is a misunderstanding of one of nine columns. A GFF3 line has exactly these fields, in order: seqid, source, type, start, end, score, strand, phase, attributes.

first_cds = next(f for f in reference if f["type"] == "CDS")
for name in ("seqid", "source", "type", "start", "end", "score", "strand", "phase"):
    print(f"{name:8s} = {first_cds[name]}")
#> seqid    = NC_000908.2
#> source   = Protein Homology
#> type     = CDS
#> start    = 686
#> end      = 1828
#> score    = .
#> strand   = +
#> phase    = 0
for name in ("ID", "Parent", "gene", "product", "protein_id", "transl_table"):
    print(f"  attr {name:12s} = {first_cds['attributes'].get(name)}")
#>   attr ID           = cds-WP_009885562.1
#>   attr Parent       = gene-MG_RS00005
#>   attr gene         = dnaN
#>   attr product      = DNA polymerase III subunit beta
#>   attr protein_id   = WP_009885562.1
#>   attr transl_table = 4

seqid must match the FASTA identifier exactly, and the commonest cause of an empty result from a tool that takes both files is a mismatch between NC_000908.2 here and NC_000908 or chr1 there. source records the program or database that made the claim, which lets you filter a mixed annotation by provenance — here RefSeq genes carry RefSeq while their CDS children carry Protein Homology, recording that the coding region was called from protein alignment evidence. type should be a Sequence Ontology term. score and phase are . when not applicable; phase on a CDS is 0, 1 or 2 and gives the number of bases to skip at the start of that feature to reach the first complete codon, which matters only for CDS split across multiple lines.

Coordinates are not interchangeable between formats

GFF3, GTF, SAM and VCF are 1-based and inclusive: a feature from 686 to 1828 includes both endpoints and has length 1828 − 686 + 1 = 1143 bp. BED and the internal coordinates of most programming libraries are 0-based and half-open: the same interval is written 685–1828 and its length is simply 1828 − 685. Python and R slicing inherit this split — Python strings are 0-based half-open, R vectors are 1-based inclusive. Converting in the wrong direction shifts every feature by one base, which silently changes reading frames, moves variants between codons, and produces proteins that are almost but not quite right. Write the conversion once, in a named function, and test it on a feature whose sequence you know.

The attributes column is a semicolon-separated list of tag=value pairs, and it is where the structure lives. ID is unique within the file; Parent points at another feature’s ID and builds the hierarchy. In GFF3 the relationship is explicit and may be many levels deep: gene → mRNA → exon and CDS. GTF, the older format that many RNA-seq tools still require, instead encodes the same hierarchy implicitly through gene_id and transcript_id attributes on every line, and uses spaces and quotes rather than =. A GTF parser applied to a GFF3 file will often run without error and return nothing.

by_id = {f["attributes"]["ID"]: f for f in reference if "ID" in f["attributes"]}
children = Counter()
for feature in reference:
    parent = feature["attributes"].get("Parent")
    if parent in by_id:
        children[(by_id[parent]["type"], feature["type"])] += 1
for (parent_type, child_type), number in children.most_common():
    print(f"{parent_type:12s} -> {child_type:8s} : {number}")
#> gene         -> CDS      : 504
#> gene         -> tRNA     : 36
#> tRNA         -> exon     : 36
#> pseudogene   -> CDS      : 20
#> gene         -> rRNA     : 3
#> rRNA         -> exon     : 3
#> gene         -> SRP_RNA  : 1
#> SRP_RNA      -> exon     : 1
#> gene         -> RNase_P_RNA : 1
#> RNase_P_RNA  -> exon     : 1
#> gene         -> tmRNA    : 1
#> tmRNA        -> exon     : 1

Two hierarchies are visible. Protein-coding genes have a single CDS child and no exon feature, because a bacterial CDS is contiguous. The structural RNA genes have an RNA-level child which in turn has an exon child. That asymmetry is the second classic pitfall: CDS and exon are not the same feature. An exon is what is present in the mature transcript, including untranslated regions; a CDS is the translated part. A read-counting tool configured to count over exon features will report nothing for a bacterial annotation that contains CDS features only, and will count UTR reads in a eukaryotic annotation that contains both. Always check which feature type your counter is using.

The third pitfall is discontinuous features. A spliced CDS is written as several lines sharing one ID, not as one line spanning the introns. Code that treats each line as a separate gene will multiply your gene count by the mean exon number; code that takes min(start) and max(end) will include the introns in the length. Group by ID and sum the segment lengths.

8.9 Assessing and releasing an annotation

An annotation needs its own quality assessment, separate from the assembly’s.

Start with internal consistency. Every CDS length should be a multiple of three unless it is explicitly marked as a pseudogene or as partial; every complete CDS should begin with a valid start codon and end with a stop codon in the chosen translation table; no CDS should contain an internal stop.

stop_codons = {"TAA", "TAG"}          # genetic code 4: TGA encodes tryptophan
complement = str.maketrans("ACGT", "TGCA")

def cds_sequence(feature):
    """Extract a CDS in its coding orientation, 1-based inclusive coordinates."""
    segment = genome[feature["start"] - 1:feature["end"]]
    return segment if feature["strand"] == "+" else segment.translate(complement)[::-1]

bad_length = sum(1 for f in predicted if (f["end"] - f["start"] + 1) % 3)
bad_stop = sum(1 for f in predicted if cds_sequence(f)[-3:] not in stop_codons)
internal = sum(1 for f in predicted
               if any(cds_sequence(f)[i:i + 3] in stop_codons
                      for i in range(0, len(cds_sequence(f)) - 3, 3)))
print(f"length not a multiple of three : {bad_length}")
#> length not a multiple of three : 0
print(f"does not end in a stop codon   : {bad_stop}")
#> does not end in a stop codon   : 0
print(f"contains an internal stop codon: {internal}")
#> contains an internal stop codon: 0

All three counts are zero, which is the expected result for a complete ab initio gene set run with the right translation table. The same check applied to the code-11 predictions is where the earlier mistake finally becomes visible.

#> code-11 predictions ending in TGA: 612 of 995

Under code 4, TGA is tryptophan, so 612 of the 995 code-11 predictions terminate at a codon that is not a stop codon in this organism. A check of this kind — does the gene set obey the rules of the genetic code you believe applies? — costs three lines and catches an error that no amount of staring at a GFF file will reveal.

Then compare against expectation. Coding density of 85–90% is normal for bacteria; a prediction at 60% suggests a missed translation table or a contaminated assembly, and one at 99% suggests spurious short ORFs. Mean protein length near 300 residues is typical; a sharp drop indicates fragmentation. BUSCO can be run on the predicted proteins rather than the genome, which separates “the gene is absent from the assembly” from “the gene is present but was not predicted”. Checking the fraction of proteins with a Pfam domain gives a rough measure of how much of the gene set is plausible.

For release, the conventions are strict and worth learning early. Sequences go to GenBank, ENA or DDBJ, which exchange records nightly, so a deposit in one appears in all three. A prokaryotic submission is usually contigs plus a feature table; NCBI will run PGAP on it, and the RefSeq record you download later is NCBI’s annotation, not necessarily yours. Locus tags must be requested as a registered prefix — MG_RS00005 follows that convention — and must be unique and stable, because they become the handle by which your genes are cited. Product names follow guidelines that forbid molecular weights, database identifiers and the unqualified word “homolog”. The assembly receives a versioned accession such as GCF_000027325.1, and that accession, not the organism name, belongs in a methods section.

Annotation provenance is part of the result

When you publish an analysis that depends on an annotation, record the assembly accession, the annotation source and release, the gene finder and its version, the translation table, and the evidence used for functional assignment. Without these a reader cannot reproduce your gene counts, and an unreproducible gene count undermines every statistic computed from it. This is as true for a clinical pipeline, where an annotation release change can alter a variant’s reported consequence, as for a comparative genomics paper.

8.10 Summary

  • An assembly is a hypothesis; check its contiguity, completeness and contamination before annotating, because annotation inherits every assembly error and adds its own.
  • N50 measures contiguity, not correctness, and rewards over-aggressive assembly. Report it with total length, contig count, BUSCO completeness and, where a reference size is known, NG50.
  • The translation table is a parameter you must set deliberately. On M. genitalium the bacterial default produces 995 predicted genes against 527 with the correct code 4, and nothing in the output signals the error.
  • Prokaryotic gene finding is a solved problem to a first approximation: ab initio Prodigal and the curated RefSeq pipeline agree on the 3’ end of 501 genes out of roughly 525, and on both ends of 464.
  • Disagreements between annotations concentrate in start codons rather than in gene presence, because the start is a prediction and the stop is a consequence of the reading frame.
  • Eukaryotic prediction requires external evidence — RNA-seq, long-read cDNA, protein alignments — because introns and alternative splicing make the ab initio problem underdetermined.
  • Functional annotation is inference by similarity, and the inference chain is usually invisible. Prefer domain and orthology assignments to best-hit transfer, and read the evidence code before the term: all 985 GO annotations in this genome are IEA.
  • GFF3 bugs are coordinate bugs. Remember that GFF3 is 1-based inclusive and BED is 0-based half-open, that CDS and exon are different features, and that a spliced feature occupies several lines sharing one ID.

An assembler hands you a FASTA file. That file is a hypothesis about the order of
bases in an organism's genome, and nothing more: it contains no genes, no operons,
no promoters and no names. Annotation is the process of turning that string of
letters into a set of claims about biology — this region is transcribed, that one
encodes a protein, this protein probably hydrolyses ATP — each of which can be
right, wrong, or right for the wrong reason. The claims are what everyone
downstream will actually use. A differential expression analysis counts reads
against annotated gene models; a variant effect predictor decides whether a
substitution is synonymous by consulting annotated reading frames; a pathway
enrichment test inherits every functional label the annotator assigned. Errors
introduced here do not stay here.

This chapter works through annotation on a genome small enough to hold in your head
and real enough to behave badly: *Mycoplasmoides genitalium* G37 [@fraser1995],
bundled as `data/sequences/mycoplasma-genitalium-NC_000908.2.fasta`. At 580 kb it is among the
smallest genomes of any free-living organism, which means it can be annotated
completely in under a second on a laptop and its annotation can be checked by hand.
Alongside it sits the current RefSeq annotation,
`data/annotation/mycoplasma-genitalium.gff`, produced by a curated pipeline. You
will measure the genome, predict its genes yourself with Prodigal, and then compare
your predictions with RefSeq's — not to decide who is right, but to learn what the
disagreements between two competent annotations look like and where they come from.

By the end you will have produced a gene set, quantified its agreement with a
reference annotation at both the gene level and the start-codon level, measured
coding density along the chromosome, and dissected the GFF3 file format closely
enough that you can debug one when a downstream tool refuses to read it. The habits
that matter are smaller than any of that: check the genome before you annotate it,
record which evidence supports which claim, and never let an inherited label
masquerade as a measurement.

## Judging an assembly before you annotate it {#sec-08-assembly-quality}

An assembler takes reads and reports **contigs**: contiguous stretches of sequence
that the evidence supports without interruption. If paired-end or long-range linking
information is available, contigs can be ordered and oriented into **scaffolds**,
in which the gaps of unknown sequence between contigs are represented by runs of
`N`. A scaffold is therefore a weaker claim than a contig — it asserts adjacency and
approximate distance, not sequence. Only when scaffolds are placed on a genetic or
physical map, or assembled telomere to telomere, do they become **chromosomes**
[@nurk2022]. Short-read assemblers such as SPAdes [@bankevich2012] fragment at
repeats longer than the read pairs can span; long-read assemblers such as Canu
[@koren2017] and Flye [@kolmogorov2019] resolve most of those repeats but inherit
the higher per-base error of the underlying chemistry.

The statistic you will see quoted first is almost always **N50**: the length of the
contig such that contigs of that length or longer contain at least half of the
assembly's total bases. It is a weighted median, and that is exactly its weakness.

```{python}
#| label: n50
def n50(lengths):
    """Length of the contig at which half the assembly's bases are reached."""
    ordered = sorted(lengths, reverse=True)
    half = sum(ordered) / 2
    running = 0
    for length in ordered:
        running += length
        if running >= half:
            return length
    raise ValueError("empty assembly")

careful = [900_000, 800_000, 700_000, 600_000, 500_000] + [1_000] * 500
reckless = [2_500_000] + [1_000] * 1_500
print(f"careful : {len(careful):4d} contigs, {sum(careful):,} bp, N50 = {n50(careful):,}")
print(f"reckless: {len(reckless):4d} contigs, {sum(reckless):,} bp, N50 = {n50(reckless):,}")
```

Both assemblies have the same total length. The second has a much larger N50 and is
much worse: it was produced by an assembler that resolved an ambiguity aggressively
and fused regions that the data did not justify joining. N50 rewards exactly that
behaviour, because it measures contiguity and says nothing about correctness. It is
also not comparable between assemblies of different total size, which is why
**NG50**, computed against an expected genome size rather than the assembled total,
is preferred when a reference size is known. QUAST [@gurevich2013] reports both,
along with misassembly counts when a reference is available.

Two further checks matter more than contiguity. **Completeness** is usually
estimated with BUSCO [@manni2021], which searches the assembly for a lineage-specific
set of genes present in single copy in nearly all members of that lineage and
reports each as complete, duplicated, fragmented or missing. A high fragmented
count points at an under-assembled or error-rich draft; a high duplicated count
points at uncollapsed haplotypes or genuine polyploidy. **Contamination** is the
other check: Kraken 2 [@wood2019] classifies contigs taxonomically, and contigs
whose taxonomy disagrees with the rest of the assembly are candidates for removal.
Annotating a contaminated assembly produces genes that are real but belong to the
wrong organism, and they propagate into every database that mirrors your
submission.

::: {.callout-tip title="Three numbers before you annotate"}
Record total length, number of contigs and BUSCO completeness in your laboratory
notebook before the annotation step, and again after. If the gene count surprises
you later, these three numbers tell you immediately whether to suspect the assembly
or the gene finder.
:::

## Measuring the genome in hand {#sec-08-genome-stats}

The bundled *M. genitalium* assembly is a finished, circular, single-contig genome,
so contiguity statistics are trivial: N50 equals the genome length. What is worth
measuring is composition, because composition constrains everything that follows.

```{python}
#| label: genome-stats
from collections import Counter
from Bio import SeqIO

record = SeqIO.read(book_data("sequences", "mycoplasma-genitalium-NC_000908.2.fasta"),
                    "fasta")
genome = str(record.seq).upper()
counts = Counter(genome)
gc_percent = 100 * (counts["G"] + counts["C"]) / len(genome)

print(f"{record.id}: {len(genome):,} bp")
print(f"A={counts['A']:,}  C={counts['C']:,}  G={counts['G']:,}  T={counts['T']:,}")
print(f"ambiguous bases: {len(genome) - sum(counts[b] for b in 'ACGT')}")
print(f"GC content: {gc_percent:.2f}%")
```

Three things follow from that output. There are no ambiguous bases, so this is a
finished sequence rather than a draft with gaps. The GC content of 31.69% is low,
which matters for gene finding because codon usage and start-codon preference both
shift with composition, and because a naive open-reading-frame scan in an AT-rich
genome produces more spurious long ORFs by chance. And at 580,076 bp the genome is
small enough that an exhaustive six-frame analysis costs nothing.

*M. genitalium* belongs to the Mollicutes, which use **genetic code 4**: the codon
TGA encodes tryptophan rather than termination. Getting this wrong is not a
subtlety. Under the bacterial default, code 11, every internal TGA is read as a
stop, and genes are chopped into fragments.

::: {.callout-warning title="The genetic code is a parameter, not a default"}
Translation tables differ between bacterial clades, between nuclear and
mitochondrial genomes, and between some ciliates and everything else. A gene finder
run with the wrong table does not fail — it returns a plausible-looking answer with
roughly twice as many, roughly half as long, genes. You will see this quantified
in @sec-08-prokaryotic-prediction. Always look up the translation table for your
organism before the first run, and record it in your methods.
:::

### Repeats and why masking comes first

A **repeat** is a sequence that occurs in multiple copies in a genome: tandem
satellite arrays, transposable elements, segmental duplications, and the dispersed
families such as *Alu* and LINE-1 that between them cover roughly half of the human
genome [@lander2001]. Repeats cause two separate problems. They break assemblies, as
described above. And they wreck gene prediction, because a transposon carries its
own open reading frames, its own splice-site-like motifs and its own promoters;
an unmasked eukaryotic genome yields thousands of predicted "genes" that are
transposase fragments.

**Masking** marks repetitive regions so that downstream tools can ignore or
down-weight them. Hard masking replaces the bases with `N`, soft masking lower-cases
them, and soft masking is almost always the right choice because it preserves the
sequence for any tool that wants it while letting repeat-aware tools skip it.
Prokaryotic genomes are repeat-poor enough — *M. genitalium* has no large repeat
families — that masking is usually skipped, which is one of several reasons
bacterial annotation is easier.

## Finding genes in a compact bacterial genome {#sec-08-prokaryotic-prediction}

Bacterial protein-coding genes are, with rare exceptions, uninterrupted: one start
codon, one reading frame, one stop codon. That makes the search space finite. A gene
finder enumerates every open reading frame (ORF) — every stretch from a potential
start codon to the next in-frame stop — and then has to decide which ORFs are real
and, for each real one, which of several possible start codons the ribosome
actually uses.

Prodigal [@hyatt2010] makes both decisions with models it trains on the genome
itself, which is why it needs no reference database. It first builds a coding model
from the hexamer composition of long, unambiguous ORFs, so that a candidate's
**coding score** reflects how closely its codon statistics resemble the genome's own
genes. It then examines the region immediately upstream of each candidate start and
learns the translation initiation signal: in most bacteria a Shine–Dalgarno
ribosome-binding site, a purine-rich motif such as AGGAGG positioned 5–10 bp before
the start codon and complementary to the 3' end of the 16S rRNA. Where no
Shine–Dalgarno signal is detectable, Prodigal switches to a generic upstream motif
model. The final start call combines coding score, upstream motif score and
start-codon type.

Prodigal is an external binary, but it is pinned in the book's `environment.yml`
and runs on this genome in well under a second, so the chapter runs it for real
rather than quoting a stored result. The command-line form is the one you would
type:

```{.bash}
prodigal -i data/sequences/mycoplasma-genitalium-NC_000908.2.fasta \
         -o mgen_prodigal.gff -f gff -a mgen_prodigal.faa -g 4
```

`-f gff` asks for GFF output rather than Prodigal's default GenBank-like format,
`-a` writes the translated proteins, and `-g 4` selects the genetic code. The same
command is issued below from Python so that its output feeds the rest of the
chapter.

```{python}
#| label: parse-gff
def parse_gff(path):
    """Yield one dictionary per GFF3 feature line, attributes already split."""
    features = []
    with open(path) as handle:
        for line in handle:
            if line.startswith("#") or not line.strip():
                continue
            fields = line.rstrip("\n").split("\t")
            if len(fields) != 9:
                continue
            attributes = dict(pair.split("=", 1)
                              for pair in fields[8].rstrip(";").split(";")
                              if "=" in pair)
            features.append({"seqid": fields[0], "source": fields[1],
                             "type": fields[2], "start": int(fields[3]),
                             "end": int(fields[4]), "score": fields[5],
                             "strand": fields[6], "phase": fields[7],
                             "attributes": attributes})
    return features

predicted = parse_gff(gff_code4)
print(f"{len(predicted)} predicted CDS features")
print(Counter(feature["attributes"]["start_type"] for feature in predicted))
```

The start-codon distribution is itself informative: ATG dominates, but GTG and TTG
together account for a substantial minority. A gene finder that only considered ATG
would truncate every one of those genes at the first internal ATG, shortening the
protein and shifting the annotated promoter region.

## Comparing two annotations of the same genome {#sec-08-agreement}

The RefSeq annotation of this genome was produced by the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome
Annotation Pipeline [@tatusova2016], which combines *ab initio* prediction with
protein homology evidence and curated rules. It is a useful comparator precisely
because it was built differently from the Prodigal run.

```{python}
#| label: parse-refseq
reference = parse_gff(book_data("annotation", "mycoplasma-genitalium.gff"))
print(Counter(feature["type"] for feature in reference).most_common())
```

Count the feature types before anything else. There are 546 `gene` features but only
524 `CDS` features, and 20 `pseudogene` features; the difference is made up of the
tRNA, rRNA and other structural RNA genes, which have a `gene` parent but no CDS
child. Conflating "number of gene lines" with "number of proteins" is the single
most common arithmetic error in annotation summaries.

The right way to ask whether two annotations agree is to match genes by their
**stop codon**, not their start. The stop is determined by the reading frame and is
rarely ambiguous; the start is a prediction in its own right. On the plus strand the
stop is at the feature's `end` coordinate, on the minus strand at its `start`.

```{python}
#| label: match-genes
reference_cds = [f for f in reference if f["type"] == "CDS"]

def stop_key(feature):
    """Strand plus the coordinate of the 3' end: a frame-anchored gene identity."""
    return (feature["strand"],
            feature["end"] if feature["strand"] == "+" else feature["start"])

reference_by_stop = {stop_key(f): f for f in reference_cds}
predicted_by_stop = {stop_key(f): f for f in predicted}
shared = set(reference_by_stop) & set(predicted_by_stop)

print(f"RefSeq CDS        : {len(reference_cds)}")
print(f"Prodigal CDS      : {len(predicted)}")
print(f"shared 3' ends    : {len(shared)}")
print(f"RefSeq only       : {len(set(reference_by_stop) - set(predicted_by_stop))}")
print(f"Prodigal only     : {len(set(predicted_by_stop) - set(reference_by_stop))}")
```

Of 524 RefSeq coding sequences and 527 Prodigal predictions, 501 share a 3' end.
That is roughly 95% gene-level agreement between an evidence-informed curated pipeline and
a database-free *ab initio* run, which is the usual state of affairs for a compact
bacterial genome and should not be extrapolated to anything larger or more
repetitive.

The 23 RefSeq-only features are the more interesting side of the disagreement.

```{python}
#| label: refseq-only
reference_only = set(reference_by_stop) - set(predicted_by_stop)
pseudo = [key for key in reference_only
          if reference_by_stop[key]["attributes"].get("pseudo") == "true"]
print(f"{len(pseudo)} of {len(reference_only)} RefSeq-only features are pseudogenes")

```

Nineteen of the 23 are annotated pseudogenes: genes that homology evidence shows
were once functional but that now carry a frameshift or an internal stop. RefSeq
records them because a reader needs to know the locus exists and is broken.
Prodigal does not report them because they are not open reading frames. Neither
behaviour is a bug; they answer different questions. Five of these RefSeq CDS
features have a length that is not a multiple of three, which is a deliberate
signal that the feature spans a frameshift and must not be naively translated.

Boundary disagreements are the other, larger, class.

```{python}
#| label: start-offsets
def start_offset(reference_feature, predicted_feature):
    """Signed distance from the RefSeq start to the Prodigal start, in bp."""
    if reference_feature["strand"] == "+":
        return predicted_feature["start"] - reference_feature["start"]
    return reference_feature["end"] - predicted_feature["end"]

offsets = {key: start_offset(reference_by_stop[key], predicted_by_stop[key])
           for key in shared}
identical = sum(1 for value in offsets.values() if value == 0)
print(f"identical at both ends: {identical} of {len(shared)}")
print(f"start-codon disagreements: {len(shared) - identical}")
in_frame = sum(1 for value in offsets.values() if value and value % 3 == 0)
print(f"of which in-frame (a different start in the same frame): {in_frame}")
```

Of the 501 genes both methods find, 464 agree on both coordinates exactly. Of the
37 that differ, 36 differ by a multiple of three: the two methods chose different
start codons within the same reading frame. The single exception is a RefSeq
pseudogene annotated across a frameshift, whose CDS length is deliberately not a
multiple of three. This is the classic
boundary disagreement and it has a biological cause. Many bacterial genes have
several in-frame candidate starts within the first 60 bp, the ribosome-binding
signal is weak or absent, and the choice between them rests on a probabilistic
model rather than on evidence. Two models trained on different data choose
differently. The consequence is a protein that is a handful of residues longer or
shorter at the N-terminus — usually harmless, occasionally fatal if the difference
removes a signal peptide or a start-proximal catalytic residue.

## Gene prediction in eukaryotes {#sec-08-eukaryotic}

Everything above depended on the assumption that a gene is one uninterrupted reading
frame. In eukaryotes it is not, and every part of the problem becomes harder.

A eukaryotic protein-coding gene is transcribed as a pre-mRNA containing **exons**
separated by **introns** that are spliced out. The spliceosome recognises short,
degenerate signals: a GT at the intron's 5' end, an AG at its 3' end, a branch point
and a polypyrimidine tract. Those motifs are far too short to locate reliably on
their own — a genome contains a GT every 16 bp by chance — so a predictor must
combine them with coding-potential models on both sides and with constraints on
intron length. Human introns range from under 100 bp to over a megabase, so the
search space is enormous. **Alternative splicing** then means that the correct
answer is not one gene model but several: the same locus can produce transcripts
with different exon content, different reading frames and different protein
products. A tool asked to report a single model per locus is being asked the wrong
question.

*Ab initio* eukaryotic predictors such as AUGUSTUS [@stanke2006] use generalised
hidden Markov models whose states correspond to the parts of a gene — intergenic,
5' UTR, initial exon, intron, internal exon, terminal exon — and whose parameters
must be trained on a set of known genes from a related species. Run on a novel
genome with mis-matched training parameters, they produce gene sets whose counts can
be wrong by a factor of two.

Modern practice is therefore **evidence-based annotation**: predictions are made in
the presence of data that directly observes transcription. RNA-seq reads aligned
with a spliced aligner give exon boundaries and splice junctions empirically — a
junction supported by 200 reads is not a hypothesis — and full-length long-read
cDNA gives complete models. Proteins from related species, aligned to the genome,
confirm coding regions and reading frames. A combiner reconciles all three into a
consensus gene set, so the annotation's quality is bounded by the tissue coverage
of the evidence: a gene expressed only in a developmental stage you did not sample
will be annotated badly or not at all.

::: {.callout-note title="Annotation versions are not interchangeable"}
Ensembl [@cunningham2022] and RefSeq [@sayers2021] annotate the same human genome
and do not produce identical gene models, and each changes between releases. Gene
counts, exon boundaries and even the set of genes considered protein-coding move.
Record the exact annotation release used in any analysis — Ensembl 110, RefSeq
GCF_000001405.40-RS_2023_10 — because a count computed against one release cannot
be compared with a count computed against another.
:::

## From a predicted protein to a biological function {#sec-08-functional}

Structural annotation says where the genes are. **Functional annotation** says what
they do, and it is almost entirely an exercise in inference by similarity.

The simplest form is **homology transfer**: search the predicted protein against a
curated database with BLAST [@altschul1990] or a more sensitive profile method,
and copy the description of the best hit. This works when the hit is strong, nearly
full length and to a protein whose function was experimentally determined. It fails
in characteristic ways. Similarity over a shared domain does not imply shared
overall function; a protein kinase domain tells you the protein phosphorylates
something, not what. Enzymes in the same superfamily can differ in substrate.
And best-hit transfer confuses **orthologues** — genes in different species that
descend from a single ancestral gene via speciation, and usually do keep their
function — with **paralogues**, which arose by duplication within a lineage and
frequently diverge in function. Orthology-aware resources exist because the best
BLAST hit is often a paralogue.

**Domain annotation** is more conservative and more durable. Pfam [@mistry2021]
models protein families and domains as profile hidden Markov models built from
curated seed alignments, and searching a protein against them with HMMER
[@eddy2011] reports which domains are present, in what order, and over which
residues. InterPro [@paysanlafosse2023] integrates Pfam with a dozen other
signature databases and maps the result onto Gene Ontology terms. A domain
architecture is a statement about the protein's parts and is far less likely to be
wrong than a transferred description, which is why curated resources show domains
and evidence rather than a single functional label.

::: {.callout-warning title="Annotation errors propagate and compound"}
When a protein is annotated by copying the description of its best database hit,
and that description was itself copied from an earlier best hit, the chain of
inference can be many steps long and no step is recorded. A single mis-annotation
in a well-populated family can be re-copied into hundreds of genomes within a few
years, and the resulting consensus looks like independent corroboration. The
defences are specific: prefer domain-level and orthology-based assignments to
best-hit transfer; demand alignment coverage of at least 70–80% of both query and
subject before transferring a description; treat a hit whose own annotation carries
an electronic evidence code as a hypothesis, not a fact; and propagate the evidence
alongside the label so the next person can re-evaluate it.
:::

It is worth measuring how much of a well-studied proteome is still unexplained.

```{python}
#| label: unknown-function
products = [f["attributes"].get("product", "") for f in reference_cds]
hypothetical = sum(1 for p in products if "hypothetical" in p)
duf = sum(1 for p in products if "DUF" in p)
print(f"hypothetical protein : {hypothetical}")
print(f"domain of unknown function (DUF): {duf}")
print(f"combined: {100 * (hypothetical + duf) / len(products):.1f}% of coding sequences")
```

Just over 10% of the proteome of one of the most intensively studied minimal
organisms is still unannotated after three decades of work on it. That is a useful
calibration for how much of any new genome's functional annotation is genuinely
known.

### Controlled vocabularies and evidence codes

Free-text product descriptions cannot be computed on: `DNA polymerase III subunit
beta`, `DNA pol III beta` and `beta clamp` are the same thing to a reader and three
different strings to a program. Controlled vocabularies solve this by assigning
stable identifiers to concepts.

The **Gene Ontology** [@ashburner2000; @go2023] is the most widely used. It provides
three independent hierarchies — molecular function, biological process and cellular
component — organised as a directed acyclic graph in which a term may have several
parents and in which an annotation to a term implies annotation to all its
ancestors. A gene annotated to `GO:0003887` (DNA-directed DNA polymerase activity)
is thereby also annotated to the broader nucleotidyltransferase and catalytic
activity terms, which is what makes enrichment analysis possible. **EC numbers**
provide a complementary four-level classification of enzymatic reactions
(`2.7.7.7` is DNA-directed DNA polymerase), and KEGG [@kanehisa2000] maps gene
products onto metabolic and signalling pathways.

Every GO annotation carries an **evidence code** stating how it was arrived at.
`EXP` and its children mark experimental evidence; `IDA` is a direct assay; `IMP`
is inference from a mutant phenotype; `ISS` is inference from sequence similarity,
assigned by a curator; `IEA` is inferred from electronic annotation, assigned
automatically with no curator review. The RefSeq GFF carries these in its
`go_function`, `go_process` and `go_component` attributes.

```{python}
#| label: evidence-codes
evidence = Counter()
with_go = 0
for feature in reference_cds:
    has_go = False
    for key in ("go_function", "go_process", "go_component"):
        if key in feature["attributes"]:
            has_go = True
            for item in feature["attributes"][key].split(","):
                parts = item.split("|")
                if len(parts) >= 4:
                    evidence[parts[3]] += 1
    with_go += has_go

print(f"CDS with at least one GO term: {with_go} of {len(reference_cds)}")
print("evidence codes:", dict(evidence))
```

Every one of the 985 GO annotations in this file carries the code `IEA`. Not one
was reviewed by a curator for this organism. This is entirely normal — the
overwhelming majority of GO annotations in any genome are IEA — but it changes how
you should read an enrichment result. An enrichment of a GO term among your
differentially expressed genes, computed against an IEA-only background, is a
statement about which sequence features your gene set shares, filtered through a
transfer pipeline. It is a hypothesis-generating observation, not evidence that the
process is active.

::: {.callout-tip title="Read the evidence code before the term"}
When a functional enrichment result surprises you, retrieve the evidence codes for
the genes driving it. A term driven entirely by IEA annotations on hypothetical
proteins is a different kind of finding from one driven by IDA annotations on
characterised enzymes, and only the second justifies a confident claim in a
discussion section.
:::

## The anatomy of GFF3 and GTF {#sec-08-gff3}

Annotation is exchanged as tab-delimited feature tables, and almost every
frustrating bug in a genomics pipeline is a misunderstanding of one of nine
columns. A GFF3 line has exactly these fields, in order: `seqid`, `source`, `type`,
`start`, `end`, `score`, `strand`, `phase`, `attributes`.

```{python}
#| label: gff-columns
first_cds = next(f for f in reference if f["type"] == "CDS")
for name in ("seqid", "source", "type", "start", "end", "score", "strand", "phase"):
    print(f"{name:8s} = {first_cds[name]}")
for name in ("ID", "Parent", "gene", "product", "protein_id", "transl_table"):
    print(f"  attr {name:12s} = {first_cds['attributes'].get(name)}")
```

`seqid` must match the FASTA identifier exactly, and the commonest cause of an empty
result from a tool that takes both files is a mismatch between `NC_000908.2` here
and `NC_000908` or `chr1` there. `source` records the program or database that made
the claim, which lets you filter a mixed annotation by provenance — here RefSeq
genes carry `RefSeq` while their CDS children carry `Protein Homology`, recording
that the coding region was called from protein alignment evidence. `type` should be
a Sequence Ontology term. `score` and `phase` are `.` when not applicable; `phase`
on a CDS is 0, 1 or 2 and gives the number of bases to skip at the start of that
feature to reach the first complete codon, which matters only for CDS split across
multiple lines.

::: {.callout-warning title="Coordinates are not interchangeable between formats"}
GFF3, GTF, SAM and VCF are **1-based and inclusive**: a feature from 686 to 1828
includes both endpoints and has length 1828 − 686 + 1 = 1143 bp. BED and the
internal coordinates of most programming libraries are **0-based and half-open**:
the same interval is written 685–1828 and its length is simply 1828 − 685. Python
and R slicing inherit this split — Python strings are 0-based half-open, R vectors
are 1-based inclusive. Converting in the wrong direction shifts every feature by
one base, which silently changes reading frames, moves variants between codons, and
produces proteins that are almost but not quite right. Write the conversion once,
in a named function, and test it on a feature whose sequence you know.
:::

The `attributes` column is a semicolon-separated list of `tag=value` pairs, and it
is where the structure lives. `ID` is unique within the file; `Parent` points at
another feature's `ID` and builds the hierarchy. In GFF3 the relationship is
explicit and may be many levels deep: gene → mRNA → exon and CDS. GTF, the older
format that many RNA-seq tools still require, instead encodes the same hierarchy
implicitly through `gene_id` and `transcript_id` attributes on every line, and uses
spaces and quotes rather than `=`. A GTF parser applied to a GFF3 file will often
run without error and return nothing.

```{python}
#| label: parent-child
by_id = {f["attributes"]["ID"]: f for f in reference if "ID" in f["attributes"]}
children = Counter()
for feature in reference:
    parent = feature["attributes"].get("Parent")
    if parent in by_id:
        children[(by_id[parent]["type"], feature["type"])] += 1
for (parent_type, child_type), number in children.most_common():
    print(f"{parent_type:12s} -> {child_type:8s} : {number}")
```

Two hierarchies are visible. Protein-coding genes have a single CDS child and no
exon feature, because a bacterial CDS is contiguous. The structural RNA genes have
an RNA-level child which in turn has an `exon` child. That asymmetry is the second
classic pitfall: **CDS and exon are not the same feature**. An exon is what is
present in the mature transcript, including untranslated regions; a CDS is the
translated part. A read-counting tool configured to count over `exon` features will
report nothing for a bacterial annotation that contains CDS features only, and will
count UTR reads in a eukaryotic annotation that contains both. Always check which
feature type your counter is using.

The third pitfall is **discontinuous features**. A spliced CDS is written as several
lines sharing one `ID`, not as one line spanning the introns. Code that treats each
line as a separate gene will multiply your gene count by the mean exon number;
code that takes `min(start)` and `max(end)` will include the introns in the length.
Group by `ID` and sum the segment lengths.

## Assessing and releasing an annotation {#sec-08-quality-submission}

An annotation needs its own quality assessment, separate from the assembly's.

Start with internal consistency. Every CDS length should be a multiple of three
unless it is explicitly marked as a pseudogene or as partial; every complete CDS
should begin with a valid start codon and end with a stop codon in the chosen
translation table; no CDS should contain an internal stop.

```{python}
#| label: sanity-check
stop_codons = {"TAA", "TAG"}          # genetic code 4: TGA encodes tryptophan
complement = str.maketrans("ACGT", "TGCA")

def cds_sequence(feature):
    """Extract a CDS in its coding orientation, 1-based inclusive coordinates."""
    segment = genome[feature["start"] - 1:feature["end"]]
    return segment if feature["strand"] == "+" else segment.translate(complement)[::-1]

bad_length = sum(1 for f in predicted if (f["end"] - f["start"] + 1) % 3)
bad_stop = sum(1 for f in predicted if cds_sequence(f)[-3:] not in stop_codons)
internal = sum(1 for f in predicted
               if any(cds_sequence(f)[i:i + 3] in stop_codons
                      for i in range(0, len(cds_sequence(f)) - 3, 3)))
print(f"length not a multiple of three : {bad_length}")
print(f"does not end in a stop codon   : {bad_stop}")
print(f"contains an internal stop codon: {internal}")
```

All three counts are zero, which is the expected result for a complete *ab initio*
gene set run with the right translation table. The same check applied to the
code-11 predictions is where the earlier mistake finally becomes visible.

```{python}
#| label: sanity-check-wrong-table
wrong_table = parse_gff(gff_code11)
ends_in_tga = sum(1 for f in wrong_table if cds_sequence(f)[-3:] == "TGA")
print(f"code-11 predictions ending in TGA: {ends_in_tga} of {len(wrong_table)}")
```

Under code 4, TGA is tryptophan, so 612 of the 995 code-11 predictions terminate at
a codon that is not a stop codon in this organism. A check of this kind — does the
gene set obey the rules of the genetic code you believe applies? — costs three lines
and catches an error that no amount of staring at a GFF file will reveal.

Then compare against expectation. Coding density of 85–90% is normal for bacteria;
a prediction at 60% suggests a missed translation table or a contaminated assembly,
and one at 99% suggests spurious short ORFs. Mean protein length near 300 residues
is typical; a sharp drop indicates fragmentation. BUSCO can be run on the predicted
proteins rather than the genome, which separates "the gene is absent from the
assembly" from "the gene is present but was not predicted". Checking the fraction of
proteins with a Pfam domain gives a rough measure of how much of the gene set is
plausible.

For release, the conventions are strict and worth learning early. Sequences go to
GenBank, ENA or DDBJ, which exchange records nightly, so a deposit in one appears
in all three. A prokaryotic submission is usually contigs plus a feature table;
NCBI will run PGAP on it, and the RefSeq record you download later is NCBI's
annotation, not necessarily yours. Locus tags must be requested as a registered
prefix — `MG_RS00005` follows that convention — and must be unique and stable,
because they become the handle by which your genes are cited. Product names follow
guidelines that forbid molecular weights, database identifiers and the unqualified
word "homolog". The assembly receives a versioned accession such as
`GCF_000027325.1`, and that accession, not the organism name, belongs in a methods
section.

::: {.callout-important title="Annotation provenance is part of the result"}
When you publish an analysis that depends on an annotation, record the assembly
accession, the annotation source and release, the gene finder and its version, the
translation table, and the evidence used for functional assignment. Without these a
reader cannot reproduce your gene counts, and an unreproducible gene count
undermines every statistic computed from it. This is as true for a clinical
pipeline, where an annotation release change can alter a variant's reported
consequence, as for a comparative genomics paper.
:::

## Summary {#sec-08-summary}

- An assembly is a hypothesis; check its contiguity, completeness and contamination
  before annotating, because annotation inherits every assembly error and adds its
  own.
- N50 measures contiguity, not correctness, and rewards over-aggressive assembly.
  Report it with total length, contig count, BUSCO completeness and, where a
  reference size is known, NG50.
- The translation table is a parameter you must set deliberately. On *M. genitalium*
  the bacterial default produces 995 predicted genes against 527 with the correct
  code 4, and nothing in the output signals the error.
- Prokaryotic gene finding is a solved problem to a first approximation: *ab initio*
  Prodigal and the curated RefSeq pipeline agree on the 3' end of 501 genes out of
  roughly 525, and on both ends of 464.
- Disagreements between annotations concentrate in start codons rather than in gene
  presence, because the start is a prediction and the stop is a consequence of the
  reading frame.
- Eukaryotic prediction requires external evidence — RNA-seq, long-read cDNA,
  protein alignments — because introns and alternative splicing make the *ab initio*
  problem underdetermined.
- Functional annotation is inference by similarity, and the inference chain is
  usually invisible. Prefer domain and orthology assignments to best-hit transfer,
  and read the evidence code before the term: all 985 GO annotations in this genome
  are IEA.
- GFF3 bugs are coordinate bugs. Remember that GFF3 is 1-based inclusive and BED is
  0-based half-open, that CDS and exon are different features, and that a spliced
  feature occupies several lines sharing one `ID`.

## Solutions {#sec-08-solutions}

::: {.callout-caution collapse="true" title="Solutions"}

**1.** Coding regions are GC-richer than the remainder. In an AT-rich genome,
selection on codon usage and on amino acid composition keeps coding sequence closer
to a compositional equilibrium than the unconstrained intergenic DNA, which drifts
towards the mutational bias.

```{python}
#| label: soln-08-1
coding_mask = np.zeros(len(genome), dtype=bool)
for feature in predicted:
    coding_mask[feature["start"] - 1:feature["end"]] = True

bases = np.frombuffer(genome.encode(), dtype="S1")
is_gc = (bases == b"G") | (bases == b"C")
print(f"coding GC    : {100 * is_gc[coding_mask].mean():.2f}%")
print(f"non-coding GC: {100 * is_gc[~coding_mask].mean():.2f}%")
```

**2.** Most of the short predictions are confirmed by RefSeq, but a tenth of the
Prodigal gene set is short and a quarter of those short genes have no independent
support — which is the honest statement of how much to trust a short *ab initio*
ORF.

```{python}
#| label: soln-08-2
short = [f for f in predicted if (f["end"] - f["start"] + 1) < 300]
confirmed = sum(1 for f in short if stop_key(f) in reference_by_stop)
print(f"{len(short)} predictions under 300 bp; median "
      f"{int(np.median([f['end'] - f['start'] + 1 for f in short]))} bp")
print(f"{confirmed} of them share a 3' end with a RefSeq CDS")
```

**3.** The difference is made up of the structural RNA genes and the pseudogenes,
which have `gene` features but no protein-coding CDS child of their own.

```{python}
#| label: soln-08-3
biotypes = Counter(f["attributes"].get("gene_biotype")
                   for f in reference if f["type"] == "gene")
for biotype, number in biotypes.most_common():
    print(f"{str(biotype):18s} {number}")
```

**4.** The conversion subtracts one from the start and leaves the end unchanged;
the length is then simply `end - start`.

```{python}
#| label: soln-08-4
def gff_to_bed(feature):
    """GFF3 1-based inclusive -> BED 0-based half-open."""
    return (feature["seqid"], feature["start"] - 1, feature["end"])

for feature in reference_cds[:5]:
    seqid, bed_start, bed_end = gff_to_bed(feature)
    gff_length = feature["end"] - feature["start"] + 1
    assert bed_end - bed_start == gff_length
    print(f"{seqid}\t{bed_start}\t{bed_end}\tlength {gff_length}")
```

**5.** The two directions are close to balanced, 17 upstream against 20 downstream,
so neither method is systematically more permissive than the other. The
disagreement reflects a real ambiguity — several in-frame candidate starts with a
weak or absent ribosome-binding signal — rather than a known bias that could be
corrected for. When the distinction matters, for example when the N-terminus
carries a signal peptide, the start must be settled with N-terminal proteomics
rather than with a better model.

```{python}
#| label: soln-08-5
upstream = sum(1 for value in offsets.values() if value < 0)
downstream = sum(1 for value in offsets.values() if value > 0)
print(f"Prodigal start upstream of RefSeq  : {upstream}")
print(f"Prodigal start downstream of RefSeq: {downstream}")
```

**6.** Overlaps of 1 bp (45 pairs) and 8 bp (32 pairs) are the commonest, with 4 bp
and 11 bp close behind. A 1 bp overlap is the TAATG arrangement, in which the final
A of the stop codon TAA is also the first base of the downstream ATG; a 4 bp
overlap is the ATGA arrangement, in which the whole start codon sits inside the
stop codon. Both are hallmarks of translational coupling, where the ribosome
reinitiates on the downstream cistron without releasing the message, which in turn
means the two genes are obligately co-expressed. Note that the tabulation below
pairs features by position alone and so includes some pairs on opposite strands,
which are not translationally coupled; a stricter analysis would first filter on a
shared strand.

```{python}
#| label: soln-08-6
ordered = sorted(reference_cds, key=lambda f: f["start"])
overlaps = [first["end"] - second["start"] + 1
            for first, second in zip(ordered, ordered[1:])
            if second["start"] <= first["end"]]
print(f"{len(overlaps)} overlapping adjacent pairs")
print("most frequent overlap lengths:", Counter(overlaps).most_common(5))
```

**7.** Prodigal makes nine predictions. Eight of them match an annotated RefSeq
CDS at both coordinates exactly — S, ORF3a, M, ORF6, ORF7a, ORF8, N, and the
13,218 bp first segment of ORF1ab — which is better than the exercise's framing
suggests. The failures are the instructive part. ORF1ab is reported as two separate
genes because it is translated as one polyprotein only through a programmed −1
ribosomal frameshift, and no ORF-based model represents that. The envelope protein
E (228 bp), ORF7b (132 bp) and ORF10 (117 bp) are missed entirely because they fall
below the length at which an ORF can be distinguished from chance in a 30 kb
sequence. The lesson is not that the tool is bad but that its gene model — one ORF,
one protein, no frameshifting, enough sequence to train on — does not describe a
coronavirus.

fungal genome yields thousands of transposase ORFs; and check whether the
predictions include both an *ab initio* set and an evidence-based set that were
concatenated rather than reconciled, which shows up as near-duplicate models at the
same locus.

:::

## Further reading {#sec-08-further-reading}

- @hyatt2010 is the Prodigal paper; its description of how the start-site model is
  trained explains most of the boundary disagreements seen in @sec-08-agreement.
- @seemann2014 describes Prokka, which wraps Prodigal together with tRNA, rRNA and
  functional annotation steps and is the practical starting point for annotating a
  bacterial draft.
- @tatusova2016 documents the NCBI pipeline that produced the RefSeq annotation used
  here, and is worth reading before you submit a genome and receive an annotation
  you did not make.
- @stanke2006 introduces AUGUSTUS and, more usefully, sets out clearly why
  eukaryotic gene prediction needs training data and external evidence.
- @manni2021 explains what BUSCO does and does not measure; read it before quoting a
  completeness percentage in a manuscript.
- @paysanlafosse2023 describes InterPro and the integration of member databases; it
  is the right reference for understanding what a domain-based functional assignment
  is actually claiming.
- @ashburner2000 and @go2023 together give the original rationale for the Gene
  Ontology and its current state, including the evidence-code system that governs
  how much weight an annotation deserves.
