## 3. What the book is, in one paragraph

Bioinformatics is learned the way laboratory work is learned: by doing it, making
mistakes, and developing judgement about what the results mean. This book is built on
that premise. Every figure was generated by code printed in the book, running on data
the reader downloads from public archives, and every command's output is shown as it was
actually produced — including the cases where a sensible default gives the wrong answer
and the reader has to work out why. It begins at the first command typed into a terminal
and ends with a complete, reproducible analysis written up to the standard a journal
expects.

## 4. Why it is different

Most introductory bioinformatics texts either describe methods without running them, or
run them on toy data chosen to behave. This one is organised around the opposite
discipline:

- **Nothing is illustrated with results that were never computed.** 466 code chunks,
  454 of which execute at build time; the remaining 12 are live database calls and
  installer commands, which carry their genuine captured output and are marked.
- **The failures are the lesson.** Chapter 15 runs Prodigal on *Mycoplasmoides
  genitalium* with the bacterial translation table and recovers 995 genes against
  RefSeq's 524, because 71.6% of that organism's coding sequences contain an in-frame
  TGA that the default table reads as a stop. The chapter does not mention the problem
  and move on; it measures it — 96.6% of TGA-free reference genes recovered against
  52.5% of TGA-containing ones — and then re-runs with code 4.
- **Reproducibility is demonstrated, not asserted.** All 21 datasets are listed with
  source archive, accession and SHA-256; one script fetches and verifies them; the
  whole book rebuilds with `quarto render`.

## Strategy note 1 — where to send it, and to whom

Two Chapman & Hall/CRC series fit, and they list the same commissioning editor.

- **Chapman & Hall/CRC Computational Biology Series** — its own description names
  genomics and bioinformatics and is aimed at "students, researchers, and professionals
  in all areas of computational biology". Proposals go to **Elliott Morsia**
  (Elliott.Morsia@tandf.co.uk).
- **Chapman & Hall/CRC Mathematical and Computational Biology** — which states that
  "the inclusion of concrete examples and applications and programming techniques and
  examples is highly encouraged", which is this book's whole design. Proposals go to
  Elliott Morsia as Editor for Computer Science, or Callum Fraser for Mathematics.

Send to Elliott Morsia, naming the Computational Biology Series as the fit and the
Mathematical and Computational Biology series as the alternative. Confirm the address
on the series page before sending — commissioning editors move.

Expect: an acknowledgement, then peer review of the proposal and sample chapters by two
to four reviewers, then a proposal to the editorial board, then a contract or a decline.
Three to six months is normal. Because the manuscript is finished, ask the editor
directly whether they will review the complete book rather than sample chapters; it
removes their delivery risk, which is an argument in your favour.

## Strategy note 2 — the free online edition, and Professor Mulder

**Declare the free HTML edition in the first email.** Do not let an editor discover it
later. It is not a barrier in itself — CRC publishes several books whose full text is
free online, *Computational Genomics with R* among them — but it is a commercial
decision they must make with their eyes open, and concealing it would poison the
negotiation. Say plainly: the HTML edition is free and complete, the print and EPUB
editions are the product, and name the comparator.

**Nicola Mulder is listed as a series editor of Chapman & Hall/CRC Mathematical and
Computational Biology.** That is the same person you intend to ask for a foreword, and
it changes the order of operations:

1. **Ask for the foreword first, on its own merits, and say nothing about CRC.** A
   request that arrives bundled with a publishing favour is a different and less
   attractive request, and it puts her in an awkward position as a series editor.
2. **If she accepts and the foreword is written**, then approach the editor — and at
   that point you may say the book carries a foreword by a series editor, which is a
   strong signal.
3. **If she declines the foreword**, nothing is lost and the proposal route is
   untouched.

Do not ask her to recommend the book to the series. If she wants to, she will.

Verify her current series editorship before relying on it: editorial boards change, and
the listing I read may be out of date.
