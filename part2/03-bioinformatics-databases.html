3  Biological databases and how to get data out of them

After working through this chapter you will be able to

  • distinguish primary archives, curated knowledgebases and derived resources, and state how that distinction changes the weight you give a record
  • decode an accession number, name the resource it belongs to, and explain what the digits after the full stop mean
  • justify a choice between a RefSeq and a GenBank record, and between a Swiss-Prot and a TrEMBL entry
  • dissect a GenBank feature table and a UniProtKB flat file with Biopython and extract named annotations, cross-references and evidence codes
  • construct a query against the Entrez E-utilities and the UniProt and Ensembl REST interfaces, and handle pagination and rate limits courteously
  • record the provenance of every file an analysis depends on, so that a result can be reproduced a year later

A colleague sends you a sequence and asks what it is. You paste it into a search box, and within a minute you have fifty answers. Some come from a genome that a consortium finished, polished and curated over a decade; some come from a fragment that a graduate student deposited in 2003 and never looked at again; and some come from an automatic annotation pipeline that has never been inspected by a human being. All fifty look identical on the screen. Knowing which is which is the difference between an analysis that holds up and one that quietly embarrasses you at review.

That is what this chapter is about. It is not a tour of websites. Websites change; the structural logic of public biological data does not. There are archives, which record what somebody submitted; there are knowledgebases, which record what curators believe after reading the literature; and there are derived resources, which compute something new from the first two. Each answers a different question, each fails in a different way, and reading a record without knowing which kind you are holding is the most common way that careful people reach wrong conclusions.

You will work with three bundled records throughout. The first is the SARS-CoV-2 reference genome in GenBank format (data/sequences/sars-cov-2-NC_045512.2.gb), whose feature table you will take apart feature by feature. The second is the full UniProtKB flat file for human p53 (data/proteins/P04637.txt), a 447 kB document containing 1,120 cross-references and more than 1,500 sequence features, which you will mine for evidence codes. The third is the book’s own data manifest (data/MANIFEST.tsv), which you will use as a model for recording provenance in your own projects. By the end you will have written a function that decodes any accession you are likely to meet, and a checksum routine that proves a file has not changed under you.

3.2 Three kinds of resource

A primary archive stores submissions. GenBank, the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA), the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ), the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) and the Protein Data Bank (PDB) all work this way: a submitter uploads data and metadata, the archive performs format and consistency checks, assigns an accession, and from that moment the record is permanent. Archives do not correct biology. If a submitter annotated a pseudogene as a protein-coding gene, or recorded the wrong organism, the error stays in the archive until the submitter themselves revises it. The archive’s promise is narrow and valuable: this is exactly what was deposited, and it will still be here in twenty years.

A curated knowledgebase stores conclusions. UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot, RefSeq, ClinVar’s reviewed assertions and Reactome contain records that a human curator has assembled by reading primary literature, reconciling conflicting reports and attaching evidence to each statement. A curated record is an argument, not an observation. It can be wrong, but it is wrong in a way that someone is accountable for, and it usually tells you what the claim rests on.

A derived resource computes. STRING scores protein associations from co-expression, co-occurrence, text mining and imported interactions (Szklarczyk et al. 2022); the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database holds predicted structures rather than measured ones (Varadi et al. 2021); gnomAD aggregates allele frequencies across tens of thousands of exomes and genomes (Karczewski et al. 2020). Derived resources are often the most immediately useful thing on your screen and the most dangerous to quote without qualification, because the number you are reading is a model output whose assumptions live in a methods paper you have not read.

The practical consequence is a habit. Before you use a record, ask which of the three you are holding, and let the answer set the sentence you are allowed to write. From an archive you may write “a sequence deposited as X has property Y”. From a knowledgebase you may write “X is annotated as Y, on the evidence of Z”. From a derived resource you may write “X is predicted to be Y by method M”. Those three sentences are not interchangeable, and reviewers notice.

The INSDC and what mirroring means

GenBank, ENA and DDBJ form the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (INSDC) (Arita, Karsch-Mizrachi, and Cochrane 2020). They exchange new and updated records daily, so a sequence submitted to any one of them appears in all three within about 24 hours, under the same accession. The record content is the same; the presentation is not. GenBank shows you a flat file, ENA shows you an XML-or-flat-file service with a different browser, and DDBJ a third. A paper citing an ENA accession and a paper citing the same GenBank accession are citing the same data. Raw reads behave the same way: SRA, ENA and DDBJ’s DRA mirror each other.

3.3 Accessions, versions and what the dot means

Readers lose days to accession numbers, usually by assuming that two strings that look alike mean alike. They do not. The prefix tells you which resource minted the identifier and, in the case of RefSeq, how the record was produced; the digits after the full stop are a version, but the thing being versioned differs between resources.

The RefSeq prefixes are worth memorising because they encode provenance directly. NC_ is a complete genomic molecule that has been curated; NM_ and NP_ are a curated messenger RNA and its protein product; XM_ and XP_ are the same two things produced by an automatic annotation pipeline with no human review (Tatusova et al. 2016). The difference between NM_000546 and a hypothetical XM_ record for the same locus is the difference between a transcript a curator vouched for and a transcript a program proposed. Genome assemblies carry two parallel accessions: GCA_ is the GenBank assembly, exactly as the submitting group built it, and GCF_ is the RefSeq copy of that assembly, with NCBI’s own annotation attached. The underlying contigs are usually identical; the gene models frequently are not.

The following function encodes those rules so that you can check an identifier instead of guessing at it.

import re

ACCESSION_PATTERNS = [
    (r"^NC_\d+",  "RefSeq complete genomic molecule, curated"),
    (r"^NM_\d+",  "RefSeq messenger RNA, curated"),
    (r"^NP_\d+",  "RefSeq protein, curated"),
    (r"^XM_\d+",  "RefSeq messenger RNA, predicted by automated annotation"),
    (r"^XP_\d+",  "RefSeq protein, predicted by automated annotation"),
    (r"^GCF_\d+", "RefSeq genome assembly"),
    (r"^GCA_\d+", "GenBank (INSDC) genome assembly"),
    (r"^ENS[A-Z]*G\d{11}", "Ensembl gene"),
    (r"^ENS[A-Z]*T\d{11}", "Ensembl transcript"),
    (r"^rs\d+",   "dbSNP variant"),
    (r"^[OPQ][0-9][A-Z0-9]{3}[0-9]$", "UniProtKB accession"),
    (r"^[A-NR-Z][0-9]([A-Z][A-Z0-9]{2}[0-9]){1,2}$", "UniProtKB accession"),
    (r"^[0-9][A-Za-z0-9]{3}$", "PDB entry"),
    (r"^[A-Z]{1,2}\d{5,6}", "INSDC nucleotide, author-submitted"),
]


def describe_accession(accession):
    """Name the resource an accession belongs to and report its version."""
    stem, _, version = accession.partition(".")
    for pattern, meaning in ACCESSION_PATTERNS:
        if re.match(pattern, stem):
            return f"{meaning}, " + (f"version {version}" if version else "unversioned")
    return "unrecognised identifier"

Running it over a set of identifiers drawn from the datasets used in this book shows how much information a well-formed accession carries before you have fetched anything at all.

examples = ["NC_045512.2", "NM_000546.6", "NP_000537.3", "XM_011525846.3",
            "GCF_000027325.1", "GCA_000001405.29", "P04637",
            "ENSG00000141510", "1TUP", "rs28934578", "MN908947.3"]

for accession in examples:
    print(f"{accession:<17} {describe_accession(accession)}")
#> NC_045512.2       RefSeq complete genomic molecule, curated, version 2
#> NM_000546.6       RefSeq messenger RNA, curated, version 6
#> NP_000537.3       RefSeq protein, curated, version 3
#> XM_011525846.3    RefSeq messenger RNA, predicted by automated annotation, version 3
#> GCF_000027325.1   RefSeq genome assembly, version 1
#> GCA_000001405.29  GenBank (INSDC) genome assembly, version 29
#> P04637            UniProtKB accession, unversioned
#> ENSG00000141510   Ensembl gene, unversioned
#> 1TUP              PDB entry, unversioned
#> rs28934578        dbSNP variant, unversioned
#> MN908947.3        INSDC nucleotide, author-submitted, version 3

The last line repays attention. MN908947.3 is the author-submitted GenBank record for the Wuhan-Hu-1 isolate; NC_045512.2 is the RefSeq record derived from it. They contain the same 29,903 bases. They are not the same record, they have separate version histories, and a methods section that cites one when the analysis used the other is wrong in a way that is tedious to correct later.

A version is not an afterthought

The suffix after the full stop means different things in different resources, and all of them matter. In an INSDC or RefSeq nucleotide accession, the version increments whenever the sequence changes — NC_045512.1 and NC_045512.2 are different strings of bases. In a GCA_/GCF_ assembly accession, the suffix versions the whole assembly, so new scaffolding alone will increment it. Ensembl versions genes and transcripts separately from the assembly (ENST00000269305.9), and increments the suffix when the model changes. An unversioned accession such as NC_045512 silently resolves to whatever is current on the day you fetch it, which means an unversioned accession in a methods section does not specify the data you used. Always write the version.

3.4 The anatomy of a nucleotide record

A GenBank flat file has three parts: a header of record-level metadata, a feature table of annotations attached to coordinate ranges, and the sequence itself. Biopython’s SeqIO parses all three into a SeqRecord, from which the header fields come out as annotations.

from Bio import SeqIO

genome = SeqIO.read(book_data("sequences", "sars-cov-2-NC_045512.2.gb"), "genbank")

print(f"Accession:  {genome.name}")
#> Accession:  NC_045512
print(f"Version:    {genome.id}")
#> Version:    NC_045512.2
print(f"Molecule:   {len(genome.seq):,} bp, "
      f"{genome.annotations['molecule_type']}, {genome.annotations['topology']}")
#> Molecule:   29,903 bp, ss-RNA, linear
print(f"Updated:    {genome.annotations['date']}")
#> Updated:    18-JUL-2020
print(f"Keywords:   {genome.annotations['keywords']}")
#> Keywords:   ['RefSeq']
print(f"Project:    {genome.dbxrefs}")
#> Project:    ['BioProject:PRJNA485481']
print(f"Lineage:    {' > '.join(genome.annotations['taxonomy'][-4:])}")
#> Lineage:    Orthocoronavirinae > Betacoronavirus > Sarbecovirus > Betacoronavirus pandemicum

Three of those fields are already doing work. The keyword RefSeq confirms this is the curated copy rather than the submitted one. The DBLINK field points at BioProject PRJNA485481, which is the umbrella record that ties the genome to the submission it came from — following project identifiers is often the fastest route from a sequence back to the study that produced it. And the date is the date of the record, not of the sample: this record was last updated in July 2020, which is a fact about curation, not about the virus.

The feature table is where biology lives. Each feature has a type, a location and a set of qualifiers.

from collections import Counter

for feature_type, count in Counter(f.type for f in genome.features).most_common():
    print(f"{feature_type:>12}  {count}")
#>  mat_peptide  26
#>          CDS  12
#>         gene  11
#>    stem_loop  5
#>       source  1
#>        5'UTR  1
#>        3'UTR  1

Eleven gene features but twelve CDS features is not a bug, and spotting that asymmetry is exactly the kind of reading this section is teaching. The extra coding sequence belongs to ORF1a, which shares a gene feature with ORF1ab because the two products come from the same transcript: a ribosomal frameshift partway along produces either the short or the long polyprotein. The feature table encodes that explicitly as a joined location.

coding_sequences = [f for f in genome.features if f.type == "CDS"]

for feature in coding_sequences[:5]:
    gene = feature.qualifiers["gene"][0]
    segments = " + ".join(f"{int(p.start) + 1}..{int(p.end)}"
                          for p in feature.location.parts)
    protein = feature.qualifiers["protein_id"][0]
    print(f"{gene:<7} {protein:<16} {segments:<26} "
          f"{len(feature.qualifiers['translation'][0]):>5} aa")
#> ORF1ab  YP_009724389.1   266..13468 + 13468..21555   7096 aa
#> ORF1ab  YP_009725295.1   266..13483                  4405 aa
#> S       YP_009724390.1   21563..25384                1273 aa
#> ORF3a   YP_009724391.1   25393..26220                 275 aa
#> E       YP_009724392.1   26245..26472                  75 aa

The first row shows the joined location 266..13468 + 13468..21555. Those two intervals overlap at position 13468, which is the hallmark of a programmed −1 ribosomal frameshift rather than a splice junction: the ribosome slips back one base and continues in a new reading frame, so one base is read twice. A tool that assumes a join() always means splicing will translate this feature incorrectly. Reading the location, rather than trusting a start and an end coordinate, is what prevents that.

Features other than CDS carry annotation that is easy to overlook. The 26 mat_peptide features record the individual non-structural proteins that proteases cut out of the ORF1ab polyprotein, and they are the only place in the record where nsp12, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, appears as its own entity.

mature_peptides = [f for f in genome.features if f.type == "mat_peptide"]

for feature in mature_peptides[:4]:
    product = feature.qualifiers["product"][0]
    print(f"{product:<24} {int(feature.location.start) + 1:>6}..{int(feature.location.end)}")
#> leader protein              266..805
#> nsp2                        806..2719
#> nsp3                       2720..8554
#> nsp4                       8555..10054
print(f"... {len(mature_peptides)} mature peptides in total")
#> ... 26 mature peptides in total

3.5 The anatomy of a protein record

UniProtKB is one database in two sections (Bateman et al. 2022). Swiss-Prot entries have been read, assembled and signed off by a curator; TrEMBL entries are translations of coding sequences that have been annotated automatically and never reviewed. There are roughly six hundred thousand of the former and more than two hundred million of the latter. Every analysis you run against “all of UniProt” is therefore dominated by machine annotation unless you filter, which is what the reviewed:true term in a UniProt query does.

What manual curation buys is best seen by opening a curated record and counting. Biopython parses the flat-file format with Bio.SwissProt (Cock et al. 2009).

from Bio import SwissProt

with open(book_data("proteins", "P04637.txt")) as handle:
    p53 = SwissProt.read(handle)

print(f"Entry name:      {p53.entry_name} ({p53.data_class})")
#> Entry name:      P53_HUMAN (Reviewed)
print(f"Primary accession: {p53.accessions[0]}  "
      f"(+{len(p53.accessions) - 1} secondary accessions)")
#> Primary accession: P04637  (+26 secondary accessions)
print(f"Gene:            {p53.gene_name[0]['Name']}")
#> Gene:            TP53
print(f"Length:          {p53.sequence_length} aa")
#> Length:          393 aa
print(f"Created:         {p53.created[0]};  annotation updated {p53.annotation_update[0]}")
#> Created:         13-AUG-1987;  annotation updated 02-SEP-2026
print(f"Literature:      {len(p53.references)} cited references")
#> Literature:      238 cited references
print(f"Cross-references: {len(p53.cross_references)}")

print(f"Sequence features: {len(p53.features)}")
#> Sequence features: 1523

The data class reads Reviewed, which is how a Swiss-Prot entry identifies itself; a TrEMBL entry would read Unreviewed. The 26 secondary accessions are the fossil record of entries that were later merged into this one, and they still resolve, which is why an old accession in an old paper does not break. The counts at the bottom are the curation dividend: 238 papers read, 1,120 links to other resources, and over 1,500 annotated features on a protein of just 393 amino acids.

Those features are typed, and the types matter. CHAIN marks the mature polypeptide, DNA_BIND a DNA-binding region, REGION a functional or structural segment, VARIANT a known sequence variant, MOD_RES a modified residue, and HELIX/STRAND/TURN secondary structure transferred from solved structures.

feature_counts = Counter(feature.type for feature in p53.features)
for feature_type, count in feature_counts.most_common(8):
    print(f"{feature_type:>10}  {count}")
#>    VARIANT  1363

The DNA-binding region at residues 102–292 is the one to remember: it is where the great majority of cancer-associated missense mutations fall, and it is the domain captured in the bundled structure 1tup.pdb (Cho et al. 1994). Counting the variants that the curators flagged as germline Li-Fraumeni syndrome alleles makes the point quantitatively.

Evidence codes, and why they are the most useful part of a record

Every functional statement in a curated entry carries an evidence tag. Gene Ontology (GO) annotations (Ashburner et al. 2000; Aleksander et al. 2023) attached to a UniProt entry each end in a three-letter code that says how the assignment was made. IDA means inferred from direct assay, IMP from mutant phenotype, IPI from physical interaction, IGI from genetic interaction and IEP from expression pattern; those are experimental. ISS means inferred from sequence similarity, TAS traceable author statement, and IEA inferred from electronic annotation with no curator involvement at all. An IEA annotation is a hypothesis; an IDA annotation is somebody’s figure.

Figure 3.2 shows the evidence profile of p53’s GO annotations alongside the resources the entry links out to.

EXPERIMENTAL = {"EXP", "IDA", "IPI", "IMP", "IGI", "IEP",
                "HTP", "HDA", "HMP", "HGI", "HEP"}

go_annotations = [x for x in p53.cross_references if x[0] == "GO"]
code_counts = Counter(x[3].split(":")[0] for x in go_annotations)
database_counts = Counter(x[0] for x in p53.cross_references).most_common(10)

fig, (ax_evidence, ax_databases) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(6.6, 3.4))

ordered = sorted(code_counts.items(), key=lambda item: item[1])

                  for code, _ in ordered], [n for _, n in ordered], color=PALETTE[1])
ax_evidence.set(xlabel="GO annotations", title="(a) Evidence behind GO terms")
ax_evidence.tick_params(axis="y", labelsize=7)
ax_evidence.grid(axis="y", visible=False)

                  [n for _, n in database_counts][::-1], color=PALETTE[0])
ax_databases.set(xlabel="Cross-references", title="(b) Databases linked from P04637")
ax_databases.tick_params(axis="y", labelsize=7)
ax_databases.grid(axis="y", visible=False)
fig.tight_layout()

      f"({100 * experimental / len(go_annotations):.0f}%) rest on experimental evidence")
#> 104 of 119 GO annotations (87%) rest on experimental evidence

Panel (a) is the argument for using Swiss-Prot in preference to a bulk download: 104 of this entry’s 119 GO annotations are experimental, and only eight are electronic inferences. Run the same count on a TrEMBL entry and almost everything will be IEA. Panel (b) shows the second thing curation buys, which is integration: the entry is simultaneously an index into 311 PDB structures, 46 Reactome pathways, 22 Orphanet disease records and 17 DrugBank compounds. This is why a UniProt accession is usually the best starting point for a protein-centred question, whatever the question is.

Read the evidence before you read the annotation

When a database tells you a protein does something, find the evidence tag before you quote the claim. In a UniProt web page the evidence appears as a small coloured icon beside each statement; in the flat file it is the ECO: term in the feature’s evidence qualifier or the three-letter code in a GO cross-reference. A function supported only by IEA or ISS is a lead to follow, not a result to build on.

3.6 Structures, genomes and the browser model

The Protein Data Bank is the archive of experimentally determined three-dimensional structures, now jointly managed as the worldwide PDB (Berman 2000; Burley et al. 2022). A PDB entry is a four-character code such as 1TUP and contains atomic coordinates together with the experimental conditions that produced them. Two attributes of a PDB entry govern how much you should trust any particular atom: the method (X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy or cryo-electron microscopy) and, for crystal structures, the resolution. A 1.5 Å structure resolves individual side-chain positions; a 3.5 Å structure resolves the backbone and little else, and side-chain orientations in it are largely modelled rather than observed. The structural bioinformatics chapter takes this up in detail using the bundled 1tup.pdb and 1mbn.pdb files.

Ensembl takes a different approach from an archive (Cunningham et al. 2021). It is built around a genome assembly as a coordinate system, with every other kind of data — gene models, transcripts, regulatory features, variants, comparative alignments — attached to intervals on that assembly. This is the genome browser model, and it has one consequence that beginners trip over constantly: every coordinate is meaningful only with respect to an assembly. Position 7,676,154 on chromosome 17 is a different base in GRCh37 and in GRCh38. Ensembl labels each release with its assembly, and so must you.

A variant list, a BED file or a set of peak calls is therefore meaningless without the assembly it was called against, and converting between assemblies with a liftover tool is lossy: some intervals fail to map and a few map to the wrong place. Record the assembly name in the file, in the filename and in the methods section, and if you receive coordinates with no assembly stated, ask before you use them.

The browser model also explains the Ensembl identifier scheme. ENSG prefixes a gene, ENST a transcript, ENSP a protein and ENSE an exon, each with its own version suffix because a gene model can be revised without the gene being redefined. Species other than human carry a species code in the middle: ENSMUSG for mouse.

3.7 Specialised and derived resources

Beyond the general archives sit resources organised around one kind of question. You will not use all of them, but you need to know which one to reach for.

For human variation, dbSNP accessions every submitted variant with an rs identifier (Sherry 2001), and is a registry rather than an assessment: an rs number says a variant has been observed, not that it matters. ClinVar records clinical interpretations of variants, each with a submitter, a review status and a date (Landrum et al. 2017); its “review status” stars are the field you must read, because a one-star assertion from a single laboratory and a four-star expert-panel assertion are very different objects. The bundled data/variants/tp53_clinvar.vcf holds 3,888 ClinVar records across TP53 and is used in the clinical genomics chapter. Interpretation of a clinical variant follows the ACMG/AMP framework rather than a database lookup (Richards et al. 2015). OMIM is the long-running catalogue of Mendelian phenotypes and the genes behind them (Amberger et al. 2018), and remains the best narrative account of what is known about a disease gene.

For functional genomics data, GEO archives processed expression datasets with their experimental design (Barrett et al. 2012), while SRA and ENA archive the raw reads underneath. The division matters when you want to reanalyse something: GEO gives you the authors’ processed matrix, and the archives give you the reads you need in order to disagree with it.

For pathways and interactions, Reactome is a curated, peer-reviewed pathway knowledgebase (Milacic et al. 2023); KEGG organises molecular interaction networks into maps with their own identifier space (Kanehisa 2000); STRING scores functional associations and is explicitly a derived resource (Szklarczyk et al. 2022); and the Gene Ontology supplies the controlled vocabulary that most functional analysis depends on (Ashburner et al. 2000). The bundled data/networks/p53_string_network.tsv and data/annotation/human_go_annotations.tsv are drawn from the last two and are used in the systems biology chapter.

Human data carries obligations

Aggregate resources such as gnomAD publish allele frequencies precisely because individual-level human genomes cannot be published freely. Controlled-access repositories exist for the individual-level data, and access to them is granted for a stated purpose under a data access agreement that binds you personally. Downloading human genomic data because a URL happens to work is not consent, and the question of what you may do with it is legal as well as ethical. The chapter on ethical, legal and social issues sets out the framework; the obligation applies from the first file you download.

3.8 Programmatic access

Clicking is fine for one record and indefensible for a thousand. Every major resource exposes a programmatic interface, and learning two of them — NCBI’s Entrez E-utilities and the REST services at EMBL-EBI and Ensembl — covers most of what you will need (Sayers et al. 2021; Cantelli et al. 2020).

The code in this section is shown but not executed, because this book must build without a network connection and because a textbook that queried NCBI on every build would be a poor citizen. Every output shown below was captured from a real run of exactly the code above it, on 15 October 2026.

Entrez E-utilities

The E-utilities are a family of endpoints that share a query syntax. esearch turns a query into a list of internal unique identifiers (UIDs); esummary returns brief metadata for UIDs; efetch returns full records; and elink walks between databases. Biopython wraps all of them in Bio.Entrez, which also handles the XML parsing.

Queries use the same field-tagged syntax as the NCBI search boxes: term[field], combined with AND, OR and NOT. Common fields are [Organism], [Title], [Accession], [Gene] and the filter pseudo-field refseq[filter]. Building the query precisely is what keeps the result set small enough to be meaningful.

import os
from Bio import Entrez

# NCBI asks every automated client to identify itself; keeping the address in an
# environment variable keeps it out of the manuscript and out of version control.
Entrez.email = os.environ["NCBI_EMAIL"]
Entrez.tool = "practical-bioinformatics-textbook"

query = ('"Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2"[Organism] '
         'AND "complete genome"[Title] AND refseq[filter]')
with Entrez.esearch(db="nuccore", term=query, retmax=5) as handle:
    search = Entrez.read(handle)
print("records matching:", search["Count"])
print("UIDs:", search["IdList"])

with Entrez.esummary(db="nuccore", id=",".join(search["IdList"])) as handle:
    for summary in Entrez.read(handle):
        print(f'{summary["AccessionVersion"]}  {int(summary["Length"]):,} bp  '
              f'updated {summary["UpdateDate"]}')

Rate limits are enforced, and politeness is cheap

NCBI permits three E-utilities requests per second without an API key and ten with one; an API key is free from your NCBI account and is passed as Entrez.api_key. Exceeding the limit returns HTTP 429, and sustained abuse gets your institution’s address blocked, which is a conversation nobody wants to have. Beyond the hard limit there are three courtesy rules: identify yourself with Entrez.email and Entrez.tool; run large jobs outside United States business hours; and post batches of identifiers with the history server (usehistory="y") instead of issuing one request per record. The same principles apply to every public API in this chapter.

Three things in that output are worth taking away. Every one of these calls can fail. Write the retrieval code so that it says so rather than writing a truncated file: check response.status_code or call raise_for_status(), set a timeout, and back off and retry on HTTP 429 and 5xx rather than hammering a service that is already struggling.

3.9 Provenance, or how to still be able to reproduce this next year

Databases are living objects. RefSeq re-annotates, UniProt merges entries, ClinVar reclassifies variants, Ensembl releases a new gene set every quarter. None of that is a problem until you try to reproduce a result and cannot, at which point it is the whole problem. The remedy is to record, for every file an analysis touches, four things: the accession, the version, the date of retrieval and a checksum of the bytes you actually have.

This book practises what it is recommending. Every bundled file is listed in data/MANIFEST.tsv with its size, its SHA-256 digest, its source and a description.

import csv

with open(book_data("MANIFEST.tsv")) as handle:
    manifest = list(csv.DictReader(handle, delimiter="\t"))

print(f"{len(manifest)} files recorded")
#> 21 files recorded
entry = next(row for row in manifest if row["path"].endswith("NC_045512.2.gb"))
for field in ("path", "bytes", "source", "sha256"):
    print(f"{field:>7}: {entry[field]}")
#>    path: sequences/sars-cov-2-NC_045512.2.gb
#>   bytes: 78517
#>  source: NCBI Nucleotide NC_045512.2
#>  sha256: 87744b176aa9ce71350c8becf0d062950dbc485a59766fcfe723ec859f6b6d5c

The digest is what turns provenance from an assertion into a check. Recomputing it takes three lines, and doing so at the start of a pipeline catches the truncated download, the file that was edited in a text editor and saved with different line endings, and the colleague who replaced a file in place without telling you.

import hashlib
from pathlib import Path

def sha256_of(path):
    """Return the SHA-256 digest of a file, read in chunks so that large files fit."""
    digest = hashlib.sha256()
    with open(path, "rb") as handle:
        for block in iter(lambda: handle.read(1 << 20), b""):
            digest.update(block)
    return digest.hexdigest()

checked = 0
for row in manifest:
    path = Path(book_data(*row["path"].split("/")))
    if sha256_of(path) != row["sha256"]:
        print(f"MISMATCH: {row['path']}")
    else:
        checked += 1
print(f"{checked} of {len(manifest)} bundled files match their recorded digest")
#> 21 of 21 bundled files match their recorded digest

A pipeline that begins with that loop fails loudly on the day a file changes, which is far better than succeeding quietly on the wrong data. The same idea scales up: the FAIR principles — findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable — formalise it as community practice (Wilkinson et al. 2016), and the reproducibility literature is unanimous that recording versions is cheaper than reconstructing them (Sandve et al. 2013).

The habit this builds is small. For every external file, write down the resource, the accession with its version, the retrieval date and the digest, in a plain text file beside the data, and record the exact query string or URL alongside them so that the retrieval itself can be repeated. The difference it makes is not small. A methods section that says “sequences were downloaded from NCBI” is not reproducible; one that says “the RefSeq record NC_045512.2 was retrieved from NCBI Nucleotide on 15 October 2026 (SHA-256 87744b17…)” is.

3.10 Summary

  • Resources divide into primary archives, which record submissions; curated knowledgebases, which record conclusions with evidence; and derived resources, which compute. The kind of resource determines the sentence you are entitled to write about the record.
  • INSDC mirroring means GenBank, ENA and DDBJ hold the same records under the same accessions, so a citation to one is a citation to all three.
  • An accession prefix encodes provenance. NM_/NP_ are curated, XM_/XP_ are machine-predicted, GCA_ is a submitted assembly and GCF_ is the RefSeq copy of it with NCBI’s annotation.
  • The digits after the full stop are a version, and what is versioned differs between resources. An unversioned accession does not identify data.
  • A GenBank feature table is read feature by feature, not by its coordinates alone: joined locations, mature-peptide features and gene-to-CDS asymmetries all carry biology that a coordinate pair discards.
  • Swiss-Prot entries are reviewed and TrEMBL entries are not, and the difference shows up directly in the evidence codes attached to their annotations.
  • Evidence codes are the first thing to read in a functional annotation. IDA and IMP are experiments; IEA and ISS are hypotheses.
  • Programmatic access is the normal way to work. Construct a precise query, request only the fields you need, follow pagination links, respect rate limits, identify yourself, and handle failures explicitly.
  • Record accession, version, retrieval date and checksum for every file. A verification loop at the start of a pipeline costs seconds and prevents the class of error that is hardest to detect after the fact.

A colleague sends you a sequence and asks what it is. You paste it into a search
box, and within a minute you have fifty answers. Some come from a genome that a
consortium finished, polished and curated over a decade; some come from a
fragment that a graduate student deposited in 2003 and never looked at again;
and some come from an automatic annotation pipeline that has never been
inspected by a human being. All fifty look identical on the screen. Knowing
which is which is the difference between an analysis that holds up and one that
quietly embarrasses you at review.

That is what this chapter is about. It is not a tour of websites. Websites
change; the structural logic of public biological data does not. There are
archives, which record what somebody submitted; there are knowledgebases, which
record what curators believe after reading the literature; and there are derived
resources, which compute something new from the first two. Each answers a
different question, each fails in a different way, and reading a record without
knowing which kind you are holding is the most common way that careful people
reach wrong conclusions.

You will work with three bundled records throughout. The first is the SARS-CoV-2
reference genome in GenBank format
(`data/sequences/sars-cov-2-NC_045512.2.gb`), whose feature table you will take

## Three kinds of resource {#sec-db-kinds}

A **primary archive** stores submissions. GenBank, the European Nucleotide
Archive (ENA), the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ), the Sequence Read Archive
(SRA) and the Protein Data Bank (PDB) all work this way: a submitter uploads
data and metadata, the archive performs format and consistency checks, assigns
an accession, and from that moment the record is permanent. Archives do not
correct biology. If a submitter annotated a pseudogene as a protein-coding gene,
or recorded the wrong organism, the error stays in the archive until the
submitter themselves revises it. The archive's promise is narrow and valuable:
*this is exactly what was deposited, and it will still be here in twenty years*.

A **curated knowledgebase** stores conclusions. UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot, RefSeq,
ClinVar's reviewed assertions and Reactome contain records that a human curator
has assembled by reading primary literature, reconciling conflicting reports and
attaching evidence to each statement. A curated record is an argument, not an
observation. It can be wrong, but it is wrong in a way that someone is
accountable for, and it usually tells you what the claim rests on.

A **derived resource** computes. STRING scores protein associations from
co-expression, co-occurrence, text mining and imported interactions
[@szklarczyk2023]; the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database holds predicted
structures rather than measured ones [@varadi2022]; gnomAD aggregates allele
frequencies across tens of thousands of exomes and genomes [@karczewski2020].
Derived resources are often the most immediately useful thing on your screen and
the most dangerous to quote without qualification, because the number you are
reading is a model output whose assumptions live in a methods paper you have not
read.

The practical consequence is a habit. Before you use a record, ask which of the
three you are holding, and let the answer set the sentence you are allowed to
write. From an archive you may write "a sequence deposited as *X* has property
*Y*". From a knowledgebase you may write "*X* is annotated as *Y*, on the
evidence of *Z*". From a derived resource you may write "*X* is predicted to be
*Y* by method *M*". Those three sentences are not interchangeable, and reviewers
notice.

::: {.callout-note title="The INSDC and what mirroring means"}
GenBank, ENA and DDBJ form the International Nucleotide Sequence Database
Collaboration (INSDC) [@arita2021]. They exchange new and updated records daily,
so a sequence submitted to any one of them appears in all three within about 24
hours, under the same accession. The record content is the same; the
presentation is not. GenBank shows you a flat file, ENA shows you an
XML-or-flat-file service with a different browser, and DDBJ a third. A paper
citing an ENA accession and a paper citing the same GenBank accession are citing
the same data. Raw reads behave the same way: SRA, ENA and DDBJ's DRA mirror
each other.
:::

## Accessions, versions and what the dot means {#sec-db-accessions}

Readers lose days to accession numbers, usually by assuming that two strings
that look alike mean alike. They do not. The prefix tells you which resource
minted the identifier and, in the case of RefSeq, how the record was produced;
the digits after the full stop are a version, but the thing being versioned
differs between resources.

The RefSeq prefixes are worth memorising because they encode provenance
directly. `NC_` is a complete genomic molecule that has been curated; `NM_` and
`NP_` are a curated messenger RNA and its protein product; `XM_` and `XP_` are
the same two things produced by an automatic annotation pipeline with no human
review [@tatusova2016]. The difference between `NM_000546` and a hypothetical
`XM_` record for the same locus is the difference between a transcript a curator
vouched for and a transcript a program proposed. Genome assemblies carry two
parallel accessions: `GCA_` is the GenBank assembly, exactly as the submitting
group built it, and `GCF_` is the RefSeq copy of that assembly, with NCBI's own
annotation attached. The underlying contigs are usually identical; the gene
models frequently are not.

The following function encodes those rules so that you can check an identifier
instead of guessing at it.

```{python}
#| label: accession-classifier
import re

ACCESSION_PATTERNS = [
    (r"^NC_\d+",  "RefSeq complete genomic molecule, curated"),
    (r"^NM_\d+",  "RefSeq messenger RNA, curated"),
    (r"^NP_\d+",  "RefSeq protein, curated"),
    (r"^XM_\d+",  "RefSeq messenger RNA, predicted by automated annotation"),
    (r"^XP_\d+",  "RefSeq protein, predicted by automated annotation"),
    (r"^GCF_\d+", "RefSeq genome assembly"),
    (r"^GCA_\d+", "GenBank (INSDC) genome assembly"),
    (r"^ENS[A-Z]*G\d{11}", "Ensembl gene"),
    (r"^ENS[A-Z]*T\d{11}", "Ensembl transcript"),
    (r"^rs\d+",   "dbSNP variant"),
    (r"^[OPQ][0-9][A-Z0-9]{3}[0-9]$", "UniProtKB accession"),
    (r"^[A-NR-Z][0-9]([A-Z][A-Z0-9]{2}[0-9]){1,2}$", "UniProtKB accession"),
    (r"^[0-9][A-Za-z0-9]{3}$", "PDB entry"),
    (r"^[A-Z]{1,2}\d{5,6}", "INSDC nucleotide, author-submitted"),
]


def describe_accession(accession):
    """Name the resource an accession belongs to and report its version."""
    stem, _, version = accession.partition(".")
    for pattern, meaning in ACCESSION_PATTERNS:
        if re.match(pattern, stem):
            return f"{meaning}, " + (f"version {version}" if version else "unversioned")
    return "unrecognised identifier"
```

Running it over a set of identifiers drawn from the datasets used in this book
shows how much information a well-formed accession carries before you have
fetched anything at all.

```{python}
#| label: accession-demo
examples = ["NC_045512.2", "NM_000546.6", "NP_000537.3", "XM_011525846.3",
            "GCF_000027325.1", "GCA_000001405.29", "P04637",
            "ENSG00000141510", "1TUP", "rs28934578", "MN908947.3"]

for accession in examples:
    print(f"{accession:<17} {describe_accession(accession)}")
```

The last line repays attention. `MN908947.3` is the author-submitted GenBank
record for the Wuhan-Hu-1 isolate; `NC_045512.2` is the RefSeq record derived
from it. They contain the same 29,903 bases. They are not the same record, they
have separate version histories, and a methods section that cites one when the
analysis used the other is wrong in a way that is tedious to correct later.

::: {.callout-warning title="A version is not an afterthought"}
The suffix after the full stop means different things in different resources,
and all of them matter. In an INSDC or RefSeq nucleotide accession, the version
increments whenever the *sequence* changes — `NC_045512.1` and `NC_045512.2`
are different strings of bases. In a `GCA_`/`GCF_` assembly accession, the
suffix versions the whole assembly, so new scaffolding alone will increment it.
Ensembl versions genes and transcripts separately from the assembly
(`ENST00000269305.9`), and increments the suffix when the model changes. An
unversioned accession such as `NC_045512` silently resolves to whatever is
current on the day you fetch it, which means an unversioned accession in a
methods section does not specify the data you used. Always write the version.
:::

## The anatomy of a nucleotide record {#sec-db-genbank-anatomy}

A GenBank flat file has three parts: a header of record-level metadata, a
feature table of annotations attached to coordinate ranges, and the sequence
itself. Biopython's `SeqIO` parses all three into a `SeqRecord`, from which the
header fields come out as `annotations`.

```{python}
#| label: genbank-header
from Bio import SeqIO

genome = SeqIO.read(book_data("sequences", "sars-cov-2-NC_045512.2.gb"), "genbank")

print(f"Accession:  {genome.name}")
print(f"Version:    {genome.id}")
print(f"Molecule:   {len(genome.seq):,} bp, "
      f"{genome.annotations['molecule_type']}, {genome.annotations['topology']}")
print(f"Updated:    {genome.annotations['date']}")
print(f"Keywords:   {genome.annotations['keywords']}")
print(f"Project:    {genome.dbxrefs}")
print(f"Lineage:    {' > '.join(genome.annotations['taxonomy'][-4:])}")
```

Three of those fields are already doing work. The keyword `RefSeq` confirms this
is the curated copy rather than the submitted one. The `DBLINK` field points at
BioProject PRJNA485481, which is the umbrella record that ties the genome to the
submission it came from — following project identifiers is often the fastest
route from a sequence back to the study that produced it. And the date is the
date of the *record*, not of the sample: this record was last updated in July
2020, which is a fact about curation, not about the virus.

The feature table is where biology lives. Each feature has a type, a location
and a set of qualifiers.

```{python}
#| label: genbank-feature-counts
from collections import Counter

for feature_type, count in Counter(f.type for f in genome.features).most_common():
    print(f"{feature_type:>12}  {count}")
```

Eleven `gene` features but twelve `CDS` features is not a bug, and spotting that
asymmetry is exactly the kind of reading this section is teaching. The extra
coding sequence belongs to ORF1a, which shares a gene feature with ORF1ab
because the two products come from the same transcript: a ribosomal frameshift
partway along produces either the short or the long polyprotein. The feature
table encodes that explicitly as a joined location.

```{python}
#| label: genbank-cds-table
coding_sequences = [f for f in genome.features if f.type == "CDS"]

for feature in coding_sequences[:5]:
    gene = feature.qualifiers["gene"][0]
    segments = " + ".join(f"{int(p.start) + 1}..{int(p.end)}"
                          for p in feature.location.parts)
    protein = feature.qualifiers["protein_id"][0]
    print(f"{gene:<7} {protein:<16} {segments:<26} "
          f"{len(feature.qualifiers['translation'][0]):>5} aa")
```

The first row shows the joined location `266..13468 + 13468..21555`. Those two
intervals overlap at position 13468, which is the hallmark of a programmed −1
ribosomal frameshift rather than a splice junction: the ribosome slips back one
base and continues in a new reading frame, so one base is read twice. A tool
that assumes a `join()` always means splicing will translate this feature
incorrectly. Reading the location, rather than trusting a start and an end
coordinate, is what prevents that.

Features other than `CDS` carry annotation that is easy to overlook. The 26
`mat_peptide` features record the individual non-structural proteins that
proteases cut out of the ORF1ab polyprotein, and they are the only place in the
record where nsp12, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, appears as its own entity.

```{python}
#| label: genbank-mat-peptides
mature_peptides = [f for f in genome.features if f.type == "mat_peptide"]

for feature in mature_peptides[:4]:
    product = feature.qualifiers["product"][0]
    print(f"{product:<24} {int(feature.location.start) + 1:>6}..{int(feature.location.end)}")
print(f"... {len(mature_peptides)} mature peptides in total")
```

## The anatomy of a protein record {#sec-db-uniprot-anatomy}

UniProtKB is one database in two sections [@uniprot2023]. **Swiss-Prot** entries
have been read, assembled and signed off by a curator; **TrEMBL** entries are
translations of coding sequences that have been annotated automatically and
never reviewed. There are roughly six hundred thousand of the former and more
than two hundred million of the latter. Every analysis you run against "all of
UniProt" is therefore dominated by machine annotation unless you filter, which
is what the `reviewed:true` term in a UniProt query does.

What manual curation buys is best seen by opening a curated record and counting.
Biopython parses the flat-file format with `Bio.SwissProt` [@cock2009].

```{python}
#| label: uniprot-header
from Bio import SwissProt

with open(book_data("proteins", "P04637.txt")) as handle:
    p53 = SwissProt.read(handle)

print(f"Entry name:      {p53.entry_name} ({p53.data_class})")
print(f"Primary accession: {p53.accessions[0]}  "
      f"(+{len(p53.accessions) - 1} secondary accessions)")
print(f"Gene:            {p53.gene_name[0]['Name']}")
print(f"Length:          {p53.sequence_length} aa")
print(f"Created:         {p53.created[0]};  annotation updated {p53.annotation_update[0]}")
print(f"Literature:      {len(p53.references)} cited references")
print(f"Cross-references: {len(p53.cross_references)}")
print(f"Sequence features: {len(p53.features)}")
```

The data class reads `Reviewed`, which is how a Swiss-Prot entry identifies
itself; a TrEMBL entry would read `Unreviewed`. The 26 secondary accessions are
the fossil record of entries that were later merged into this one, and they
still resolve, which is why an old accession in an old paper does not break. The
counts at the bottom are the curation dividend: 238 papers read, 1,120 links to
other resources, and over 1,500 annotated features on a protein of just 393
amino acids.

Those features are typed, and the types matter. `CHAIN` marks the mature
polypeptide, `DNA_BIND` a DNA-binding region, `REGION` a functional or
structural segment, `VARIANT` a known sequence variant, `MOD_RES` a modified
residue, and `HELIX`/`STRAND`/`TURN` secondary structure transferred from solved
structures.

```{python}
#| label: uniprot-feature-types
feature_counts = Counter(feature.type for feature in p53.features)
for feature_type, count in feature_counts.most_common(8):
    print(f"{feature_type:>10}  {count}")
```

Most of the 1,523 features are variants, which tells you something about p53
specifically — it is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer, and the
curators have recorded the literature exhaustively. Pulling out the structural
annotation gives the domain architecture that every p53 paper draws as a
cartoon.

```{python}
#| label: uniprot-domains
for feature in p53.features:
    if feature.type in {"DNA_BIND", "MOTIF"} or (
            feature.type == "REGION" and "Interaction" not in feature.qualifiers.get("note", "")):

```

The DNA-binding region at residues 102–292 is the one to remember: it is where
the great majority of cancer-associated missense mutations fall, and it is the

### Evidence codes, and why they are the most useful part of a record {#sec-db-evidence}

Every functional statement in a curated entry carries an evidence tag. Gene
Ontology (GO) annotations [@ashburner2000; @go2023] attached to a UniProt entry
each end in a three-letter code that says how the assignment was made. `IDA`
means inferred from direct assay, `IMP` from mutant phenotype, `IPI` from
physical interaction, `IGI` from genetic interaction and `IEP` from expression
pattern; those are experimental. `ISS` means inferred from sequence similarity,
`TAS` traceable author statement, and `IEA` inferred from electronic annotation
with no curator involvement at all. An `IEA` annotation is a hypothesis; an
`IDA` annotation is somebody's figure.

@fig-db-curation shows the evidence profile of p53's GO annotations alongside
the resources the entry links out to.

which is integration: the entry is simultaneously an index into 311 PDB

for a protein-centred question, whatever the question is.

::: {.callout-tip title="Read the evidence before you read the annotation"}
When a database tells you a protein does something, find the evidence tag before
you quote the claim. In a UniProt web page the evidence appears as a small
coloured icon beside each statement; in the flat file it is the `ECO:` term in
the feature's `evidence` qualifier or the three-letter code in a GO
cross-reference. A function supported only by `IEA` or `ISS` is a lead to
follow, not a result to build on.
:::

## Structures, genomes and the browser model {#sec-db-pdb-ensembl}

The Protein Data Bank is the archive of experimentally determined
three-dimensional structures, now jointly managed as the worldwide PDB
[@berman2000; @burley2023]. A PDB entry is a four-character code such as `1TUP`
and contains atomic coordinates together with the experimental conditions that
produced them. Two attributes of a PDB entry govern how much you should trust
any particular atom: the method (X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic
resonance spectroscopy or cryo-electron microscopy) and, for crystal structures,
the resolution. A 1.5 Å structure resolves individual side-chain positions; a
3.5 Å structure resolves the backbone and little else, and side-chain
orientations in it are largely modelled rather than observed. The structural
bioinformatics chapter takes this up in detail using the bundled `1tup.pdb` and
`1mbn.pdb` files.

Ensembl takes a different approach from an archive [@cunningham2022]. It is
built around a genome assembly as a coordinate system, with every other kind of
data — gene models, transcripts, regulatory features, variants, comparative
alignments — attached to intervals on that assembly. This is the **genome
browser model**, and it has one consequence that beginners trip over constantly:
*every coordinate is meaningful only with respect to an assembly*. Position
7,676,154 on chromosome 17 is a different base in GRCh37 and in GRCh38. Ensembl
labels each release with its assembly, and so must you.

A variant list, a BED file or a set of peak calls is therefore meaningless
without the assembly it was called against, and converting between assemblies
with a liftover tool is lossy: some intervals fail to map and a few map to the
wrong place. Record the assembly name in the file, in the filename and in the
methods section, and if you receive coordinates with no assembly stated, ask
before you use them.

The browser model also explains the Ensembl identifier scheme. `ENSG` prefixes a
gene, `ENST` a transcript, `ENSP` a protein and `ENSE` an exon, each with its own
version suffix because a gene model can be revised without the gene being
redefined. Species other than human carry a species code in the middle:
`ENSMUSG` for mouse.

## Specialised and derived resources {#sec-db-specialised}

Beyond the general archives sit resources organised around one kind of question.
You will not use all of them, but you need to know which one to reach for.

For human variation, **dbSNP** accessions every submitted variant with an `rs`
identifier [@sherry2001], and is a registry rather than an assessment: an `rs`
number says a variant has been observed, not that it matters. **ClinVar**
records clinical interpretations of variants, each with a submitter, a review
status and a date [@landrum2018]; its "review status" stars are the field you
must read, because a one-star assertion from a single laboratory and a four-star
expert-panel assertion are very different objects. The bundled
`data/variants/tp53_clinvar.vcf` holds 3,888 ClinVar records across *TP53* and
is used in the clinical genomics chapter. Interpretation of a clinical variant
follows the ACMG/AMP framework rather than a database lookup [@richards2015].
**OMIM** is the long-running catalogue of Mendelian phenotypes and the genes
behind them [@amberger2019], and remains the best narrative account of what is
known about a disease gene.

For functional genomics data, **GEO** archives processed expression datasets
with their experimental design [@barrett2013], while **SRA** and **ENA** archive
the raw reads underneath. The division matters when you want to reanalyse
something: GEO gives you the authors' processed matrix, and the archives give
you the reads you need in order to disagree with it.

For pathways and interactions, **Reactome** is a curated, peer-reviewed pathway
knowledgebase [@milacic2024]; **KEGG** organises molecular interaction networks
into maps with their own identifier space [@kanehisa2000]; **STRING** scores
functional associations and is explicitly a derived resource [@szklarczyk2023];
and the **Gene Ontology** supplies the controlled vocabulary that most functional
analysis depends on [@ashburner2000]. The bundled
`data/networks/p53_string_network.tsv` and
`data/annotation/human_go_annotations.tsv` are drawn from the last two and are
used in the systems biology chapter.

::: {.callout-important title="Human data carries obligations"}
Aggregate resources such as gnomAD publish allele frequencies precisely because
individual-level human genomes cannot be published freely. Controlled-access
repositories exist for the individual-level data, and access to them is granted
for a stated purpose under a data access agreement that binds you personally.
Downloading human genomic data because a URL happens to work is not consent, and
the question of what you may do with it is legal as well as ethical. The chapter
on ethical, legal and social issues sets out the framework; the obligation
applies from the first file you download.
:::

## Programmatic access {#sec-db-programmatic}

Clicking is fine for one record and indefensible for a thousand. Every major
resource exposes a programmatic interface, and learning two of them — NCBI's
Entrez E-utilities and the REST services at EMBL-EBI and Ensembl — covers most
of what you will need [@sayers2021; @cantelli2022].

The code in this section is shown but not executed, because this book must build
without a network connection and because a textbook that queried NCBI on every
build would be a poor citizen. Every output shown below was captured from a real
run of exactly the code above it, on 15 October 2026.

### Entrez E-utilities {#sec-db-entrez}

The E-utilities are a family of endpoints that share a query syntax. `esearch`
turns a query into a list of internal unique identifiers (UIDs); `esummary`
returns brief metadata for UIDs; `efetch` returns full records; and `elink`
walks between databases. Biopython wraps all of them in `Bio.Entrez`, which also
handles the XML parsing.

Queries use the same field-tagged syntax as the NCBI search boxes:
`term[field]`, combined with `AND`, `OR` and `NOT`. Common fields are
`[Organism]`, `[Title]`, `[Accession]`, `[Gene]` and the filter pseudo-field
`refseq[filter]`. Building the query precisely is what keeps the result set
small enough to be meaningful.

```{python}
#| label: entrez-esearch
#| eval: false
import os
from Bio import Entrez

# NCBI asks every automated client to identify itself; keeping the address in an
# environment variable keeps it out of the manuscript and out of version control.
Entrez.email = os.environ["NCBI_EMAIL"]
Entrez.tool = "practical-bioinformatics-textbook"

query = ('"Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2"[Organism] '
         'AND "complete genome"[Title] AND refseq[filter]')
with Entrez.esearch(db="nuccore", term=query, retmax=5) as handle:
    search = Entrez.read(handle)
print("records matching:", search["Count"])
print("UIDs:", search["IdList"])

with Entrez.esummary(db="nuccore", id=",".join(search["IdList"])) as handle:
    for summary in Entrez.read(handle):
        print(f'{summary["AccessionVersion"]}  {int(summary["Length"]):,} bp  '
              f'updated {summary["UpdateDate"]}')
```

That code, run against NCBI on 15 October 2026, printed:

```

NC_045512.2  29,903 bp  updated 2020/07/18
```

Note that the UID is not the accession. Entrez identifies records internally by
an integer; accessions are metadata on those records. `esearch` returns UIDs,
`efetch` accepts either, and conflating the two is a common source of confusion
when a script that worked yesterday returns nothing today.

Fetching the full record is one more call. The `rettype` and `retmode` pair
chooses the format: `gb`/`text` for a GenBank flat file, `fasta`/`text` for
FASTA, `gb`/`xml` for structured XML.

```{python}

print(f"retrieved {len(flat_file.splitlines())} lines")
print("\n".join(flat_file.splitlines()[:6]))
```

::: {.callout-warning title="Rate limits are enforced, and politeness is cheap"}
NCBI permits three E-utilities requests per second without an API key and ten
with one; an API key is free from your NCBI account and is passed as
`Entrez.api_key`. Exceeding the limit returns HTTP 429, and sustained abuse gets
your institution's address blocked, which is a conversation nobody wants to
have. Beyond the hard limit there are three courtesy rules: identify yourself
with `Entrez.email` and `Entrez.tool`; run large jobs outside United States
business hours; and post batches of identifiers with the history server
(`usehistory="y"`) instead of issuing one request per record. The same
principles apply to every public API in this chapter.
:::

### UniProt and Ensembl REST {#sec-db-rest}

REST services answer with a document whose format you choose through a parameter
or an `Accept` header, and they take ordinary URL query strings. The UniProt
search endpoint accepts the same query language as the website, plus a `fields`
parameter that selects columns and a `format` parameter that chooses between
JSON, TSV, FASTA and the flat-file format.

```{python}
#| label: uniprot-rest-search
#| eval: false
import requests

UNIPROT = "https://rest.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/search"
response = requests.get(UNIPROT, timeout=30, params={
    "query": "(gene:TP53 OR gene:TP63 OR gene:TP73) "
             "AND organism_id:9606 AND reviewed:true",
    "fields": "accession,id,gene_primary,length",
    "format": "tsv", "size": 10})
response.raise_for_status()
print("total matches:", response.headers["x-total-results"])
print(response.text.strip())
```

The captured output was:

```

Entry   Entry Name  Gene Names (primary)    Length
P04637  P53_HUMAN   TP53    393
O15350  P73_HUMAN   TP73    636
Q9H3D4  P63_HUMAN   TP63    680
```

Requesting a column list and a tab-separated format, rather than downloading

Large result sets are paginated, and UniProt paginates by returning a `Link`

```{python}
print("rows in this page:", len(response.text.strip().splitlines()) - 1)
print("next page:", response.links["next"]["url"][:96], "...")
```


That total, 20,431 reviewed human proteins, is the same number as the row count
of the bundled `data/annotation/human_go_annotations.tsv`, which was built from

that the number will drift as curation continues.

Ensembl's REST service is organised around the browser model: endpoints are
named for the operation (`/lookup/symbol`, `/sequence/id`, `/overlap/region`,
`/homology/symbol`) and return JSON by default. It returns its rate-limit state
in response headers, which is the pattern to look for in any API you have not
used before.

```{python}
headers = {"Content-Type": "application/json"}
gene = requests.get(f"{ENSEMBL}/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens/TP53",
                    headers=headers, params={"expand": 1}, timeout=60)
gene.raise_for_status()
record = gene.json()
print(f'{record["id"]}  chromosome {record["seq_region_name"]}:'
      f'{record["start"]}-{record["end"]}  strand {record["strand"]}')
print("assembly:", record["assembly_name"], "| transcripts:", len(record["Transcript"]))
canonical = next(t for t in record["Transcript"] if t["is_canonical"])
print("canonical transcript:", canonical["id"], canonical["biotype"])
print("remaining request allowance:", gene.headers["X-RateLimit-Remaining"],
```

The real run printed:

Three things in that output are worth taking away. The gene is on the minus
strand, so its transcription start site is at the *higher* coordinate — a
frequent source of off-by-a-gene errors when extracting promoters. The assembly
is stated in the response, so there is no excuse for not recording it. And
Ensembl's quota, 55,000 requests per hour, is generous but finite; a script that
loops over 200,000 genes one at a time will exhaust it, which is why the service
also offers POST endpoints that accept a list of identifiers in a single
request.

rather than writing a truncated file: check `response.status_code` or call
`raise_for_status()`, set a `timeout`, and back off and retry on HTTP 429 and
5xx rather than hammering a service that is already struggling.

## Provenance, or how to still be able to reproduce this next year {#sec-db-provenance}

Databases are living objects. RefSeq re-annotates, UniProt merges entries,
ClinVar reclassifies variants, Ensembl releases a new gene set every quarter.
None of that is a problem until you try to reproduce a result and cannot, at
which point it is the whole problem. The remedy is to record, for every file an
analysis touches, four things: the **accession**, the **version**, the **date of
retrieval** and a **checksum** of the bytes you actually have.

This book practises what it is recommending. Every bundled file is listed in
`data/MANIFEST.tsv` with its size, its SHA-256 digest, its source and a
description.

```{python}
#| label: manifest-read
import csv

with open(book_data("MANIFEST.tsv")) as handle:
    manifest = list(csv.DictReader(handle, delimiter="\t"))

print(f"{len(manifest)} files recorded")
entry = next(row for row in manifest if row["path"].endswith("NC_045512.2.gb"))
for field in ("path", "bytes", "source", "sha256"):
    print(f"{field:>7}: {entry[field]}")
```

The digest is what turns provenance from an assertion into a check. Recomputing
it takes three lines, and doing so at the start of a pipeline catches the
truncated download, the file that was edited in a text editor and saved with
different line endings, and the colleague who replaced a file in place without
telling you.

```{python}
#| label: manifest-verify
import hashlib
from pathlib import Path

def sha256_of(path):
    """Return the SHA-256 digest of a file, read in chunks so that large files fit."""
    digest = hashlib.sha256()
    with open(path, "rb") as handle:
        for block in iter(lambda: handle.read(1 << 20), b""):
            digest.update(block)
    return digest.hexdigest()

checked = 0
for row in manifest:
    path = Path(book_data(*row["path"].split("/")))
    if sha256_of(path) != row["sha256"]:
        print(f"MISMATCH: {row['path']}")
    else:
        checked += 1
print(f"{checked} of {len(manifest)} bundled files match their recorded digest")
```

A pipeline that begins with that loop fails loudly on the day a file changes,
which is far better than succeeding quietly on the wrong data. The same idea
scales up: the FAIR principles — findable, accessible, interoperable,
reusable — formalise it as community practice [@wilkinson2016], and the
reproducibility literature is unanimous that recording versions is cheaper than
reconstructing them [@sandve2013].

The habit this builds is small. For every external file, write down the
resource, the accession with its version, the retrieval date and the digest, in
a plain text file beside the data, and record the exact query string or URL
alongside them so that the retrieval itself can be repeated. The difference it
makes is not small. A methods section that says "sequences were downloaded from
NCBI" is not reproducible; one that says "the RefSeq record NC_045512.2 was
retrieved from NCBI Nucleotide on 15 October 2026 (SHA-256 87744b17…)" is.

## Summary {#sec-03-summary}

- Resources divide into primary archives, which record submissions; curated
  knowledgebases, which record conclusions with evidence; and derived resources,
  which compute. The kind of resource determines the sentence you are entitled
  to write about the record.
- INSDC mirroring means GenBank, ENA and DDBJ hold the same records under the
  same accessions, so a citation to one is a citation to all three.
- An accession prefix encodes provenance. `NM_`/`NP_` are curated, `XM_`/`XP_`
  are machine-predicted, `GCA_` is a submitted assembly and `GCF_` is the RefSeq
  copy of it with NCBI's annotation.
- The digits after the full stop are a version, and what is versioned differs
  between resources. An unversioned accession does not identify data.
- A GenBank feature table is read feature by feature, not by its coordinates
  alone: joined locations, mature-peptide features and gene-to-CDS asymmetries
  all carry biology that a coordinate pair discards.
- Swiss-Prot entries are reviewed and TrEMBL entries are not, and the difference
  shows up directly in the evidence codes attached to their annotations.
- Evidence codes are the first thing to read in a functional annotation. `IDA`
  and `IMP` are experiments; `IEA` and `ISS` are hypotheses.
- Programmatic access is the normal way to work. Construct a precise query,
  request only the fields you need, follow pagination links, respect rate
  limits, identify yourself, and handle failures explicitly.
- Record accession, version, retrieval date and checksum for every file. A
  verification loop at the start of a pipeline costs seconds and prevents the
  class of error that is hardest to detect after the fact.

```{python}
cds_feature = next(f for f in transcript.features if f.type == "CDS")
translated = cds_feature.extract(transcript.seq).translate(to_stop=True)
stored = cds_feature.qualifiers["translation"][0]
print(f"translated {len(translated)} aa; stored {len(stored)} aa; "
      f"identical: {str(translated) == stored}")
```

**5.** Phosphorylation dominates, with acetylation second; both are reversible
marks placed by signalling enzymes, which is consistent with p53 being
controlled largely after translation rather than at the level of transcription.

**6.** Two reasons it misleads: first, the fraction depends on how much
attention a protein has received rather than on how well it is understood, so a
heavily studied protein and an obscure one are not comparable; second,
electronic annotations are added in bulk to every entry by pipelines, so an
entry with few experimental annotations may simply have a large `IEA`
denominator. It is informative within a single protein over time, or when
comparing the same protein's entry between Swiss-Prot and TrEMBL, because then
the denominator is controlled.

**9.** With only `IEA` evidence: "*X* is predicted to have protein kinase
activity by electronic annotation (GO:0004672, IEA)." With `IDA` evidence: "*X*
has protein kinase activity, demonstrated by direct assay (GO:0004672, IDA)."
Moving between them requires an experiment in which the purified protein
transfers phosphate to a substrate — typically an in vitro kinase assay with
a radiolabelled or phospho-specific readout, with a catalytically dead mutant as
the control.

## Further reading {#sec-03-further-reading}

- @sayers2021 is the annual overview of the NCBI resources and is the quickest
  way to find out which database holds the kind of record you need.
- @cantelli2022 does the same job for the EMBL-EBI services, including ENA,
  UniProt and the proteomics and expression archives.
- @uniprot2023 explains how Swiss-Prot curation is actually carried out, which
  is the background you need in order to judge how much an annotation is worth.
- @cunningham2022 describes the Ensembl data model and release cycle; read it
  before you compare results computed against two different Ensembl releases.
- @ashburner2000 is the paper that introduced the Gene Ontology, and the
  clearest statement of why a controlled vocabulary was necessary.
- @landrum2018 sets out ClinVar's submission and review model, including what
  the review-status stars mean in practice.
- @wilkinson2016 states the FAIR principles, which are the standard that funders
  and journals increasingly expect data management plans to meet.
- @cock2009 is the Biopython reference; `Bio.SeqIO`, `Bio.SwissProt` and
  `Bio.Entrez`, all used in this chapter, are documented in its tutorial.
