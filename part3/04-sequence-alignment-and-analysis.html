4  Sequence alignment and what it tells you

After working through this chapter you will be able to

  • distinguish homology from similarity, and orthologues from paralogues, and state which of the three a sequence comparison can actually establish
  • derive and implement the Needleman–Wunsch recursion, and read a score matrix and its traceback
  • choose between global and local alignment, and between linear and affine gap penalties, for a stated biological question
  • explain how PAM and BLOSUM matrices are constructed and demonstrate that the choice of matrix changes the alignment you obtain
  • run a BLAST search against a database you built yourself and interpret bit scores and E-values correctly
  • build a multiple sequence alignment with MAFFT, quantify conservation in it, and infer and criticise a distance-based tree

Almost everything in bioinformatics reduces to a comparison. Is this gene the one that causes the disease? Is this virus related to the one from last month? Does this protein have the active site that would make it a drug target? None of those questions can be answered by looking at a sequence on its own. They are answered by lining one sequence up against another and asking how the differences are distributed — which is to say, by alignment.

Alignment is therefore the algorithmic heart of the subject, and it is worth understanding rather than invoking. A great many published errors come from treating an aligner as an oracle: accepting a percentage identity without knowing how many columns it was computed over, reporting a BLAST hit without reading its E-value, or inferring that two proteins share a function because a multiple alignment coloured some columns the same. Each of those mistakes is prevented by knowing what the algorithm actually optimises.

This chapter builds the machinery from the bottom. You will implement Needleman–Wunsch yourself in about twenty lines of Python, print the score matrix, and follow the traceback that recovers the alignment; you will then see where Smith–Waterman differs by one line and why that line changes the question being asked. From there you will work with real sequences: the four bundled globins (data/proteins/globins.fasta — human haemoglobin alpha and beta, human myoglobin and sperm whale myoglobin) for pairwise work, and the eight bundled p53 orthologues (data/proteins/p53-orthologs.fasta, spanning human to medaka) for multiple alignment and tree building. You will build a BLAST database from those sequences and search it, and you will finish with a neighbour-joining tree carrying bootstrap support values that you computed, not assumed.

4.2 Homology, similarity, and the words that get them confused

Similarity is a measurement. Two sequences are 38% identical over 120 aligned columns; that is an observation you can repeat. Homology is a historical claim: it asserts that two sequences descend from a common ancestral sequence. Homology is not a quantity. Sequences are homologous or they are not, and no sequence is “40% homologous” to another, however often the phrase appears in print. What similarity gives you is evidence for homology, and the strength of that evidence depends on how much similarity you observed and how long the region was.

Homologous sequences come in two kinds that are constantly conflated. Orthologues are homologues separated by a speciation event: human TP53 and mouse Trp53 are orthologues, because the gene was present in the last common ancestor and the lineages diverged. Paralogues are homologues separated by a gene duplication within a lineage: human haemoglobin alpha and human haemoglobin beta are paralogues, products of an ancient duplication of an ancestral globin. The distinction is not pedantry. Orthologues usually retain the ancestral function, which is why annotation is routinely transferred between them; paralogues are free to diverge in function, which is why transferring annotation between them is how a gene acquires a wrong name that then propagates through every database that copies it.

The reason similarity cannot simply be read as homology is that short stretches of high identity arise by chance. The following experiment makes the point with the bundled globins. Human haemoglobin alpha and human myoglobin are genuine distant homologues; we compare their local alignment with local alignments of haemoglobin alpha against 50 shuffled versions of myoglobin, which have exactly the same amino acid composition but no shared history.

import random
import statistics
from Bio import SeqIO
from Bio.Align import PairwiseAligner, substitution_matrices

globins = {record.id.split("|")[2]: record
           for record in SeqIO.parse(book_data("proteins", "globins.fasta"), "fasta")}
blosum62 = substitution_matrices.load("BLOSUM62")


def percent_identity(alignment):
    """Percentage identity over aligned columns only, ignoring gap columns."""
    pairs = [(x, y) for x, y in zip(alignment[0], alignment[1])
             if x != "-" and y != "-"]
    return 100 * sum(x == y for x, y in pairs) / len(pairs)


aligner = PairwiseAligner(mode="local", substitution_matrix=blosum62,
                          open_gap_score=-11, extend_gap_score=-1)
genuine = aligner.align(globins["HBA_HUMAN"].seq, globins["MYG_HUMAN"].seq)[0]
print(f"HBA vs myoglobin: score {genuine.score:.0f}, "
      f"{percent_identity(genuine):.1f}% identity over {len(genuine[0])} columns")
#> HBA vs myoglobin: score 124, 27.5% identity over 148 columns
random.seed(7)
null_scores, null_identities, null_lengths = [], [], []
for _ in range(50):
    shuffled = "".join(random.sample(str(globins["MYG_HUMAN"].seq),
                                     len(globins["MYG_HUMAN"].seq)))
    decoy = aligner.align(globins["HBA_HUMAN"].seq, shuffled)[0]
    null_scores.append(decoy.score)
    null_identities.append(percent_identity(decoy))
    null_lengths.append(len(decoy[0]))

print(f"shuffled decoys: mean score {statistics.mean(null_scores):.0f} "
      f"(best {max(null_scores):.0f})")
#> shuffled decoys: mean score 30 (best 42)
print(f"                 mean identity {statistics.mean(null_identities):.1f}% "
      f"(best {max(null_identities):.1f}%)")
#>                  mean identity 41.8% (best 87.5%)
print(f"                 mean alignment length {statistics.mean(null_lengths):.0f} columns")
#>                  mean alignment length 25 columns

The genuine comparison scores 124; the best of 50 decoys scores 42. The score separates signal from noise cleanly. Percentage identity does not: the decoys average around 42% identity, and the best of them reaches 87.5%, because a local aligner on random sequence finds a short stretch that happens to match and stops there. Those high identities are computed over a handful of columns.

A percentage identity without a length is not a result

Always report identity together with the number of aligned columns, and treat any identity computed over fewer than about 50 residues as uninformative. The region between roughly 20% and 35% identity over a protein-length alignment is known as the twilight zone: real homologues live there, and so do convincing accidents. Deciding between them needs a statistical model, which is what the E-value in Section 4.7 provides, or a profile method that uses information from a whole family.

4.3 Global alignment by dynamic programming

An alignment is a choice of which residues to place opposite which, with gaps allowed. For two sequences of length 150 the number of possible alignments is astronomically large, so an exhaustive search is impossible — and yet the optimal alignment can be found in time proportional to the product of the lengths. The trick, published by Needleman and Wunsch, is the observation that the best alignment of two sequences ends in exactly one of three ways (Needleman and Wunsch 1970).

Write \(S(i, j)\) for the score of the best alignment of the first \(i\) residues of sequence \(a\) against the first \(j\) residues of sequence \(b\). Whatever that best alignment is, its last column must be one of: \(a_i\) opposite \(b_j\); \(a_i\) opposite a gap; or a gap opposite \(b_j\). Each possibility reduces the problem to a smaller one whose answer is already known, which gives the recursion

\[ S(i, j) = \max \begin{cases} S(i-1, j-1) + s(a_i, b_j) & \text{(align $a_i$ with $b_j$)} \\ S(i-1, j) + g & \text{(gap in $b$)} \\ S(i, j-1) + g & \text{(gap in $a$)} \end{cases} \]

where \(s(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the substitution score for a pair of residues and \(g\) is the gap penalty. The first row and column are initialised with cumulative gap penalties, because aligning a prefix against nothing costs one gap per residue. Filling the table is then a double loop.

import numpy as np

def fill_matrix(seq_a, seq_b, matrix, gap):
    """Fill the Needleman-Wunsch score matrix for a linear gap penalty."""
    n, m = len(seq_a), len(seq_b)
    score = np.zeros((n + 1, m + 1))
    score[:, 0] = np.arange(n + 1) * gap          # prefix of a against nothing
    score[0, :] = np.arange(m + 1) * gap          # prefix of b against nothing
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(1, m + 1):
            score[i, j] = max(score[i - 1, j - 1] + matrix[seq_a[i - 1], seq_b[j - 1]],
                              score[i - 1, j] + gap,
                              score[i, j - 1] + gap)
    return score

The matrix holds scores, not the alignment. Recovering the alignment means starting at the bottom-right corner and walking back, at each step asking which of the three predecessors the cell’s value actually came from. A diagonal step emits an aligned pair, a vertical step emits a gap in the second sequence, and a horizontal step a gap in the first.

def traceback(score, seq_a, seq_b, matrix, gap):
    """Walk back from the corner, recovering the alignment and the path taken."""
    i, j = len(seq_a), len(seq_b)
    top, bottom, path = "", "", [(i, j)]
    while i > 0 or j > 0:
        if i and j and score[i, j] == score[i - 1, j - 1] + matrix[seq_a[i - 1], seq_b[j - 1]]:
            top, bottom, i, j = seq_a[i - 1] + top, seq_b[j - 1] + bottom, i - 1, j - 1
        elif i and score[i, j] == score[i - 1, j] + gap:
            top, bottom, i = seq_a[i - 1] + top, "-" + bottom, i - 1
        else:
            top, bottom, j = "-" + top, seq_b[j - 1] + bottom, j - 1
        path.append((i, j))
    return top, bottom, path[::-1]

Twenty lines of code, and the optimality is guaranteed: no other alignment of these two sequences under this scoring scheme scores higher. That guarantee is what distinguishes dynamic programming from the heuristics later in the chapter.

We run it on two short peptides, scoring with BLOSUM50 and a gap penalty of −8, which is the parameter set used in the classical worked example of this algorithm.

4.4 Local alignment, and when each is correct

Needleman–Wunsch aligns the sequences end to end, which is the right question when the two sequences are expected to correspond along their whole length — two orthologous proteins of similar size, two assemblies of the same genome, a read against the reference window it came from. It is the wrong question when one sequence is a fragment of the other, when the two share only a domain, or when you are searching a database and most of the target has nothing to do with the query.

Smith and Waterman modified the recursion by one term (Smith and Waterman 1981). Adding zero as a fourth option,

\[ S(i, j) = \max\{0,\; S(i-1,j-1) + s(a_i,b_j),\; S(i-1,j) + g,\; S(i,j-1) + g\}, \]

means that a region whose alignment has become unprofitable is abandoned rather than carried forward, and the first row and column are initialised to zero rather than to cumulative penalties. The alignment is then read from the highest cell anywhere in the matrix back to the first zero. Those two changes convert the algorithm from “align everything” to “find the best-scoring segment pair”, which is the operation every database search tool approximates.

def smith_waterman(seq_a, seq_b, matrix, gap):
    """Local alignment: the same recursion, floored at zero."""
    n, m = len(seq_a), len(seq_b)
    score = np.zeros((n + 1, m + 1))
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(1, m + 1):
            score[i, j] = max(0,
                              score[i - 1, j - 1] + matrix[seq_a[i - 1], seq_b[j - 1]],
                              score[i - 1, j] + gap,
                              score[i, j - 1] + gap)
    best = np.unravel_index(np.argmax(score), score.shape)
    return score, best


local_score, best_cell = smith_waterman(seq_a, seq_b, blosum50, GAP)
print(f"best local score {local_score[best_cell]:.0f} at cell {best_cell} "
      f"(global score was {score[-1, -1]:.0f})")
#> best local score 28 at cell (np.int64(9), np.int64(5)) (global score was 1)
print(f"negative cells in the global matrix: {int((score < 0).sum())} of {score.size}")
#> negative cells in the global matrix: 84 of 88
print(f"negative cells in the local matrix:  {int((local_score < 0).sum())}")
#> negative cells in the local matrix:  0

The local score of 28 is much higher than the global score of 1, because the local algorithm is allowed to report only the AWGHE/AW-HE region and discard the rest. That is not a better alignment of the same thing; it is an alignment of a different thing. Choosing between them is a biological decision: use global alignment when you believe the sequences correspond throughout, and local alignment when you believe only part of them does.

A quick rule for choosing the mode

Ask what you would do if the two sequences shared only 30 residues out of 300. If the right answer is “report that 30-residue match”, you want local alignment. If the right answer is “report that these sequences do not correspond”, you want global. Biopython’s PairwiseAligner switches between them with mode="global" and mode="local", and most errors come from leaving the mode at whatever it was for the previous analysis.

4.5 Gap models

The implementations above charge a fixed penalty \(g\) for every gap position, a linear gap penalty. Biologically that is wrong. Insertions and deletions occur as events, and a single event can remove six residues as easily as one. A linear penalty charges six times as much for the six-residue deletion as for the one-residue deletion, and so prefers to explain the data with many short gaps scattered through the alignment rather than one long gap in the loop where the indel actually happened.

An affine gap penalty charges a large cost \(d\) to open a gap and a small cost \(e\) to extend it, so that a gap of length \(k\) costs \(d + (k-1)e\) (Gotoh 1982). The classical BLAST protein defaults, \(d = 11\) and \(e = 1\), make a six-residue gap cost 16 rather than 66. The effect on real sequences is easy to see.

import re

def gap_summary(alignment):
    """Count gap runs and the longest run in a pairwise alignment."""
    runs = re.findall(r"-+", str(alignment[0])) + re.findall(r"-+", str(alignment[1]))
    return len(runs), max((len(run) for run in runs), default=0)


for name, (open_score, extend_score) in {"linear (-4 each)": (-4, -4),
                                         "affine (-11, -1)": (-11, -1)}.items():
    model = PairwiseAligner(mode="global", substitution_matrix=blosum62,
                            open_gap_score=open_score, extend_gap_score=extend_score)
    alignment = model.align(globins["HBA_HUMAN"].seq, globins["MYG_HUMAN"].seq)[0]
    n_gaps, longest = gap_summary(alignment)
    print(f"{name:<18} score {alignment.score:6.0f}  columns {len(alignment[0]):4d}  "
          f"gap runs {n_gaps:2d}  longest {longest}  identity {percent_identity(alignment):.1f}%")
#> linear (-4 each)   score    126  columns  158  gap runs 15  longest 3  identity 31.9%
#> affine (-11, -1)   score    108  columns  154  gap runs  2  longest 6  identity 27.5%

With a linear penalty the aligner opens 15 separate gaps, none longer than three residues; with an affine penalty it opens two, one of them six residues long. The affine alignment is the one that corresponds to a plausible evolutionary history: haemoglobin and myoglobin differ by a small number of indel events in surface loops, not by fifteen. This is why every production aligner — BLAST, MAFFT, BWA, minimap2 (Li 2018) — uses affine or piecewise-affine gap costs, and why the two numbers that control it are worth setting deliberately rather than leaving at a default you have not read.

4.6 Substitution matrices

Everything above assumed a function \(s(a_i, b_j)\) that scores a pair of residues. For DNA a simple match/mismatch score is often adequate. For protein it is not, because amino acid substitutions are not equally likely: leucine-for-isoleucine is conservative and common, tryptophan-for-aspartate is neither. A substitution matrix encodes those asymmetries as log-odds scores. For a pair of residues \(a\) and \(b\),

\[ s(a, b) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \log \frac{q_{ab}}{p_a p_b}, \]

where \(q_{ab}\) is the frequency with which \(a\) and \(b\) are seen aligned in trusted alignments of related sequences, \(p_a\) and \(p_b\) are their background frequencies, and \(\lambda\) is a scale factor that lets the result be rounded to integers. A positive score means the pair occurs in homologues more often than chance would produce; a negative score means less often. The scores are therefore statements about evolution, not about chemistry, although the two largely agree.

The two families differ in how \(q_{ab}\) was estimated. PAM matrices were built by counting substitutions in alignments of very closely related proteins, fitting a Markov model of accepted point mutations, and extrapolating that model to greater evolutionary distances (Dayhoff, Schwartz, and Orcutt 1978). PAM1 corresponds to one accepted point mutation per hundred residues; PAM250 is that model multiplied by itself 250 times, and is intended for distant comparisons. BLOSUM matrices were built directly from observed ungapped blocks of aligned protein families, with the blocks clustered at a chosen identity threshold to avoid over-weighting close relatives (Henikoff and Henikoff 1992). BLOSUM62 was derived from blocks clustered at 62% identity, BLOSUM80 at 80%. The crucial and frequently misremembered consequence is that the numbering runs in opposite directions: a high BLOSUM number suits close sequences, whereas a high PAM number suits distant ones.

4.7 Heuristic database search with BLAST

Smith–Waterman is optimal and far too slow for a database search: comparing one 300-residue query against a database of \(10^8\) residues means filling \(3 \times 10^{10}\) cells. The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool trades that guarantee for speed (S. F. Altschul et al. 1990). Its reasoning is that any alignment worth reporting almost certainly contains a short exact or near-exact match, so instead of examining every cell it looks only at the neighbourhoods of such seeds.

The procedure has three stages. Seeding: the query is broken into overlapping words — three residues for protein, eleven bases for nucleotide by default — and for proteins a neighbourhood of similar words scoring above a threshold is generated for each. Extension: each database position matching a seed word is extended outwards, and extension stops when the running score falls too far below the best value seen, which is what makes the search sublinear in practice. Evaluation: surviving high-scoring segment pairs are assembled, gapped alignments are computed around them, and the results are scored statistically (S. Altschul 1997). The original FASTA algorithm uses the same seed-and-extend idea with a different seeding rule and remains a useful cross-check (Pearson and Lipman 1988).

The flavours differ only in what is translated before the comparison: blastn compares nucleotide against nucleotide, blastp protein against protein, blastx a translated nucleotide query against proteins, tblastn a protein query against a translated nucleotide database, and tblastx both translated. The decision follows from what you have and what you are looking for; when in doubt, comparing at the protein level detects homology at considerably greater evolutionary distance than comparing at the nucleotide level, because synonymous changes are invisible to it.

The statistics, and why the database size is in them

Three numbers describe each hit. The raw score is the sum of substitution and gap scores for the alignment, and it is uninterpretable on its own because it depends on the matrix and gap costs used. The bit score normalises the raw score by those parameters, \(S' = (\lambda S - \ln K)/\ln 2\), so that bit scores computed with different scoring schemes are comparable and a bit score of 40 means the same thing in any search. The E-value is the number of hits with at least this score that you would expect to see by chance in a search of this size:

\[ E = \frac{mn}{2^{S'}}, \]

where \(m\) is the query length and \(n\) the total number of residues in the database. The bit score is a property of the alignment; the E-value is a property of the alignment and the database you searched. Doubling the database doubles the E-value of every hit without changing a single alignment. BLAST+ lets you set the effective database size directly, which turns that statement into a demonstration.

Record the database and its version or date alongside every result, because the E-values are not reproducible without it. Check the self-hit before reading anything else. And look at the alignment, not just the table: a high-scoring hit confined to a low-complexity run of glutamines or a coiled-coil region is a compositional artefact, not evidence of shared ancestry, which is why BLAST filters such regions by default and why turning that filter off requires a reason.

4.8 Multiple sequence alignment

A pairwise alignment compares two sequences; a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) arranges many sequences into a single set of columns, so that each column is a hypothesis that the residues in it descend from one ancestral position. That is a much stronger claim than any pairwise comparison makes, and everything downstream — conservation analysis, profile construction, tree building, structure-guided mutagenesis — depends on it being roughly right.

Extending dynamic programming to \(N\) sequences is possible and useless: the table has \(N\) dimensions and the cost grows exponentially. Progressive alignment, introduced in the form that ClustalW made standard (Thompson, Higgins, and Gibson 1994), sidesteps this. It computes all pairwise distances, builds a quick guide tree from them, and then aligns sequences in the order that tree suggests, most similar first, merging alignments into larger alignments as it climbs. Each merge is a pairwise problem, so each is tractable.

The weakness of the approach is stated in its own name. Gaps placed early are never revisited: once a gap, always a gap. An error made while aligning two close sequences propagates into every later merge, and the order in which sequences are supplied can change the result. Modern tools mitigate this with iterative refinement, which realigns subsets of the alignment and keeps changes that improve an objective function. MAFFT does this, and additionally uses a fast Fourier transform to find homologous segments quickly, which is what makes it practical on thousands of sequences (Katoh and Standley 2013). MUSCLE (Edgar 2004) and Clustal Omega (Sievers et al. 2011) are the other two tools you will meet most often; Clustal Omega scales to the largest datasets, and MUSCLE’s refinement is thorough on moderate ones.

4.9 Distance-based phylogenetic inference

A multiple alignment implies a history, and a tree is an explicit hypothesis about it. The cheapest route from one to the other has two steps: convert the alignment into a matrix of pairwise distances, then build a tree whose branch lengths reproduce those distances as closely as possible.

Biopython’s DistanceCalculator computes the matrix. The blosum62 model scores each pair of aligned sequences with the substitution matrix and converts the normalised score into a distance; it is a similarity-derived measure, not an estimate of substitutions per site, and the numbers should be read as relative rather than absolute.

The tree is partly convincing and partly not, and saying which is which is the whole point of putting numbers on the branches. The two fish group together in every replicate; so do mouse and rat; so do human and tree shrew, which is consistent with tree shrews being close relatives of primates. The branching order among cattle, cat and the primate pair is supported in 18% and 34% of replicates, which is to say the alignment does not resolve it. Reporting that tree without the numbers would present a resolution the data does not contain.

Three limitations of this approach deserve stating plainly. First, reducing an alignment to a distance matrix throws away which columns disagree, and the lost information is exactly what distinguishes competing topologies; character-based methods — maximum likelihood as implemented in RAxML (Stamatakis 2014) or IQ-TREE (Minh et al. 2019), or Bayesian inference — use it and are the standard for published phylogenies. Second, a similarity-derived distance is not corrected for multiple substitutions at the same site, so long branches are systematically underestimated; MEGA and comparable packages offer corrected models (Tamura, Stecher, and Kumar 2021). Third, bootstrap support measures how consistently the data support a branch under this method, not whether the method is right: a systematically biased analysis can produce 100% support for the wrong tree, and frequently has. Use distance methods for a quick look at a family, as here, and a likelihood method for anything you intend to publish.

4.10 Summary

  • Similarity is measured; homology is inferred. Orthologues arise by speciation and usually keep their function; paralogues arise by duplication and need not, which is why annotation transferred between paralogues is a common source of error.
  • Dynamic programming finds the optimal alignment under a scoring scheme by building a table of prefix alignments and tracing back. The recursion is three lines long and the guarantee it provides is exact.
  • Global alignment assumes the sequences correspond throughout; local alignment finds the best-scoring segment pair. Choosing between them is a biological decision, not a default.
  • Affine gap penalties charge once to open a gap and little to extend it, which reproduces the fact that indels are events. A linear penalty scatters many short gaps where one long gap belongs.
  • Substitution matrices are log-odds tables. High BLOSUM numbers suit close sequences and high PAM numbers suit distant ones, and using a matrix built for the wrong distance silently shortens and re-gaps the alignment rather than failing visibly.
  • A percentage identity without an alignment length is uninterpretable, because short alignments of unrelated sequences routinely exceed 80% identity.
  • BLAST trades the optimality guarantee for speed by seeding on short words and extending them. Bit scores are comparable across searches; E-values are not, because they scale with the size of the database searched.
  • Progressive multiple alignment is fast and order-dependent: gaps placed early are never revisited, so inspect the alignment before building anything on it.
  • Conservation in a multiple alignment localises functional constraint — for p53, onto the DNA-binding domain where cancer mutations cluster — but low conservation does not imply unimportance.
  • Distance-based trees are quick and lossy. Report bootstrap support, and read it as a measure of consistency within the method rather than of correctness.

## Homology, similarity, and the words that get them confused {#sec-aln-homology}

**Similarity** is a measurement. Two sequences are 38% identical over 120
aligned columns; that is an observation you can repeat. **Homology** is a
historical claim: it asserts that two sequences descend from a common ancestral
sequence. Homology is not a quantity. Sequences are homologous or they are not,
and no sequence is "40% homologous" to another, however often the phrase appears
in print. What similarity gives you is evidence for homology, and the strength
of that evidence depends on how much similarity you observed and how long the
region was.

Homologous sequences come in two kinds that are constantly conflated.
**Orthologues** are homologues separated by a speciation event: human *TP53* and
mouse *Trp53* are orthologues, because the gene was present in the last common
ancestor and the lineages diverged. **Paralogues** are homologues separated by a
gene duplication within a lineage: human haemoglobin alpha and human haemoglobin
beta are paralogues, products of an ancient duplication of an ancestral globin.
The distinction is not pedantry. Orthologues usually retain the ancestral
function, which is why annotation is routinely transferred between them;
paralogues are free to diverge in function, which is why transferring annotation
between them is how a gene acquires a wrong name that then propagates through
every database that copies it.

The reason similarity cannot simply be read as homology is that short stretches
of high identity arise by chance. The following experiment makes the point with
the bundled globins. Human haemoglobin alpha and human myoglobin are genuine
distant homologues; we compare their local alignment with local alignments of
haemoglobin alpha against 50 shuffled versions of myoglobin, which have exactly
the same amino acid composition but no shared history.

## Global alignment by dynamic programming {#sec-aln-nw}

An alignment is a choice of which residues to place opposite which, with gaps
allowed. For two sequences of length 150 the number of possible alignments is
astronomically large, so an exhaustive search is impossible — and yet the
optimal alignment can be found in time proportional to the product of the
lengths. The trick, published by Needleman and Wunsch, is the observation that
the best alignment of two sequences ends in exactly one of three ways
[@needleman1970].

Write $S(i, j)$ for the score of the best alignment of the first $i$ residues of
sequence $a$ against the first $j$ residues of sequence $b$. Whatever that best
alignment is, its last column must be one of: $a_i$ opposite $b_j$; $a_i$
opposite a gap; or a gap opposite $b_j$. Each possibility reduces the problem to
a smaller one whose answer is already known, which gives the recursion

$$
S(i, j) = \max \begin{cases}
S(i-1, j-1) + s(a_i, b_j) & \text{(align $a_i$ with $b_j$)} \\
S(i-1, j) + g & \text{(gap in $b$)} \\
S(i, j-1) + g & \text{(gap in $a$)}
\end{cases}
$$

where $s(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the substitution score for a pair of residues and $g$
is the gap penalty. The first row and column are initialised with cumulative gap
penalties, because aligning a prefix against nothing costs one gap per residue.
Filling the table is then a double loop.

```{python}
#| label: nw-fill
import numpy as np

def fill_matrix(seq_a, seq_b, matrix, gap):
    """Fill the Needleman-Wunsch score matrix for a linear gap penalty."""
    n, m = len(seq_a), len(seq_b)
    score = np.zeros((n + 1, m + 1))
    score[:, 0] = np.arange(n + 1) * gap          # prefix of a against nothing
    score[0, :] = np.arange(m + 1) * gap          # prefix of b against nothing
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(1, m + 1):
            score[i, j] = max(score[i - 1, j - 1] + matrix[seq_a[i - 1], seq_b[j - 1]],
                              score[i - 1, j] + gap,
                              score[i, j - 1] + gap)
    return score
```

The matrix holds scores, not the alignment. Recovering the alignment means
starting at the bottom-right corner and walking back, at each step asking which
of the three predecessors the cell's value actually came from. A diagonal step
emits an aligned pair, a vertical step emits a gap in the second sequence, and a
horizontal step a gap in the first.

```{python}
#| label: nw-traceback
def traceback(score, seq_a, seq_b, matrix, gap):
    """Walk back from the corner, recovering the alignment and the path taken."""
    i, j = len(seq_a), len(seq_b)
    top, bottom, path = "", "", [(i, j)]
    while i > 0 or j > 0:
        if i and j and score[i, j] == score[i - 1, j - 1] + matrix[seq_a[i - 1], seq_b[j - 1]]:
            top, bottom, i, j = seq_a[i - 1] + top, seq_b[j - 1] + bottom, i - 1, j - 1
        elif i and score[i, j] == score[i - 1, j] + gap:
            top, bottom, i = seq_a[i - 1] + top, "-" + bottom, i - 1
        else:
            top, bottom, j = "-" + top, seq_b[j - 1] + bottom, j - 1
        path.append((i, j))
    return top, bottom, path[::-1]
```

Twenty lines of code, and the optimality is guaranteed: no other alignment of
these two sequences under this scoring scheme scores higher. That guarantee is
what distinguishes dynamic programming from the heuristics later in the chapter.

We run it on two short peptides, scoring with BLOSUM50 and a gap penalty of −8,
which is the parameter set used in the classical worked example of this
algorithm.

## Local alignment, and when each is correct {#sec-aln-sw}

Needleman–Wunsch aligns the sequences end to end, which is the right question
when the two sequences are expected to correspond along their whole length — two
orthologous proteins of similar size, two assemblies of the same genome, a read
against the reference window it came from. It is the wrong question when one
sequence is a fragment of the other, when the two share only a domain, or when
you are searching a database and most of the target has nothing to do with the
query.

Smith and Waterman modified the recursion by one term [@smith1981]. Adding zero
as a fourth option,

$$
S(i, j) = \max\{0,\; S(i-1,j-1) + s(a_i,b_j),\; S(i-1,j) + g,\; S(i,j-1) + g\},
$$

means that a region whose alignment has become unprofitable is abandoned rather
than carried forward, and the first row and column are initialised to zero
rather than to cumulative penalties. The alignment is then read from the highest
cell anywhere in the matrix back to the first zero. Those two changes convert
the algorithm from "align everything" to "find the best-scoring segment pair",
which is the operation every database search tool approximates.

```{python}
#| label: sw-implementation
def smith_waterman(seq_a, seq_b, matrix, gap):
    """Local alignment: the same recursion, floored at zero."""
    n, m = len(seq_a), len(seq_b)
    score = np.zeros((n + 1, m + 1))
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        for j in range(1, m + 1):
            score[i, j] = max(0,
                              score[i - 1, j - 1] + matrix[seq_a[i - 1], seq_b[j - 1]],
                              score[i - 1, j] + gap,
                              score[i, j - 1] + gap)
    best = np.unravel_index(np.argmax(score), score.shape)
    return score, best


local_score, best_cell = smith_waterman(seq_a, seq_b, blosum50, GAP)
print(f"best local score {local_score[best_cell]:.0f} at cell {best_cell} "
      f"(global score was {score[-1, -1]:.0f})")
print(f"negative cells in the global matrix: {int((score < 0).sum())} of {score.size}")
print(f"negative cells in the local matrix:  {int((local_score < 0).sum())}")
```

The local score of 28 is much higher than the global score of 1, because the
local algorithm is allowed to report only the `AWGHE`/`AW-HE` region and discard
the rest. That is not a better alignment of the same thing; it is an alignment
of a different thing. Choosing between them is a biological decision: use global
alignment when you believe the sequences correspond throughout, and local
alignment when you believe only part of them does.

::: {.callout-tip title="A quick rule for choosing the mode"}
Ask what you would do if the two sequences shared only 30 residues out of 300. If
the right answer is "report that 30-residue match", you want local alignment. If
the right answer is "report that these sequences do not correspond", you want
global. Biopython's `PairwiseAligner` switches between them with
`mode="global"` and `mode="local"`, and most errors come from leaving the mode
at whatever it was for the previous analysis.
:::

## Gap models {#sec-aln-gaps}

The implementations above charge a fixed penalty $g$ for every gap position, a
**linear gap penalty**. Biologically that is wrong. Insertions and deletions
occur as events, and a single event can remove six residues as easily as one. A
linear penalty charges six times as much for the six-residue deletion as for the
one-residue deletion, and so prefers to explain the data with many short gaps
scattered through the alignment rather than one long gap in the loop where the
indel actually happened.

An **affine gap penalty** charges a large cost $d$ to open a gap and a small
cost $e$ to extend it, so that a gap of length $k$ costs $d + (k-1)e$
[@gotoh1982]. The classical BLAST protein defaults, $d = 11$ and $e = 1$, make a
six-residue gap cost 16 rather than 66. The effect on real sequences is easy to see.

```{python}
#| label: gap-models
import re

def gap_summary(alignment):
    """Count gap runs and the longest run in a pairwise alignment."""
    runs = re.findall(r"-+", str(alignment[0])) + re.findall(r"-+", str(alignment[1]))
    return len(runs), max((len(run) for run in runs), default=0)


for name, (open_score, extend_score) in {"linear (-4 each)": (-4, -4),
                                         "affine (-11, -1)": (-11, -1)}.items():
    model = PairwiseAligner(mode="global", substitution_matrix=blosum62,
                            open_gap_score=open_score, extend_gap_score=extend_score)
    alignment = model.align(globins["HBA_HUMAN"].seq, globins["MYG_HUMAN"].seq)[0]
    n_gaps, longest = gap_summary(alignment)
    print(f"{name:<18} score {alignment.score:6.0f}  columns {len(alignment[0]):4d}  "
          f"gap runs {n_gaps:2d}  longest {longest}  identity {percent_identity(alignment):.1f}%")
```

With a linear penalty the aligner opens 15 separate gaps, none longer than three
residues; with an affine penalty it opens two, one of them six residues long.
The affine alignment is the one that corresponds to a plausible evolutionary
history: haemoglobin and myoglobin differ by a small number of indel events in
surface loops, not by fifteen. This is why every production aligner — BLAST,
MAFFT, BWA, minimap2 [@li2018minimap2] — uses affine or piecewise-affine gap
costs, and why the two numbers that control it are worth setting deliberately
rather than leaving at a default you have not read.

## Substitution matrices {#sec-aln-matrices}

Everything above assumed a function $s(a_i, b_j)$ that scores a pair of
residues. For DNA a simple match/mismatch score is often adequate. For protein
it is not, because amino acid substitutions are not equally likely:
leucine-for-isoleucine is conservative and common, tryptophan-for-aspartate is
neither. A substitution matrix encodes those asymmetries as **log-odds scores**.
For a pair of residues $a$ and $b$,

$$
s(a, b) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \log \frac{q_{ab}}{p_a p_b},
$$

where $q_{ab}$ is the frequency with which $a$ and $b$ are seen aligned in
trusted alignments of related sequences, $p_a$ and $p_b$ are their background
frequencies, and $\lambda$ is a scale factor that lets the result be rounded to
integers. A positive score means the pair occurs in homologues more often than
chance would produce; a negative score means less often. The scores are
therefore statements about evolution, not about chemistry, although the two
largely agree.

The two families differ in how $q_{ab}$ was estimated. **PAM** matrices were
built by counting substitutions in alignments of very closely related proteins,
fitting a Markov model of accepted point mutations, and extrapolating that model
to greater evolutionary distances [@dayhoff1978]. PAM1 corresponds to one accepted point
mutation per hundred residues; PAM250 is that model multiplied by itself 250
times, and is intended for distant comparisons. **BLOSUM** matrices were built
directly from observed ungapped blocks of aligned protein families, with the
blocks clustered at a chosen identity threshold to avoid over-weighting close
relatives [@henikoff1992]. BLOSUM62 was derived from blocks clustered at 62%
identity, BLOSUM80 at 80%. The crucial and frequently misremembered consequence
is that the numbering runs in opposite directions: a *high* BLOSUM number suits
*close* sequences, whereas a *high* PAM number suits *distant* ones.

A small excerpt makes the content of a matrix concrete.

```{python}
#| label: blosum-excerpt
residues = "WLIKDR"
print("    " + "".join(f"{residue:>4}" for residue in residues))
for row_residue in residues:
    print(f"{row_residue:>3}" + "".join(f"{int(blosum62[row_residue, column]):4d}"
                                        for column in residues))
```

Tryptophan against itself scores 11, the highest value in BLOSUM62, because
tryptophan is rare and a conserved tryptophan is therefore strong evidence of
homology. Leucine against isoleucine scores 2 and lysine against arginine scores
2: both are conservative exchanges between residues with similar properties.
Aspartate against tryptophan scores −4. These are the numbers that decide every
alignment in this chapter.

### Showing that the choice matters {#sec-aln-matrix-choice}

It is easy to assert that matrix choice affects results and harder to believe it
until you see it. The bundled globins provide three comparisons at different
evolutionary distances: two myoglobins (close), the two haemoglobin chains
(moderate), and haemoglobin alpha against myoglobin (distant). Aligning each
pair under five matrices shows where the choice bites.

```{python}
#| label: matrix-comparison

PAIRS = [("MYG_HUMAN", "MYG_PHYMC", "close"),
```

closely related sequences, and to BLOSUM45 or PAM250 for searches at the edge of
detectability. A mismatched matrix rarely produces an obvious failure; it
produces a plausible-looking alignment that is shorter and differently gapped
than the correct one, which is far harder to notice.
:::

## Heuristic database search with BLAST {#sec-aln-blast}

Smith–Waterman is optimal and far too slow for a database search: comparing one
300-residue query against a database of $10^8$ residues means filling $3 \times
10^{10}$ cells. The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool trades that guarantee for
speed [@altschul1990]. Its reasoning is that any alignment worth reporting
almost certainly contains a short exact or near-exact match, so instead of
examining every cell it looks only at the neighbourhoods of such **seeds**.

The procedure has three stages. *Seeding*: the query is broken into overlapping
words — three residues for protein, eleven bases for nucleotide by default —
and for proteins a neighbourhood of similar words scoring above a threshold is
generated for each. *Extension*: each database position matching a seed word is
extended outwards, and extension stops when the running score falls too far
below the best value seen, which is what makes the search sublinear in practice.
*Evaluation*: surviving high-scoring segment pairs are assembled, gapped
alignments are computed around them, and the results are scored statistically
[@altschul1997]. The original FASTA algorithm uses the same seed-and-extend idea
with a different seeding rule and remains a useful cross-check [@pearson1988].

The flavours differ only in what is translated before the comparison:
`blastn` compares nucleotide against nucleotide, `blastp` protein against
protein, `blastx` a translated nucleotide query against proteins, `tblastn` a
protein query against a translated nucleotide database, and `tblastx` both
translated. The decision follows from what you have and what you are looking
for; when in doubt, comparing at the protein level detects homology at
considerably greater evolutionary distance than comparing at the nucleotide
level, because synonymous changes are invisible to it.

Building a searchable database from the bundled sequences takes one command.


### The statistics, and why the database size is in them {#sec-aln-blast-stats}

Three numbers describe each hit. The **raw score** is the sum of substitution
and gap scores for the alignment, and it is uninterpretable on its own because
it depends on the matrix and gap costs used. The **bit score** normalises the
raw score by those parameters, $S' = (\lambda S - \ln K)/\ln 2$, so that bit
scores computed with different scoring schemes are comparable and a bit score of
40 means the same thing in any search. The **E-value** is the number of hits
with at least this score that you would expect to see by chance in a search of
this size:

$$
E = \frac{mn}{2^{S'}},
$$

where $m$ is the query length and $n$ the total number of residues in the
database. The bit score is a property of the alignment; the E-value is a
property of the alignment *and the database you searched*. Doubling the database
doubles the E-value of every hit without changing a single alignment. BLAST+
lets you set the effective database size directly, which turns that statement
into a demonstration.

```{python}
#| label: blast-dbsize
def search(dbsize=None):
    command = ["blastp", "-query", str(query_fasta), "-db", str(database),
               "-outfmt", "6 " + " ".join(COLUMNS), "-evalue", "10"]
    if dbsize is not None:
        command += ["-dbsize", str(dbsize)]
    output = subprocess.run(command, check=True, capture_output=True, text=True).stdout
    table = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(output), sep="\t", names=COLUMNS)
    return table.loc[table["sseqid"].str.contains("HBA_HUMAN")].iloc[0]

actual_residues = sum(len(record.seq) for record in records)
for dbsize in (None, 1_000_000, 100_000_000):
    hit = search(dbsize)
    label = f"{actual_residues:,} (actual)" if dbsize is None else f"{dbsize:,}"
    print(f"database size {label:>22}  bit score {hit['bitscore']:5.1f}  "
          f"E-value {hit['evalue']:.2e}")
```

The bit score is 44.3 in all three searches, because the alignment never
changes. The E-value moves by more than four orders of magnitude, in exact proportion to
the database size. This is why an E-value
quoted without the database it came from is meaningless, why a hit that is
convincing against a small curated database may be unremarkable against the
whole of the non-redundant protein set, and why the common habit of reporting
"E < 1e-5" as though it were a universal threshold is unsound. The threshold you
want depends on how many comparisons you made.

::: {.callout-tip title="Three habits that make BLAST results defensible"}
Record the database and its version or date alongside every result, because the
E-values are not reproducible without it. Check the self-hit before reading
anything else. And look at the alignment, not just the table: a high-scoring hit
confined to a low-complexity run of glutamines or a coiled-coil region is a
compositional artefact, not evidence of shared ancestry, which is why BLAST
filters such regions by default and why turning that filter off requires a
reason.
:::

## Multiple sequence alignment {#sec-aln-msa}

A pairwise alignment compares two sequences; a **multiple sequence alignment
(MSA)** arranges many sequences into a single set of columns, so that each
column is a hypothesis that the residues in it descend from one ancestral
position. That is a much stronger claim than any pairwise comparison makes, and
everything downstream — conservation analysis, profile construction, tree
building, structure-guided mutagenesis — depends on it being roughly right.

Extending dynamic programming to $N$ sequences is possible and useless: the
table has $N$ dimensions and the cost grows exponentially. **Progressive
alignment**, introduced in the form that ClustalW made standard
[@thompson1994], sidesteps this. It computes all pairwise distances, builds a
quick guide tree from them, and then aligns sequences in the order that tree
suggests, most similar first, merging alignments into larger alignments as it
climbs. Each merge is a pairwise problem, so each is tractable.

The weakness of the approach is stated in its own name. Gaps placed early are
never revisited: *once a gap, always a gap*. An error made while aligning two
close sequences propagates into every later merge, and the order in which
sequences are supplied can change the result. Modern tools mitigate this with
iterative refinement, which realigns subsets of the alignment and keeps changes
that improve an objective function. MAFFT does this, and additionally uses a
fast Fourier transform to find homologous segments quickly, which is what makes
it practical on thousands of sequences [@katoh2013]. MUSCLE [@edgar2004] and
Clustal Omega [@sievers2011] are the other two tools you will meet most often;
Clustal Omega scales to the largest datasets, and MUSCLE's refinement is
thorough on moderate ones.

The bundled p53 orthologues span 400 million years of vertebrate evolution,
from human to medaka, which is enough divergence to make the alignment
non-trivial.

```{python}

      f"{alignment.get_alignment_length()} columns")
print("unaligned lengths:",
      sorted(len(record.seq.replace("-", "")) for record in alignment))
```

Eight proteins of 352 to 393 residues produce an alignment 427 columns long, so
MAFFT has added 34 columns beyond the longest sequence to accommodate insertions
that are present in some lineages and not others. Counting how the columns behave is the
first thing to do with any alignment.

```{python}
#| label: alignment-columns
from collections import Counter

def column_consensus(alignment, index):
    """Fraction of sequences carrying the most common residue in one column."""
    column = [record.seq[index] for record in alignment]
    residues = [residue for residue in column if residue != "-"]
    if not residues:
        return 0.0
    return Counter(residues).most_common(1)[0][1] / len(alignment)


n_columns = alignment.get_alignment_length()
conservation = [column_consensus(alignment, index) for index in range(n_columns)]
gap_free = sum(1 for index in range(n_columns)
               if all(record.seq[index] != "-" for record in alignment))
invariant = sum(1 for index in range(n_columns)
                if len({record.seq[index] for record in alignment}) == 1
                and alignment[0].seq[index] != "-")

print(f"columns:            {n_columns}")
print(f"gap-free columns:   {gap_free}")
print(f"invariant columns:  {invariant} "
      f"({100 * invariant / n_columns:.0f}% of all columns)")
```

A hundred and twenty-eight columns are identical in all eight species, including
zebrafish and medaka. Selection has held those positions fixed for hundreds of
millions of years, which is strong evidence that they do something the protein
cannot do without. Where those columns sit is the interesting question, and
@fig-aln-conservation answers it by projecting conservation onto human p53
coordinates.

```{python}
#| label: fig-aln-conservation

The grey trace is the per-column fraction of sequences sharing the most common residue; the heavy line is an 11-residue running mean. The shaded band marks the DNA-binding domain (residues 102–292), where conservation is markedly higher than in the flanking transactivation and regulatory regions."
human = next(record for record in alignment if record.id == "HUMAN")

       ylim=(0, 1.12), title="Conservation across eight p53 orthologues")
fig.tight_layout()
```

```{python}
#| label: conservation-domains

             if 102 <= position <= 292]

           if not 102 <= position <= 292]
print(f"mean conservation inside the DNA-binding domain:  {np.mean(in_domain):.2f}")
print(f"mean conservation outside it:                     {np.mean(outside):.2f}")
```

The DNA-binding domain averages 0.87 against 0.66 elsewhere. That is the
alignment recovering, from sequence alone, the domain structure that
crystallography established independently, and it is also an explanation of a
clinical observation: the mutations that matter in human cancer cluster in this
domain because this is the part of the protein that cannot absorb change. Read
in the other direction, the figure is a warning about the poorly conserved
regions. Low conservation does not mean unimportant — the transactivation
domain at the N-terminus is intrinsically disordered and functionally essential
— it means that the constraint acting there is not on individual residues.

::: {.callout-warning title="Look at the alignment before you use it"}
Automatic alignment of divergent sequences is not reliable at the level of
individual columns, particularly in gap-rich regions. Before using an alignment
for a tree or for a conservation claim, view it, and consider trimming poorly
aligned blocks. An alignment that is 40% gaps is telling you that the sequences
should not all have been aligned together, or that one of them is a fragment, or
that a sequence has been included that is not a member of the family at all.
:::

## Distance-based phylogenetic inference {#sec-aln-tree}

A multiple alignment implies a history, and a tree is an explicit hypothesis
about it. The cheapest route from one to the other has two steps: convert the
alignment into a matrix of pairwise distances, then build a tree whose branch
lengths reproduce those distances as closely as possible.

Biopython's `DistanceCalculator` computes the matrix. The `blosum62` model
scores each pair of aligned sequences with the substitution matrix and converts
the normalised score into a distance; it is a similarity-derived measure, not an
estimate of substitutions per site, and the numbers should be read as relative
rather than absolute.

```{python}
#| label: distance-matrix
from Bio.Phylo.TreeConstruction import DistanceCalculator, DistanceTreeConstructor

calculator = DistanceCalculator("blosum62")
distances = calculator.get_distance(alignment)

names = [record.id for record in alignment]
print("     " + "".join(f"{name:>8}" for name in names))
for row_name in names:
    print(f"{row_name:>5}" + "".join(f"{distances[row_name, column]:8.3f}"
                                     for column in names))
```

The structure is visible before any tree is built. Human and tree shrew are the
closest pair at 0.071, with mouse and rat next at 0.114; the two fish sit at
0.44 to 0.58 from everything else. **Neighbour-joining**
converts such a matrix into a tree [@saitou1987]. It repeatedly joins the pair
of taxa that most reduces the total branch length of the tree, which is not the
same as joining the closest pair, and that distinction is what allows it to
behave correctly when lineages evolve at different rates.

A tree drawn without support values invites more confidence than it deserves.
**Bootstrapping** supplies the missing caveat [@felsenstein1985]: columns are
resampled from the alignment with replacement to make a pseudo-replicate of the
same width, a tree is built from each replicate, and each branch of the original
tree is labelled with the percentage of replicates that contain it. Biopython
runs the whole procedure in a few seconds for an alignment of this size.

```{python}
#| label: bootstrap
from Bio.Phylo.Consensus import bootstrap_trees, get_support

random.seed(0)
constructor = DistanceTreeConstructor(calculator, "nj")
tree = constructor.build_tree(alignment)
tree.root_at_midpoint()

replicates = list(bootstrap_trees(alignment, 100, constructor))
supported = get_support(tree, replicates)

for clade in supported.get_nonterminals():
    members = sorted(tip.name for tip in clade.get_terminals())
    if clade.confidence is not None and len(members) < len(names):
        print(f"{clade.confidence:5.0f}%  {', '.join(members)}")
```

@fig-aln-tree draws the result with those values on the branches.

```{python}
#| label: fig-aln-tree
#| fig-cap: "Neighbour-joining tree of eight vertebrate p53 orthologues, built from BLOSUM62 distances over the MAFFT alignment and rooted at its midpoint. Branch labels are bootstrap support from 100 resampled replicates. The fish clade, the mouse–rat pair and the human–tree shrew pair are each recovered in every replicate, while the branching order among the remaining mammals is supported in fewer than a third of them."
from Bio import Phylo

           label_func=lambda clade: clade.name if clade.is_terminal() else "",
           branch_labels=lambda clade: ""
           if clade.is_terminal() or clade.confidence is None
           else f"{clade.confidence:.0f}")
ax.set(xlabel="Distance (1 − normalised BLOSUM62 similarity)", ylabel="")
ax.set_yticks([])
ax.grid(False)
fig.tight_layout()
```

The tree is partly convincing and partly not, and saying which is which is the
whole point of putting numbers on the branches. The two fish group together in
every replicate; so do mouse and rat; so do human and tree shrew, which is
consistent with tree shrews being close relatives of primates. The branching
order among cattle, cat and the primate pair is supported in 18% and 34% of
replicates, which is to say the alignment does not resolve it. Reporting that
tree without the numbers would present a resolution the data does not contain.

Three limitations of this approach deserve stating plainly. First, reducing an
alignment to a distance matrix throws away which columns disagree, and the lost
information is exactly what distinguishes competing topologies; character-based
methods — maximum likelihood as implemented in RAxML [@stamatakis2014] or
IQ-TREE [@minh2020], or Bayesian inference — use it and are the standard for
published phylogenies. Second, a similarity-derived distance is not corrected
for multiple substitutions at the same site, so long branches are
systematically underestimated; MEGA and comparable packages offer corrected
models [@tamura2021]. Third, bootstrap support measures how consistently the
*data* support a branch under this method, not whether the method is right: a
systematically biased analysis can produce 100% support for the wrong tree, and
frequently has. Use distance methods for a quick look at a family, as here, and
a likelihood method for anything you intend to publish.

## Summary {#sec-04-summary}

- Similarity is measured; homology is inferred. Orthologues arise by
  speciation and usually keep their function; paralogues arise by duplication
  and need not, which is why annotation transferred between paralogues is a
  common source of error.
- Dynamic programming finds the optimal alignment under a scoring scheme by
  building a table of prefix alignments and tracing back. The recursion is three
  lines long and the guarantee it provides is exact.
- Global alignment assumes the sequences correspond throughout; local alignment
  finds the best-scoring segment pair. Choosing between them is a biological
  decision, not a default.
- Affine gap penalties charge once to open a gap and little to extend it, which
  reproduces the fact that indels are events. A linear penalty scatters many
  short gaps where one long gap belongs.
- Substitution matrices are log-odds tables. High BLOSUM numbers suit close
  sequences and high PAM numbers suit distant ones, and using a matrix built for
  the wrong distance silently shortens and re-gaps the alignment rather than
  failing visibly.
- A percentage identity without an alignment length is uninterpretable, because
  short alignments of unrelated sequences routinely exceed 80% identity.
- BLAST trades the optimality guarantee for speed by seeding on short words and
  extending them. Bit scores are comparable across searches; E-values are not,
  because they scale with the size of the database searched.
- Progressive multiple alignment is fast and order-dependent: gaps placed early
  are never revisited, so inspect the alignment before building anything on it.
- Conservation in a multiple alignment localises functional constraint — for
  p53, onto the DNA-binding domain where cancer mutations cluster — but low
  conservation does not imply unimportance.
- Distance-based trees are quick and lossy. Report bootstrap support, and read
  it as a measure of consistency within the method rather than of correctness.

## Solutions {#sec-04-solutions}

::: {.callout-caution collapse="true" title="Solutions"}

**1.** The optimal score is 4. `GATTACA` against `GAT-ACA` aligns six matches
and one gap: $6 \times 1 - 2 = 4$. The alignment is not unique, because the gap
can equally be placed at either of the two `T` positions — `GATTACA` against
`GA-TACA` scores the same. Equal-scoring alternatives are common and aligners
report only one of them.

```{python}
#| label: soln-04-1
from Bio.Align import PairwiseAligner as Aligner
simple = Aligner(mode="global", match_score=1, mismatch_score=-1,
                 open_gap_score=-2, extend_gap_score=-2)
alignments = simple.align("GATTACA", "GATACA")
print(f"score {alignments.score:.0f}; {len(alignments)} co-optimal alignments")
print(alignments[0])
```

**2.** Set the first row and the first column to zero instead of to cumulative
gap penalties, and take the traceback start from the best cell in the final row
or final column rather than from the corner. The recursion itself is unchanged;
only the boundary conditions and the start of the traceback differ.

**3.** The local alignment scores higher over fewer columns; the global number
is the one to quote for two full-length proteins, with the column count beside
it.

```{python}
#| label: soln-04-3
for mode in ("global", "local"):
    model = PairwiseAligner(mode=mode, substitution_matrix=blosum62,
                            open_gap_score=-11, extend_gap_score=-1)
    result = model.align(globins["HBB_HUMAN"].seq, globins["MYG_PHYMC"].seq)[0]
    aligned = sum(1 for x, y in zip(result[0], result[1]) if x != "-" and y != "-")
    print(f"{mode:<7} score {result.score:6.0f}  identity {percent_identity(result):5.1f}%  "
          f"aligned columns {aligned}")
```

**4.** A global aligner must align the whole of both sequences, so the
denominator is always close to the sequence length and chance matches are
diluted across it. Identity values are therefore not comparable between studies
unless the alignment mode, the matrix and the gap costs are all stated.

```{python}
#| label: soln-04-4
global_model = PairwiseAligner(mode="global", substitution_matrix=blosum62,
                              open_gap_score=-11, extend_gap_score=-1)
random.seed(7)
global_identities = []
for _ in range(50):
    shuffled = "".join(random.sample(str(globins["MYG_HUMAN"].seq),
                                     len(globins["MYG_HUMAN"].seq)))
    global_identities.append(percent_identity(global_model.align(
        globins["HBA_HUMAN"].seq, shuffled)[0]))
print(f"global decoys: mean identity {statistics.mean(global_identities):.1f}% "
      f"(best {max(global_identities):.1f}%)")
print(f"local decoys:  mean identity {statistics.mean(null_identities):.1f}% "
      f"(best {max(null_identities):.1f}%)")
```

**5.** None pass. Myoglobin and p53 are unrelated, so every alignment found is a
short chance match; the earlier search showed the best of them at E = 0.47,
which is more than two orders of magnitude above the threshold. The exercise is
worth running because a search that returns a table is not a search that found
anything.

**6.** E-values are comparable only within one search against one database,
because the same alignment receives a different E-value in a larger database.
Comparing hits across searches requires the bit score, which is normalised for
the scoring system and independent of database size. If the comparison really
must use E-values, both searches have to be run against the same database with
the same effective size.

**7.** MAFFT's `--auto` mode applies iterative refinement to a dataset of this
size, so the alignment is largely stable to input order and the counts usually
agree exactly; where they differ, the difference is confined to gap-rich
regions. A purely progressive aligner without refinement would show larger
differences. The general lesson is that input order is a parameter, and a
pipeline that sorts its input is more reproducible than one that does not.

**8.** The identity model ignores the substitution matrix and counts mismatches
only, so the distances change — but for these eight sequences the topology does
not: the same five groupings are recovered, including the weakly supported
cattle–cat pair. That is the useful observation. A conclusion that survives a
change of distance model is worth reporting; one that does not survive it was
never about the biology.

## Further reading {#sec-04-further-reading}

- @needleman1970 is the original global alignment paper and is short enough to
  read in an afternoon; the recursion in it is the one implemented above.
- @smith1981 introduces local alignment in two pages and is the clearest
  statement of why the zero term changes the question.
- @henikoff1992 describes how the BLOSUM matrices were constructed, including
  the clustering step that distinguishes them from PAM.
- @altschul1997 is the gapped-BLAST and PSI-BLAST paper, and the place to read
  about the alignment statistics that produce the E-value.
- @katoh2013 documents MAFFT's algorithms and, usefully, states which of its
  strategies is appropriate for which dataset size.
- @eddy2011 explains profile hidden Markov models, which detect homology well
  beyond the range of pairwise methods and underlie Pfam [@mistry2021].
- @felsenstein1985 introduced the bootstrap to phylogenetics and remains the
  best account of what the numbers on a tree do and do not mean.
- @minh2020 describes IQ-TREE, a practical route from the alignment produced in
  this chapter to a maximum-likelihood tree with model selection.
