12  Statistics and machine learning on omics data

After working through this chapter you will be able to

  • distinguish technical from biological replication and say which one licences a claim about a population
  • normalise a count matrix and demonstrate why raw counts cannot be compared across libraries
  • diagnose a batch effect, and quantify how much of a confounded comparison it explains
  • interpret a principal component analysis, including variance explained and loadings
  • choose and defend a number of clusters, and test whether a partition is stable
  • build a cross-validated classifier in scikit-learn without leaking information between folds
  • evaluate a model with precision, recall and the curves that remain informative under class imbalance

A count matrix is not a result. Between the matrix and a defensible statement about biology sit a dozen decisions — which genes to keep, how to make libraries comparable, what to do about the fact that samples were prepared in two different ways, how to correct thousands of simultaneous tests, whether an apparent cluster is real, and whether a classifier that reports 100% accuracy has learned anything at all. Each decision can turn a real effect into a null result or, more often, turn noise into a publishable figure.

This chapter works through those decisions on the bundled Drosophila RNA-seq dataset in data/expression/pasilla_gene_counts.tsv: 14,599 genes measured in seven samples, three in which the gene pasilla was knocked down by RNA interference and four untreated controls, with the sample metadata in data/expression/pasilla_sample_annotation.csv, from the study that first reported it (Brooks et al. 2010). The dataset is small enough to hold in your head and real enough to misbehave. In particular, its libraries were built two different ways — three single-read, four paired-end — and that difference is almost as large a source of variation as the treatment itself, which makes it an unusually honest teaching set.

The single most valuable result in this chapter is in the section on supervised learning. Selecting informative genes before cross-validation rather than inside it produces a classifier that reports perfect accuracy on data whose labels have been randomly shuffled. You will run that demonstration and see the number.

12.2 Designing an experiment that can answer the question

No amount of analysis repairs a design that cannot answer the question, and the most common failure is not a shortage of data but the wrong kind of replication.

A technical replicate repeats the measurement on the same biological material: the same RNA extract sequenced twice, the same library loaded on two lanes. It estimates the variability of the measurement process. A biological replicate repeats the whole experiment on independent material: a different fly culture, a different patient, a different mouse. It estimates the variability of the biological population, which is what almost every scientific claim is about.

Technical replicates cannot substitute for biological ones. Sequencing one RNA sample across twenty lanes gives twenty highly concordant measurements of that one sample, a reassuringly small standard error, and no information whatsoever about whether another fly would have behaved the same way. The resulting p-value answers a question nobody asked. The bundled metadata records that some samples were run across five or six lanes; those lanes were summed into one count column, which is the correct treatment, because they were never independent observations of biology.

sample_info = pd.read_csv(book_data("expression", "pasilla_sample_annotation.csv"))
sample_info["sample"] = sample_info["file"].str.replace("fb", "", regex=False)
sample_info = sample_info.set_index("sample")

print(sample_info[["condition", "type", "number of lanes"]])
#>             condition         type  number of lanes
#> sample                                             
#> treated1      treated  single-read                5
#> treated2      treated   paired-end                2
#> treated3      treated   paired-end                2
#> untreated1  untreated  single-read                2
#> untreated2  untreated  single-read                6
#> untreated3  untreated   paired-end                2
#> untreated4  untreated   paired-end                2

Three further design points decide whether the analysis below is worth running.

Replicate number. Three per group is the practical floor for RNA-seq and detects only large effects; the pasilla design has three treated and four untreated. With fewer than three, variance cannot be estimated per gene at all, which is why methods such as DESeq2 (Love, Huber, and Anders 2014) and edgeR (Robinson, McCarthy, and Smyth 2009) share information across genes to stabilise the estimate.

Randomisation. Assign samples to processing batches, lanes and extraction days at random. Processing all controls on Monday and all treated samples on Tuesday guarantees that a day effect is indistinguishable from a treatment effect.

Blocking. When a nuisance factor cannot be avoided — two sequencing runs, two operators — ensure every level of the factor contains both conditions, and record it. A recorded nuisance factor can be modelled; an unrecorded one cannot.

How many replicates are enough is a question of effect size, variability and the smallest difference worth detecting, and it has no universal answer. The useful discipline is to decide, before the experiment, what difference would change what you do next — a twofold change in a handful of genes, or a subtle shift across a pathway — and to ask whether the planned design could detect it. For RNA-seq the rough guidance from published power studies is that three replicates per group detect twofold changes in well-expressed genes, six begin to reach 1.5-fold, and detecting small coordinated shifts needs tens. A study powered for the wrong effect size produces a null result that cannot be distinguished from an absent effect, which is the most expensive outcome in the catalogue.

Confounding is a property of the design, not the data

If every treated sample was prepared with one protocol and every control with another, no statistical method can separate protocol from treatment. Batch correction, covariate adjustment and sophisticated models all fail identically, because the information is absent. The only fix is a different experiment.

12.3 The shape of an omics matrix

Tidy data has one row per observation and one column per variable (Wickham et al. 2019). An omics count matrix deliberately breaks that rule: it is stored with one row per gene and one column per sample, because the matrix form is what linear algebra and most statistical software expect. The tidy form is useful for plotting and joining, the matrix form for computation, and a working analysis moves between them; pandas supplies both representations and the operations that convert one into the other (McKinney 2010).

counts = pd.read_csv(book_data("expression", "pasilla_gene_counts.tsv"),
                     sep="\t", index_col=0)
counts = counts[sample_info.index]          # fix column order to match metadata

print(f"{counts.shape[0]:,} genes x {counts.shape[1]} samples")
#> 14,599 genes x 7 samples
print(counts.iloc[:3, :4])
#>              treated1  treated2  treated3  untreated1
#> gene_id                                              
#> FBgn0000003         0         0         1           0
#> FBgn0000008       140        88        70          92
#> FBgn0000014         4         0         0           5

The shape is the problem. There are 14,599 features and seven samples, so p ≫ n: vastly more variables than observations. Three consequences follow, and every statistical trap later in the chapter is one of them in disguise.

First, any two groups of samples can be separated perfectly by some subset of features, even when the labels are random. With 14,599 genes and seven samples there is always a gene that happens to split them. Second, distances become uninformative: in high dimensions the distance from a point to its nearest neighbour approaches the distance to its farthest, so “these samples cluster together” needs evidence beyond a plot. Third, the number of possible models vastly exceeds the data available to choose between them, so model selection needs external validation rather than internal fit.

Low-count genes contribute noise without information, and removing them before anything else reduces the testing burden.

expressed = counts[(counts > 5).sum(axis=1) >= 3]

print(f"genes with no counts at all: {(counts.sum(axis=1) == 0).sum():,}")
#> genes with no counts at all: 2,240
print(f"genes retained (>5 counts in >=3 samples): {expressed.shape[0]:,}")
#> genes retained (>5 counts in >=3 samples): 8,621

The filter is a judgement, not a rule. Requiring counts above five in at least three samples — the size of the smallest group — keeps genes that could show a group-specific signal and discards those that cannot. State the rule in your methods, because it changes how many tests you perform and therefore every corrected p-value.

12.4 Exploring and normalising count data

RNA-seq counts are not normally distributed. They are non-negative integers, strongly right-skewed, and their variance grows with their mean — a gene averaging 10,000 reads varies far more in absolute terms than one averaging 10. Figure 12.1 shows both facts alongside the library sizes that make raw counts incomparable.

library_sizes = expressed.sum() / 1e6
cpm = expressed / expressed.sum() * 1e6
log_cpm = np.log2(cpm + 1)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9, 3.2))

Three features of the left and centre panels deserve attention before any modelling. Counts span roughly five orders of magnitude within a single sample, so an untransformed matrix is dominated by a few hundred genes; the log transform in the centre panel is what makes the rest visible. Zeros are abundant — 2,240 of the 14,599 genes have no counts in any sample — and a zero is ambiguous, meaning either that a transcript is absent or that sequencing was too shallow to see it, which is why a pseudocount is added before taking logarithms rather than discarding the zeros. And the right-hand panel shows variance rising faster than the mean, the overdispersion that makes the Poisson distribution inadequate and the negative binomial the standard choice for count models.

Library sizes range from 8.4 to 21.9 million counts. A gene with 100 reads in the smallest library is twice as abundant, proportionally, as a gene with 100 reads in the largest. Normalisation removes that difference. The simplest method, counts per million (CPM), divides each count by its library total; it is what the code above computes. The demonstration below shows what it fixes.

pair = ["untreated2", "untreated3"]
gene = "FBgn0000008"

print("raw counts:", counts.loc[gene, pair].tolist(),
      "ratio", round(counts.loc[gene, pair[0]] / counts.loc[gene, pair[1]], 2))
#> raw counts: [161, 76] ratio 2.12
print("CPM:", cpm.loc[gene, pair].round(2).tolist(),
      "ratio", round(cpm.loc[gene, pair[0]] / cpm.loc[gene, pair[1]], 2))
#> CPM: [7.35, 9.1] ratio 0.81

raw_ratio = np.log2(expressed[pair[0]] + 1) - np.log2(expressed[pair[1]] + 1)
cpm_ratio = log_cpm[pair[0]] - log_cpm[pair[1]]
print(f"median log2 ratio across all genes - raw: {raw_ratio.median():.3f}, "
      f"CPM: {cpm_ratio.median():.3f}")
#> median log2 ratio across all genes - raw: 1.443, CPM: 0.056

The gene looks 2.12-fold up in untreated2 on raw counts and 0.81-fold — that is, slightly down — after normalisation. The direction of the effect reverses. The summary line explains why: before normalisation the median log ratio across all 8,621 genes is 1.443, almost exactly the log ratio of the two library sizes, because most genes do not change and the apparent shift is entirely a depth artefact. After CPM the median is 0.056, close to the zero that a comparison of two control samples should produce.

CPM is the simplest method, not the best one

CPM assumes total counts are comparable, which fails when a few very highly expressed genes dominate one sample — common in tissue with a dominant transcript. The median-of-ratios method in DESeq2 (Love, Huber, and Anders 2014) and trimmed mean of M-values in edgeR (Robinson, McCarthy, and Smyth 2009) estimate a size factor from the bulk of genes instead, and are the defaults for differential expression. Transcript-length normalisation (TPM, FPKM) additionally divides by gene length, which is necessary when comparing different genes but not when comparing one gene across samples.

12.5 Batch effects and confounding

A batch effect is systematic variation introduced by how samples were processed rather than by what they are: reagent lot, sequencing run, extraction day, library protocol (Leek et al. 2010). Batch effects are not noise. They are reproducible, often large, and they bias in a consistent direction — which is exactly what makes them look like biology.

The pasilla design contains a real one. Four samples were sequenced paired-end and three single-read, and that split cuts across both conditions. Because it cuts across, the effect can be measured directly: compare untreated samples with one library type against untreated samples with the other, where no treatment difference exists by construction.

def two_group_p(group_a, group_b):
    """Per-gene two-sample t-test on log-CPM; returns a Series of p-values."""
    stat, p = stats.ttest_ind(log_cpm[group_a], log_cpm[group_b], axis=1)
    return pd.Series(p, index=log_cpm.index)

batch_only = two_group_p(["untreated1", "untreated2"],    # single-read
                         ["untreated3", "untreated4"])    # paired-end

n_genes = log_cpm.shape[0]
print(f"genes tested: {n_genes:,}; expected at p < 0.05 by chance: {0.05 * n_genes:.0f}")
#> genes tested: 8,621; expected at p < 0.05 by chance: 431
print(f"library-type contrast, untreated only: {(batch_only < 0.05).sum():,} genes at p < 0.05")
#> library-type contrast, untreated only: 676 genes at p < 0.05

Within a single biological condition, 676 genes reach p < 0.05 against an expectation of 431. The library protocol changes measured expression for hundreds of genes.

Now construct the failure. Take two paired-end treated samples and two single-read untreated samples. In that four-sample subset, condition and library type are perfectly confounded — exactly the design produced by processing cases and controls separately.

confounded = two_group_p(["treated2", "treated3"],        # both paired-end
                         ["untreated1", "untreated2"])    # both single-read
proper = two_group_p(["treated1", "treated2", "treated3"],
                     ["untreated1", "untreated2", "untreated3", "untreated4"])

batch_hits = set(batch_only[batch_only < 0.05].index)
conf_hits = set(confounded[confounded < 0.05].index)
proper_hits = set(proper[proper < 0.05].index)

print(f"confounded contrast: {len(conf_hits):,} genes at p < 0.05")
#> confounded contrast: 1,533 genes at p < 0.05
print(f"  of these, also significant in the pure library-type contrast: "
      f"{len(conf_hits & batch_hits):,} "
      f"({100 * len(conf_hits & batch_hits) / len(conf_hits):.1f}%)")
#>   of these, also significant in the pure library-type contrast: 342 (22.3%)
print(f"  expected by chance: {100 * len(batch_hits) / n_genes:.1f}%")
#>   expected by chance: 7.8%
print(f"  also found by the properly balanced contrast: "
      f"{100 * len(conf_hits & proper_hits) / len(conf_hits):.1f}%")
#>   also found by the properly balanced contrast: 45.5%

The confounded comparison returns 1,533 genes. Of those, 22.3% also appear in the pure library-type contrast, against 7.8% expected by chance — a nearly threefold enrichment for genes known to respond to protocol rather than treatment. Fewer than half of the 1,533 are recovered by the balanced comparison using all seven samples. Written up without the metadata, this list would read as a treatment signature; it is in substantial part a protocol signature, and nothing in the numbers themselves distinguishes the two.

The remedies, in order of effectiveness: design the experiment so batch is balanced across conditions; record batch so it can be included as a covariate in the model; or, if neither was done, apply an empirical Bayes correction such as ComBat (Johnson, Li, and Rabinovic 2006) and report it as a limitation. Correction after the fact removes variation that may contain signal, and it can only ever approximate what balanced design gives for free.

12.6 Multiple testing and the false discovery problem

Testing 8,621 genes at a 5% threshold produces about 431 significant results when nothing is happening. The simulation below makes that concrete, then shows what the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure does about it (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995).

rng = np.random.default_rng(20250101)

def benjamini_hochberg(p_values):
    """Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values, written out rather than imported."""
    p = np.asarray(p_values)
    m = len(p)
    order = np.argsort(p)
    ranked = p[order] * m / np.arange(1, m + 1)
    adjusted = np.minimum.accumulate(ranked[::-1])[::-1]
    out = np.empty(m)
    out[order] = np.minimum(adjusted, 1)
    return out

null_data = rng.normal(size=(10_000, 20))      # no difference between groups
_, p_null = stats.ttest_ind(null_data[:, :10], null_data[:, 10:], axis=1)

print(f"pure noise: {(p_null < 0.05).sum()} of 10,000 'genes' at p < 0.05")
#> pure noise: 501 of 10,000 'genes' at p < 0.05
print(f"pure noise: {(benjamini_hochberg(p_null) < 0.05).sum()} survive BH correction")
#> pure noise: 0 survive BH correction

501 false positives before correction, none after. Now add real signal: 200 of the 10,000 genes are given a genuine difference between groups.

mixed = rng.normal(size=(10_000, 20))
mixed[:200, 10:] += 2.0                        # the first 200 genes truly differ
_, p_mixed = stats.ttest_ind(mixed[:, :10], mixed[:, 10:], axis=1)

significant = benjamini_hochberg(p_mixed) < 0.05
true_positives = significant[:200].sum()
false_positives = significant[200:].sum()

print(f"raw p < 0.05: {(p_mixed < 0.05).sum()}")
#> raw p < 0.05: 720
print(f"BH significant: {significant.sum()}  "
      f"(true {true_positives}, false {false_positives})")
#> BH significant: 133  (true 126, false 7)
print(f"observed false discovery proportion: "
      f"{false_positives / significant.sum():.3f}")
#> observed false discovery proportion: 0.053
print(f"power to detect the 200 real effects: {true_positives / 200:.2f}")
#> power to detect the 200 real effects: 0.63

The procedure delivers what it promises: 133 discoveries of which seven are false, a false discovery proportion of 0.053 against the nominal 0.05. It also shows the cost — only 126 of the 200 real effects are found, because controlling false positives always sacrifices power. The false discovery rate is the expected proportion of false positives among the results you call significant, which is the quantity that matters when the output is a list of candidates for follow-up. The family-wise error rate controlled by Bonferroni is the probability of even one false positive, which is the right target only when a single wrong call invalidates the study. Storey’s q-value refines the FDR estimate by estimating the proportion of true nulls rather than assuming it is one (Storey and Tibshirani 2003).

One legitimate way to recover power is independent filtering: removing tests that have little chance of being significant, using a criterion independent of the test statistic under the null. Mean expression is such a criterion, which is why the low-count filter applied earlier is not merely tidying — it reduces the number of tests, and therefore the correction burden, without biasing the remaining p-values. Filtering on something correlated with the test statistic, such as fold change, does bias them, and is not independent filtering at all.

Correct for every test you performed

The correction must cover all tests actually conducted, including the ones not reported. Running the analysis with three filtering thresholds and reporting the one with most hits is multiple testing with an unrecorded denominator, and no correction applied afterwards can repair it.

12.7 Finding structure without labels

Principal component analysis (PCA) re-expresses samples in new coordinates — principal components — that are uncorrelated linear combinations of the original features, ordered so that the first captures the most variance. It is the first diagnostic to run on any omics matrix, because the leading components usually reveal whichever factor dominates the experiment, and that factor is often not the one you intended to study.

Three choices control what PCA shows. Transform count data first, or the result will be dominated by a handful of highly expressed genes. Centre the features, always. Whether to scale them to unit variance depends: on log-CPM data, scaling gives low-expression noisy genes the same weight as informative ones, so centring without scaling is the usual choice for RNA-seq.

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

expression_matrix = log_cpm.T.values            # samples as rows, genes as columns
centred = expression_matrix - expression_matrix.mean(axis=0)

pca = PCA(n_components=4).fit(centred)
scores = pca.transform(centred)

variance_explained = 100 * pca.explained_variance_ratio_
for i, pct in enumerate(variance_explained, start=1):
    print(f"PC{i}: {pct:.1f}% of variance")
#> PC1: 39.7% of variance
#> PC2: 31.8% of variance
#> PC3: 14.3% of variance
#> PC4: 6.2% of variance

Non-linear embeddings

t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE) (Maaten and Hinton 2008) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) (McInnes, John, and James 2018; Becht et al. 2018) place points in two dimensions so that near neighbours in the original space stay near. They are the standard display for single-cell data, where thousands of cells make a PCA scatter plot unreadable, and they genuinely reveal structure that PCA’s linearity hides. They are also systematically misread.

Both methods preserve local neighbourhoods and deliberately discard global geometry. The consequences are specific. The distance between two clusters on a t-SNE plot carries no information — clusters that appear adjacent may be no more similar than clusters at opposite corners. Cluster sizes and densities are artefacts of the algorithm’s normalisation, not measurements of spread. The embedding depends strongly on the perplexity or neighbour-count parameter, and running the same data at two settings produces two different pictures, both correct in their own terms. Finally, t-SNE and UMAP will produce apparently clean groups from pure noise if the parameters invite it.

The defensible use is as a display of a clustering computed elsewhere: cluster in the PCA space, then colour the embedding by cluster label. Treating the embedding itself as the analysis is where the trouble starts. A seven-sample dataset like this one is far too small for either method, which is why neither is run here.

Clustering, and choosing k

Hierarchical clustering builds a tree by repeatedly joining the closest pair, and requires two choices: a distance (correlation distance is usual for expression profiles, because it ignores overall magnitude) and a linkage rule (average or Ward are the safe defaults). Cutting the tree at a height gives a partition. k-means instead places k centroids and iterates, and requires k in advance.

from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import fcluster
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

for k in (2, 3, 4):
    hierarchical = fcluster(linkage_matrix, k, criterion="maxclust")
    kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=20, random_state=0).fit_predict(expression_matrix)
    print(f"k={k}  silhouette={silhouette_score(expression_matrix, kmeans):.3f}  "
          f"hierarchical={hierarchical}  k-means={kmeans}")

The silhouette score compares each point’s distance to its own cluster with its distance to the nearest other cluster, averaged over points; higher is better. Here it is 0.241 at k = 2 and 0.239 at k = 3 — indistinguishable. The score cannot choose between them, and reporting the k with the highest silhouette would be reporting a difference of 0.002 as a finding.

A better question than “which k is best” is “which groupings are stable”. Resampling the genes and re-clustering shows which sample pairings survive.

12.8 Supervised learning on omics data

Supervised learning fits a function from features to known labels and is judged by its performance on data it has not seen. The discipline is simple to state: split the data into a training set used to fit parameters, a validation set used to choose between models and settings, and a test set opened once, at the end, to estimate performance. Cross-validation replaces the first two with repeated splits of the training data, which is essential when samples are few.

Scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al. 2011) expresses the whole pipeline as an object, and doing so is not a stylistic preference — it is the mechanism that prevents the error demonstrated below.

from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import LeaveOneOut, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline

X = log_cpm.T.values
y = (sample_info["condition"] == "treated").astype(int).values

correct_pipeline = Pipeline([
    ("select", SelectKBest(f_classif, k=50)),
    ("classify", LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000)),
])

accuracy = cross_val_score(correct_pipeline, X, y, cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()
print(f"leave-one-out accuracy, real labels: {accuracy:.3f}")
#> leave-one-out accuracy, real labels: 1.000

Leave-one-out cross-validation on the real labels gives perfect accuracy. With seven samples and a strong treatment effect that is believable but not informative: the question is whether the procedure would also report success when there is nothing to find.

Model settings — the regularisation strength, the number of genes retained, the tree depth — are chosen, not given, and choosing them is itself a use of the data. If you tune settings by cross-validation and then report the best cross-validated score as your performance estimate, that score is optimistic by however much the tuning exploited the folds. Nested cross-validation fixes this: an inner loop selects the settings and an outer loop estimates performance, so each outer fold’s estimate comes from a model whose settings never saw it. Scikit-learn expresses this by putting a GridSearchCV object inside a cross_val_score call, which is verbose but unambiguous.

Even a nested estimate describes performance on the cohort you have. The generalisation that matters clinically — to a different hospital, platform or population — is measured only by holding out an entire independent cohort. Models that look excellent within one dataset and fail on an external one are the norm rather than the exception in omics, because every cohort carries its own technical signature, and a model free to use it will.

Feature selection inside versus outside cross-validation

The error is to choose which genes to use by looking at all the data, and only then to cross-validate the classifier. The selection step has already seen the held-out sample’s label — indirectly, through the gene ranking — so the held-out sample is no longer held out. The resulting optimistic bias has been documented repeatedly in high-dimensional biology (Ambroise and McLachlan 2002; Simon et al. 2003). The test below settles it by destroying the signal: labels are randomly permuted, so no honest method can beat chance.

def leaky_accuracy(X, y, k=50):
    """Select features on ALL the data, then cross-validate. This is wrong."""
    X_selected = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=k).fit_transform(X, y)
    return cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000), X_selected,
                           y, cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()

def honest_accuracy(X, y, k=50):
    """Select features inside each fold, via a pipeline. This is right."""
    pipeline = Pipeline([("select", SelectKBest(f_classif, k=k)),
                         ("classify", LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000))])
    return cross_val_score(pipeline, X, y, cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()

rng_perm = np.random.default_rng(20250101)
leaky, honest = [], []
for _ in range(100):
    shuffled = rng_perm.permutation(y)
    leaky.append(leaky_accuracy(X, shuffled))
    honest.append(honest_accuracy(X, shuffled))

print(f"random labels, selection outside CV: mean accuracy {np.mean(leaky):.3f}")
#> random labels, selection outside CV: mean accuracy 1.000
print(f"random labels, selection inside CV:  mean accuracy {np.mean(honest):.3f}")
#> random labels, selection inside CV:  mean accuracy 0.384
print(f"leaky pipeline reported >= 85% accuracy in "
      f"{100 * np.mean(np.array(leaky) >= 0.85):.0f}% of 100 permutations")
#> leaky pipeline reported >= 85% accuracy in 100% of 100 permutations

Every preprocessing step that uses the labels, or that uses the full dataset’s distribution, belongs inside the cross-validation loop: feature selection, scaling, imputation, batch correction, oversampling. The permutation test is the general safeguard — shuffle the labels, run the entire pipeline unchanged, and check that performance collapses to chance. If it does not, the pipeline is leaking.

Leakage has many forms

Besides feature selection, the common routes are: normalising or scaling across training and test together; tuning hyperparameters on the test set and then reporting that set’s score; including replicate samples from the same individual in both training and test; and using a feature that encodes the outcome, such as a batch identifier that happens to track case status.

12.9 Evaluating a classifier

Accuracy is nearly useless in biology, because biological classes are rarely balanced. Pathogenic variants are a small fraction of all variants; responders are a minority of patients. The demonstration below uses a simulated two-class problem with 2% positives — simulated so that the true class balance is known exactly — and is the one place in this chapter where the data are not real.

from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import (precision_score, recall_score, roc_auc_score,
                             average_precision_score, roc_curve,
                             precision_recall_curve)

X_sim, y_sim = make_classification(n_samples=4000, n_features=40,
                                   n_informative=6, weights=[0.98, 0.02],
                                   random_state=20250101)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X_sim, y_sim, test_size=0.4, stratify=y_sim, random_state=1)

model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000).fit(X_train, y_train)
scores_test = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
predictions = model.predict(X_test)

      f"(always predicting negative: {1 - y_test.mean():.3f})")
#> accuracy: 0.980  (always predicting negative: 0.976)
print(f"precision: {precision_score(y_test, predictions):.3f}  "
      f"recall: {recall_score(y_test, predictions):.3f}")
#> precision: 0.706  recall: 0.308
print(f"ROC AUC: {roc_auc_score(y_test, scores_test):.3f}  "
      f"average precision: {average_precision_score(y_test, scores_test):.3f}")
#> ROC AUC: 0.858  average precision: 0.407

The model is 98.0% accurate. A model that predicts “negative” for every sample is 97.6% accurate. Accuracy has measured the class balance.

The informative quantities are precision, the fraction of positive predictions that are correct, and recall (sensitivity), the fraction of true positives that are found. Here precision is 0.706 and recall 0.308: when the model says positive it is usually right, but it misses nearly 70% of the positives. Which failure matters depends on the application — a screening test needs recall, a confirmatory test needs precision.

The ROC curve plots true positive rate against false positive rate across all decision thresholds, and the area under it (AUC) is the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative. Its weakness under imbalance is structural: the false positive rate has the large negative class in its denominator, so thousands of false positives barely move it. The precision–recall curve uses precision instead, which has the predicted positives in its denominator, and is therefore sensitive to exactly the failure that matters.

Two further habits. Report a confusion matrix, not a single number, so a reader can see which errors the model makes. 12.10 Deep learning in genomics

Deep learning is a family of models built from many layers of learned transformations, trained end to end by gradient descent (LeCun 2015). In genomics it has delivered genuinely and narrowly.

Where it has worked, three conditions hold: abundant labelled training data, a sequence-to-function mapping with local structure that convolutional or attention architectures capture naturally, and a well-posed target. Predicting regulatory activity, chromatin accessibility and expression from DNA sequence is the clearest success, with models that integrate information across long genomic distances (Avsec et al. 2021). Protein structure prediction is the other, and is discussed in the structural bioinformatics chapter. Variant effect prediction and base calling from raw signal are now routinely neural.

Where it has not worked is equally clear. On tabular omics data with a few hundred samples and tens of thousands of features, deep networks rarely beat regularised linear models or gradient-boosted trees, and the comparison is often not reported (Libbrecht and Noble 2015). Clinical outcome prediction from expression data has repeatedly produced models that fail on external cohorts. Interpretability remains genuinely hard: attribution methods indicate which inputs a model used, not which are causal, and a model can be accurate for reasons that do not generalise.

The practical advice is unglamorous. Establish a simple baseline — logistic regression with regularisation, or a random forest — and report it alongside any deep model. Hold out an entire cohort, not a random subset, because random splits of a single cohort overstate generalisation. And apply the same leakage discipline: a neural network trained on leaked features is simply a more expensive way of getting the wrong answer (Zou et al. 2018; Eraslan et al. 2019).

12.11 Summary

  • Biological replicates license claims about populations; technical replicates measure the instrument. No analysis converts one into the other.
  • An omics matrix has far more features than samples, which guarantees that some feature separates any two groups, makes distances unreliable, and forces external validation.
  • Raw counts are not comparable across libraries. Before normalisation the median log ratio between two control samples in this dataset was 1.443, the log ratio of their library sizes; after CPM it was 0.056.
  • Batch effects are reproducible and directional, so they imitate biology. In a deliberately confounded contrast, 22.3% of the significant genes were also significant in a pure library-protocol comparison, against 7.8% expected.
  • Multiple testing correction is not optional: 10,000 null tests produced 501 results at p < 0.05 and none after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment.
  • PCA on these samples put treatment on PC1 (39.7%) and library protocol on PC2 (31.8%). Always colour the PCA by every recorded technical factor.
  • t-SNE and UMAP preserve local neighbourhoods only; distances between clusters, cluster sizes and apparent gaps carry no reliable information.
  • A stable cluster is not necessarily a biological one — the stable third cluster here was the single-read library.
  • Selecting features outside the cross-validation loop reported perfect accuracy on randomly permuted labels in 100 of 100 permutations. Put every label-aware step inside the loop, and permute the labels to check.
  • Accuracy measures class balance when classes are imbalanced. Report precision, recall and the precision–recall curve instead.

A count matrix is not a result. Between the matrix and a defensible statement
about biology sit a dozen decisions — which genes to keep, how to make libraries
comparable, what to do about the fact that samples were prepared in two different
ways, how to correct thousands of simultaneous tests, whether an apparent cluster
is real, and whether a classifier that reports 100% accuracy has learned anything
at all. Each decision can turn a real effect into a null result or, more often,
turn noise into a publishable figure.

This chapter works through those decisions on the bundled *Drosophila* RNA-seq
dataset in `data/expression/pasilla_gene_counts.tsv`: 14,599 genes measured in
seven samples, three in which the gene *pasilla* was knocked down by RNA
interference and four untreated controls, with the sample metadata in
`data/expression/pasilla_sample_annotation.csv`, from the study that first
reported it [@brooks2011]. The dataset is small enough to hold in your head and
real enough to misbehave. In particular, its libraries were built two different
ways — three single-read, four paired-end — and that
difference is almost as large a source of variation as the treatment itself,
which makes it an unusually honest teaching set.

The single most valuable result in this chapter is in the section on supervised
learning. Selecting informative genes before cross-validation rather than inside
it produces a classifier that reports perfect accuracy on data whose labels have
been randomly shuffled. You will run that demonstration and see the number.

## Designing an experiment that can answer the question {#sec-12-design}

No amount of analysis repairs a design that cannot answer the question, and the
most common failure is not a shortage of data but the wrong kind of replication.

A **technical replicate** repeats the measurement on the same biological material:
the same RNA extract sequenced twice, the same library loaded on two lanes. It
estimates the variability of the measurement process. A **biological replicate**
repeats the whole experiment on independent material: a different fly culture, a
different patient, a different mouse. It estimates the variability of the
biological population, which is what almost every scientific claim is about.

Technical replicates cannot substitute for biological ones. Sequencing one RNA
sample across twenty lanes gives twenty highly concordant measurements of that
one sample, a reassuringly small standard error, and no information whatsoever
about whether another fly would have behaved the same way. The resulting
*p*-value answers a question nobody asked. The bundled metadata records that some
samples were run across five or six lanes; those lanes were summed into one count
column, which is the correct treatment, because they were never independent
observations of biology.

```{python}
#| label: load-metadata
sample_info = pd.read_csv(book_data("expression", "pasilla_sample_annotation.csv"))
sample_info["sample"] = sample_info["file"].str.replace("fb", "", regex=False)
sample_info = sample_info.set_index("sample")

print(sample_info[["condition", "type", "number of lanes"]])
```

Three further design points decide whether the analysis below is worth running.

**Replicate number.** Three per group is the practical floor for RNA-seq and
detects only large effects; the pasilla design has three treated and four
untreated. With fewer than three, variance cannot be estimated per gene at all,
which is why methods such as DESeq2 [@love2014] and edgeR [@robinson2010] share
information across genes to stabilise the estimate.

**Randomisation.** Assign samples to processing batches, lanes and extraction
days at random. Processing all controls on Monday and all treated samples on
Tuesday guarantees that a day effect is indistinguishable from a treatment
effect.

**Blocking.** When a nuisance factor cannot be avoided — two sequencing runs, two
operators — ensure every level of the factor contains both conditions, and record
it. A recorded nuisance factor can be modelled; an unrecorded one cannot.

How many replicates are enough is a question of effect size, variability and the
smallest difference worth detecting, and it has no universal answer. The useful
discipline is to decide, before the experiment, what difference would change what
you do next — a twofold change in a handful of genes, or a subtle shift across a
pathway — and to ask whether the planned design could detect it. For RNA-seq the
rough guidance from published power studies is that three replicates per group
detect twofold changes in well-expressed genes, six begin to reach 1.5-fold, and
detecting small coordinated shifts needs tens. A study powered for the wrong
effect size produces a null result that cannot be distinguished from an absent
effect, which is the most expensive outcome in the catalogue.

::: {.callout-warning title="Confounding is a property of the design, not the data"}
If every treated sample was prepared with one protocol and every control with
another, no statistical method can separate protocol from treatment. Batch
correction, covariate adjustment and sophisticated models all fail identically,
because the information is absent. The only fix is a different experiment.
:::

## The shape of an omics matrix {#sec-12-tidy-data}

**Tidy data** has one row per observation and one column per variable
[@wickham2019]. An omics count matrix deliberately breaks that rule: it is stored
with one row per gene and one column per sample, because the matrix form is what
linear algebra and most statistical software expect. The tidy form is useful for
plotting and joining, the matrix form for computation, and a working analysis
moves between them; pandas supplies both representations and the operations that
convert one into the other [@mckinney2010].

```{python}
#| label: load-counts
counts = pd.read_csv(book_data("expression", "pasilla_gene_counts.tsv"),
                     sep="\t", index_col=0)
counts = counts[sample_info.index]          # fix column order to match metadata

print(f"{counts.shape[0]:,} genes x {counts.shape[1]} samples")
print(counts.iloc[:3, :4])
```

The shape is the problem. There are 14,599 features and seven samples, so
*p* ≫ *n*: vastly more variables than observations. Three consequences follow,
and every statistical trap later in the chapter is one of them in disguise.

First, any two groups of samples can be separated perfectly by some subset of
features, even when the labels are random. With 14,599 genes and seven samples
there is always a gene that happens to split them. Second, distances become
uninformative: in high dimensions the distance from a point to its nearest
neighbour approaches the distance to its farthest, so "these samples cluster
together" needs evidence beyond a plot. Third, the number of possible models
vastly exceeds the data available to choose between them, so model selection
needs external validation rather than internal fit.

Low-count genes contribute noise without information, and removing them before
anything else reduces the testing burden.

```{python}
#| label: filter-genes
expressed = counts[(counts > 5).sum(axis=1) >= 3]

print(f"genes with no counts at all: {(counts.sum(axis=1) == 0).sum():,}")
print(f"genes retained (>5 counts in >=3 samples): {expressed.shape[0]:,}")
```

The filter is a judgement, not a rule. Requiring counts above five in at least
three samples — the size of the smallest group — keeps genes that could show a
group-specific signal and discards those that cannot. State the rule in your
methods, because it changes how many tests you perform and therefore every
corrected *p*-value.

## Exploring and normalising count data {#sec-12-normalisation}

RNA-seq counts are not normally distributed. They are non-negative integers,
strongly right-skewed, and their variance grows with their mean — a gene averaging
10,000 reads varies far more in absolute terms than one averaging 10.
@fig-12-counts shows both facts alongside the library sizes that make raw counts
incomparable.

```{python}

Centre: the distribution of log2 counts per million is approximately symmetric once the log transform is applied, unlike the raw counts. Right: per-gene variance rises with the mean on logarithmic axes, the overdispersion that count-based models exist to handle."
#| fig-width: 9

library_sizes = expressed.sum() / 1e6
cpm = expressed / expressed.sum() * 1e6
log_cpm = np.log2(cpm + 1)

axes[0].set_xticks(range(len(library_sizes)))

fig.tight_layout()
```

Three features of the left and centre panels deserve attention before any
modelling. Counts span roughly five orders of magnitude within a single sample,
so an untransformed matrix is dominated by a few hundred genes; the log transform
in the centre panel is what makes the rest visible. Zeros are abundant — 2,240 of
the 14,599 genes have no counts in any sample — and a zero is ambiguous, meaning
either that a transcript is absent or that sequencing was too shallow to see it,
which is why a pseudocount is added before taking logarithms rather than
discarding the zeros. And the right-hand panel shows variance rising faster than
the mean, the overdispersion that makes the Poisson distribution inadequate and
the negative binomial the standard choice for count models.

Library sizes range from 8.4 to 21.9 million counts. A gene with 100 reads in the
smallest library is twice as abundant, proportionally, as a gene with 100 reads
in the largest. **Normalisation** removes that difference. The simplest method,
counts per million (CPM), divides each count by its library total; it is what the
code above computes. The demonstration below shows what it fixes.

```{python}
#| label: normalisation-demo
pair = ["untreated2", "untreated3"]
gene = "FBgn0000008"

print("raw counts:", counts.loc[gene, pair].tolist(),
      "ratio", round(counts.loc[gene, pair[0]] / counts.loc[gene, pair[1]], 2))
print("CPM:", cpm.loc[gene, pair].round(2).tolist(),
      "ratio", round(cpm.loc[gene, pair[0]] / cpm.loc[gene, pair[1]], 2))

raw_ratio = np.log2(expressed[pair[0]] + 1) - np.log2(expressed[pair[1]] + 1)
cpm_ratio = log_cpm[pair[0]] - log_cpm[pair[1]]
print(f"median log2 ratio across all genes - raw: {raw_ratio.median():.3f}, "
      f"CPM: {cpm_ratio.median():.3f}")
```

The gene looks 2.12-fold up in `untreated2` on raw counts and 0.81-fold — that is,
slightly down — after normalisation. The direction of the effect reverses. The
summary line explains why: before normalisation the median log ratio across all
8,621 genes is 1.443, almost exactly the log ratio of the two library sizes,
because most genes do not change and the apparent shift is entirely a depth
artefact. After CPM the median is 0.056, close to the zero that a comparison of
two control samples should produce.

::: {.callout-note title="CPM is the simplest method, not the best one"}
CPM assumes total counts are comparable, which fails when a few very highly
expressed genes dominate one sample — common in tissue with a dominant
transcript. The median-of-ratios method in DESeq2 [@love2014] and trimmed mean of
M-values in edgeR [@robinson2010] estimate a size factor from the bulk of genes
instead, and are the defaults for differential expression. Transcript-length
normalisation (TPM, FPKM) additionally divides by gene length, which is necessary
when comparing different genes but not when comparing one gene across samples.
:::

## Batch effects and confounding {#sec-12-batch}

A **batch effect** is systematic variation introduced by how samples were
processed rather than by what they are: reagent lot, sequencing run, extraction
day, library protocol [@leek2010]. Batch effects are not noise. They are
reproducible, often large, and they bias in a consistent direction — which is
exactly what makes them look like biology.

The pasilla design contains a real one. Four samples were sequenced paired-end
and three single-read, and that split cuts across both conditions. Because it
cuts across, the effect can be measured directly: compare untreated samples with
one library type against untreated samples with the other, where no treatment
difference exists by construction.

```{python}
#| label: batch-contrast
def two_group_p(group_a, group_b):
    """Per-gene two-sample t-test on log-CPM; returns a Series of p-values."""
    stat, p = stats.ttest_ind(log_cpm[group_a], log_cpm[group_b], axis=1)
    return pd.Series(p, index=log_cpm.index)

batch_only = two_group_p(["untreated1", "untreated2"],    # single-read
                         ["untreated3", "untreated4"])    # paired-end

n_genes = log_cpm.shape[0]
print(f"genes tested: {n_genes:,}; expected at p < 0.05 by chance: {0.05 * n_genes:.0f}")
print(f"library-type contrast, untreated only: {(batch_only < 0.05).sum():,} genes at p < 0.05")
```

Within a single biological condition, 676 genes reach *p* < 0.05 against an
expectation of 431. The library protocol changes measured expression for hundreds
of genes.

Now construct the failure. Take two paired-end treated samples and two
single-read untreated samples. In that four-sample subset, condition and library
type are perfectly confounded — exactly the design produced by processing cases
and controls separately.

```{python}
#| label: confounded-contrast
confounded = two_group_p(["treated2", "treated3"],        # both paired-end
                         ["untreated1", "untreated2"])    # both single-read
proper = two_group_p(["treated1", "treated2", "treated3"],
                     ["untreated1", "untreated2", "untreated3", "untreated4"])

batch_hits = set(batch_only[batch_only < 0.05].index)
conf_hits = set(confounded[confounded < 0.05].index)
proper_hits = set(proper[proper < 0.05].index)

print(f"confounded contrast: {len(conf_hits):,} genes at p < 0.05")
print(f"  of these, also significant in the pure library-type contrast: "
      f"{len(conf_hits & batch_hits):,} "
      f"({100 * len(conf_hits & batch_hits) / len(conf_hits):.1f}%)")
print(f"  expected by chance: {100 * len(batch_hits) / n_genes:.1f}%")
print(f"  also found by the properly balanced contrast: "
      f"{100 * len(conf_hits & proper_hits) / len(conf_hits):.1f}%")
```

The confounded comparison returns 1,533 genes. Of those, 22.3% also appear in the
pure library-type contrast, against 7.8% expected by chance — a nearly threefold
enrichment for genes known to respond to protocol rather than treatment. Fewer
than half of the 1,533 are recovered by the balanced comparison using all seven
samples. Written up without the metadata, this list would read as a treatment
signature; it is in substantial part a protocol signature, and nothing in the
numbers themselves distinguishes the two.

The remedies, in order of effectiveness: design the experiment so batch is
balanced across conditions; record batch so it can be included as a covariate in
the model; or, if neither was done, apply an empirical Bayes correction such as
ComBat [@johnson2007] and report it as a limitation. Correction after the fact
removes variation that may contain signal, and it can only ever approximate what
balanced design gives for free.

## Multiple testing and the false discovery problem {#sec-12-multiple-testing}

Testing 8,621 genes at a 5% threshold produces about 431 significant results when
nothing is happening. The simulation below makes that concrete, then shows what
the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure does about it [@benjamini1995].

```{python}
#| label: fdr-simulation
rng = np.random.default_rng(20250101)

def benjamini_hochberg(p_values):
    """Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values, written out rather than imported."""
    p = np.asarray(p_values)
    m = len(p)
    order = np.argsort(p)
    ranked = p[order] * m / np.arange(1, m + 1)
    adjusted = np.minimum.accumulate(ranked[::-1])[::-1]
    out = np.empty(m)
    out[order] = np.minimum(adjusted, 1)
    return out

null_data = rng.normal(size=(10_000, 20))      # no difference between groups
_, p_null = stats.ttest_ind(null_data[:, :10], null_data[:, 10:], axis=1)

print(f"pure noise: {(p_null < 0.05).sum()} of 10,000 'genes' at p < 0.05")
print(f"pure noise: {(benjamini_hochberg(p_null) < 0.05).sum()} survive BH correction")
```

501 false positives before correction, none after. Now add real signal: 200 of
the 10,000 genes are given a genuine difference between groups.

```{python}
#| label: fdr-with-signal
mixed = rng.normal(size=(10_000, 20))
mixed[:200, 10:] += 2.0                        # the first 200 genes truly differ
_, p_mixed = stats.ttest_ind(mixed[:, :10], mixed[:, 10:], axis=1)

significant = benjamini_hochberg(p_mixed) < 0.05
true_positives = significant[:200].sum()
false_positives = significant[200:].sum()

print(f"raw p < 0.05: {(p_mixed < 0.05).sum()}")
print(f"BH significant: {significant.sum()}  "
      f"(true {true_positives}, false {false_positives})")
print(f"observed false discovery proportion: "
      f"{false_positives / significant.sum():.3f}")
print(f"power to detect the 200 real effects: {true_positives / 200:.2f}")
```

The procedure delivers what it promises: 133 discoveries of which seven are
false, a false discovery proportion of 0.053 against the nominal 0.05. It also
shows the cost — only 126 of the 200 real effects are found, because controlling
false positives always sacrifices power. The **false discovery rate** is the
expected proportion of false positives among the results you call significant,
which is the quantity that matters when the output is a list of candidates for
follow-up. The family-wise error rate controlled by Bonferroni is the probability
of even one false positive, which is the right target only when a single wrong
call invalidates the study. Storey's *q*-value refines the FDR estimate by
estimating the proportion of true nulls rather than assuming it is one
[@storey2003].

One legitimate way to recover power is **independent filtering**: removing tests
that have little chance of being significant, using a criterion independent of
the test statistic under the null. Mean expression is such a criterion, which is
why the low-count filter applied earlier is not merely tidying — it reduces the
number of tests, and therefore the correction burden, without biasing the
remaining *p*-values. Filtering on something correlated with the test statistic,
such as fold change, does bias them, and is not independent filtering at all.

::: {.callout-warning title="Correct for every test you performed"}
The correction must cover all tests actually conducted, including the ones not
reported. Running the analysis with three filtering thresholds and reporting the
one with most hits is multiple testing with an unrecorded denominator, and no
correction applied afterwards can repair it.
:::

## Finding structure without labels {#sec-12-unsupervised}

**Principal component analysis (PCA)** re-expresses samples in new coordinates —
principal components — that are uncorrelated linear combinations of the original
features, ordered so that the first captures the most variance. It is the first
diagnostic to run on any omics matrix, because the leading components usually
reveal whichever factor dominates the experiment, and that factor is often not
the one you intended to study.

Three choices control what PCA shows. Transform count data first, or the result
will be dominated by a handful of highly expressed genes. Centre the features,
always. Whether to scale them to unit variance depends: on log-CPM data, scaling
gives low-expression noisy genes the same weight as informative ones, so centring
without scaling is the usual choice for RNA-seq.

```{python}
#| label: pca
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

expression_matrix = log_cpm.T.values            # samples as rows, genes as columns
centred = expression_matrix - expression_matrix.mean(axis=0)

pca = PCA(n_components=4).fit(centred)
scores = pca.transform(centred)

variance_explained = 100 * pca.explained_variance_ratio_
for i, pct in enumerate(variance_explained, start=1):
    print(f"PC{i}: {pct:.1f}% of variance")
```

PC1 accounts for 39.7% of the variance and PC2 for 31.8%. Two components of
nearly equal size is a warning in itself: it means two comparably strong factors
are at work. @fig-12-structure identifies them.

    ax.set_xlabel(f"PC1 ({variance_explained[0]:.0f}%)")
    ax.set_ylabel(f"PC2 ({variance_explained[1]:.0f}%)")
    ax.set_title(title); ax.legend(fontsize=7)

linkage_matrix = linkage(pdist(expression_matrix, metric="correlation"),
                         method="average")
dendrogram(linkage_matrix, labels=list(log_cpm.columns), ax=axes[2],
           leaf_rotation=90, leaf_font_size=7, color_threshold=0)
axes[2].set_ylabel("Correlation distance")
axes[2].set_title("Hierarchical clustering")
axes[2].grid(False)

fig.tight_layout()
```

The left panel shows a clean separation of treated from untreated along PC1. The
centre panel shows the problem: PC2, carrying almost as much variance, separates
single-read from paired-end libraries. Had the design been confounded, these two
axes would have collapsed into one and the figure would have looked like an
unambiguous treatment effect.

Loadings say which genes drive a component. Each loading is the weight of one
gene in the linear combination, and the extreme ones are the genes that define
the axis.

#| label: loadings
pc1_loadings = pd.Series(pca.components_[0], index=log_cpm.index)
print("strongest positive PC1 loadings:")
print(pc1_loadings.nlargest(3).round(4).to_string())
print("strongest negative PC1 loadings:")
print(pc1_loadings.nsmallest(3).round(4).to_string())
```

### Non-linear embeddings

**t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE)** [@maaten2008] and
**uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP)**
[@mcinnes2018; @becht2019] place
points in two dimensions so that near neighbours in the original space stay near.
They are the standard display for single-cell data, where thousands of cells make
a PCA scatter plot unreadable, and they genuinely reveal structure that PCA's
linearity hides. They are also systematically misread.

Both methods preserve local neighbourhoods and deliberately discard global
geometry. The consequences are specific. The distance between two clusters on a
t-SNE plot carries no information — clusters that appear adjacent may be no more
similar than clusters at opposite corners. Cluster sizes and densities are
artefacts of the algorithm's normalisation, not measurements of spread. The
embedding depends strongly on the perplexity or neighbour-count parameter, and
running the same data at two settings produces two different pictures, both
correct in their own terms. Finally, t-SNE and UMAP will produce apparently clean
groups from pure noise if the parameters invite it.

The defensible use is as a display of a clustering computed elsewhere: cluster in
the PCA space, then colour the embedding by cluster label. Treating the embedding
itself as the analysis is where the trouble starts. A seven-sample dataset like
this one is far too small for either method, which is why neither is run here.

### Clustering, and choosing k

Hierarchical clustering builds a tree by repeatedly joining the closest pair, and
requires two choices: a distance (correlation distance is usual for expression
profiles, because it ignores overall magnitude) and a linkage rule (average or
Ward are the safe defaults). Cutting the tree at a height gives a partition.
*k*-means instead places *k* centroids and iterates, and requires *k* in advance.

```{python}
#| label: clustering
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import fcluster
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

for k in (2, 3, 4):
    hierarchical = fcluster(linkage_matrix, k, criterion="maxclust")
    kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=20, random_state=0).fit_predict(expression_matrix)
    print(f"k={k}  silhouette={silhouette_score(expression_matrix, kmeans):.3f}  "
          f"hierarchical={hierarchical}  k-means={kmeans}")
```

The **silhouette score** compares each point's distance to its own cluster with
its distance to the nearest other cluster, averaged over points; higher is
better. Here it is 0.241 at *k* = 2 and 0.239 at *k* = 3 — indistinguishable. The
score cannot choose between them, and reporting the *k* with the highest
silhouette would be reporting a difference of 0.002 as a finding.

A better question than "which *k* is best" is "which groupings are stable".
Resampling the genes and re-clustering shows which sample pairings survive.

```{python}
#| label: cluster-stability
rng_boot = np.random.default_rng(20250101)
n_resamples, n_features = 200, expression_matrix.shape[1]
co_cluster = np.zeros((7, 7))

for _ in range(n_resamples):
    picked = rng_boot.integers(0, n_features, n_features)   # bootstrap the genes
    labels = fcluster(linkage(pdist(expression_matrix[:, picked], metric="correlation"),
                              method="average"), 3, criterion="maxclust")
    co_cluster += labels[:, None] == labels[None, :]

co_cluster /= n_resamples
print(pd.DataFrame(np.round(co_cluster, 2),
                   index=log_cpm.columns, columns=log_cpm.columns).to_string())
```

At *k* = 3 the four untreated samples co-cluster in at least 99% of resamples,
`treated2` and `treated3` always cluster together, and `treated1` joins them in
only 1% of resamples. The three-group structure is stable. But `treated1` is the
single-read treated sample, so the third cluster is a library-protocol cluster,
not a biological one. Stability establishes that a partition is reproducible from
the data; it says nothing about what the partition means.

## Supervised learning on omics data {#sec-12-supervised}

Supervised learning fits a function from features to known labels and is judged
by its performance on data it has not seen. The discipline is simple to state:
split the data into a **training set** used to fit parameters, a **validation
set** used to choose between models and settings, and a **test set** opened once,
at the end, to estimate performance. **Cross-validation** replaces the first two
with repeated splits of the training data, which is essential when samples are
few.

Scikit-learn [@pedregosa2011] expresses the whole pipeline as an object, and
doing so is not a stylistic preference — it is the mechanism that prevents the
error demonstrated below.

```{python}
#| label: classifier
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import LeaveOneOut, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline

X = log_cpm.T.values
y = (sample_info["condition"] == "treated").astype(int).values

correct_pipeline = Pipeline([
    ("select", SelectKBest(f_classif, k=50)),
    ("classify", LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000)),
])

accuracy = cross_val_score(correct_pipeline, X, y, cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()
print(f"leave-one-out accuracy, real labels: {accuracy:.3f}")
```

Leave-one-out cross-validation on the real labels gives perfect accuracy. With
seven samples and a strong treatment effect that is believable but not
informative: the question is whether the procedure would also report success when
there is nothing to find.

Model settings — the regularisation strength, the number of genes retained, the
tree depth — are chosen, not given, and choosing them is itself a use of the
data. If you tune settings by cross-validation and then report the best
cross-validated score as your performance estimate, that score is optimistic by
however much the tuning exploited the folds. **Nested cross-validation** fixes
this: an inner loop selects the settings and an outer loop estimates performance,
so each outer fold's estimate comes from a model whose settings never saw it.
Scikit-learn expresses this by putting a `GridSearchCV` object inside a
`cross_val_score` call, which is verbose but unambiguous.

Even a nested estimate describes performance on the cohort you have. The
generalisation that matters clinically — to a different hospital, platform or
population — is measured only by holding out an entire independent cohort.
Models that look excellent within one dataset and fail on an external one are the
norm rather than the exception in omics, because every cohort carries its own
technical signature, and a model free to use it will.

### Feature selection inside versus outside cross-validation

The error is to choose which genes to use by looking at all the data, and only
then to cross-validate the classifier. The selection step has already seen the
held-out sample's label — indirectly, through the gene ranking — so the
held-out sample is no longer held out. The resulting optimistic bias has been
documented repeatedly in high-dimensional biology [@ambroise2002; @simon2003].
The test below settles it by destroying the signal: labels are randomly
permuted, so no honest method can beat chance.

```{python}
#| label: leakage-experiment
def leaky_accuracy(X, y, k=50):
    """Select features on ALL the data, then cross-validate. This is wrong."""
    X_selected = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=k).fit_transform(X, y)
    return cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000), X_selected,
                           y, cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()

def honest_accuracy(X, y, k=50):
    """Select features inside each fold, via a pipeline. This is right."""
    pipeline = Pipeline([("select", SelectKBest(f_classif, k=k)),
                         ("classify", LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000))])
    return cross_val_score(pipeline, X, y, cv=LeaveOneOut()).mean()

rng_perm = np.random.default_rng(20250101)
leaky, honest = [], []
for _ in range(100):
    shuffled = rng_perm.permutation(y)
    leaky.append(leaky_accuracy(X, shuffled))
    honest.append(honest_accuracy(X, shuffled))

print(f"random labels, selection outside CV: mean accuracy {np.mean(leaky):.3f}")
print(f"random labels, selection inside CV:  mean accuracy {np.mean(honest):.3f}")
print(f"leaky pipeline reported >= 85% accuracy in "
      f"{100 * np.mean(np.array(leaky) >= 0.85):.0f}% of 100 permutations")
```

With labels that carry no information at all, the leaky pipeline reports a mean
accuracy of 1.000 and exceeded 85% in every one of 100 permutations. The honest
pipeline reports 0.384, below the 0.571 that always guessing the majority class
would achieve — the usual behaviour of leave-one-out cross-validation on tiny
balanced-ish datasets, and the correct answer, because there is nothing to learn.
@fig-12-leakage shows the two distributions.

```{python}
#| label: fig-12-leakage
#| fig-cap: "Leave-one-out accuracy over 100 random label permutations, in which no real signal exists. Selecting the 50 most discriminating genes before cross-validation (upper) reports perfect accuracy every time, because the selection step saw the held-out labels. Selecting inside each fold (lower) reports accuracies scattered around and below chance, which is the truthful answer. The dashed line marks the majority-class baseline of 0.571."
#| fig-width: 7
#| fig-height: 3.4
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.4))
bins = np.linspace(-0.05, 1.05, 23)
ax.hist(leaky, bins=bins, color=PALETTE[3], alpha=0.85,
        label="selection outside CV (leaky)")
ax.hist(honest, bins=bins, color=PALETTE[0], alpha=0.85,
        label="selection inside CV (correct)")
ax.axvline(0.571, linestyle="--", color="grey", linewidth=1)
ax.set_xlabel("Leave-one-out accuracy under permuted labels")
ax.set_ylabel("Permutations")
ax.legend(fontsize=8)
fig.tight_layout()
```

Every preprocessing step that uses the labels, or that uses the full dataset's
distribution, belongs inside the cross-validation loop: feature selection,
scaling, imputation, batch correction, oversampling. The permutation test is the
general safeguard — shuffle the labels, run the entire pipeline unchanged, and
check that performance collapses to chance. If it does not, the pipeline is
leaking.

::: {.callout-warning title="Leakage has many forms"}
Besides feature selection, the common routes are: normalising or scaling across
training and test together; tuning hyperparameters on the test set and then
reporting that set's score; including replicate samples from the same individual
in both training and test; and using a feature that encodes the outcome, such as
a batch identifier that happens to track case status.
:::

## Evaluating a classifier {#sec-12-evaluation}

Accuracy is nearly useless in biology, because biological classes are rarely
balanced. Pathogenic variants are a small fraction of all variants; responders
are a minority of patients. The demonstration below uses a simulated
two-class problem with 2% positives — simulated so that the true class balance is
known exactly — and is the one place in this chapter where the data are not real.

```{python}
#| label: imbalance
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import (precision_score, recall_score, roc_auc_score,
                             average_precision_score, roc_curve,
                             precision_recall_curve)

X_sim, y_sim = make_classification(n_samples=4000, n_features=40,
                                   n_informative=6, weights=[0.98, 0.02],
                                   random_state=20250101)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X_sim, y_sim, test_size=0.4, stratify=y_sim, random_state=1)

model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000).fit(X_train, y_train)
scores_test = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
predictions = model.predict(X_test)

print(f"positives in test set: {y_test.sum()} of {len(y_test)}")
print(f"accuracy: {model.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}  "
      f"(always predicting negative: {1 - y_test.mean():.3f})")
print(f"precision: {precision_score(y_test, predictions):.3f}  "
      f"recall: {recall_score(y_test, predictions):.3f}")
print(f"ROC AUC: {roc_auc_score(y_test, scores_test):.3f}  "
      f"average precision: {average_precision_score(y_test, scores_test):.3f}")
```

The model is 98.0% accurate. A model that predicts "negative" for every sample is
97.6% accurate. Accuracy has measured the class balance.

The informative quantities are **precision**, the fraction of positive
predictions that are correct, and **recall** (sensitivity), the fraction of true
positives that are found. Here precision is 0.706 and recall 0.308: when the
model says positive it is usually right, but it misses nearly 70% of the
positives. Which failure matters depends on the application — a screening test
needs recall, a confirmatory test needs precision.

The **ROC curve** plots true positive rate against false positive rate across all
decision thresholds, and the area under it (AUC) is the probability that a random
positive scores above a random negative. Its weakness under imbalance is
structural: the false positive rate has the large negative class in its
denominator, so thousands of false positives barely move it. The
**precision–recall curve** uses precision instead, which has the predicted
positives in its denominator, and is therefore sensitive to exactly the failure
that matters.

```{python}
#| label: fig-12-evaluation
#| fig-cap: "The same predictions on a test set with 2.4% positives, shown two ways. Left: the ROC curve looks respectable, with an area of 0.858, because the false positive rate is diluted by 1,561 true negatives. Right: the precision-recall curve shows that precision falls away quickly as recall rises, with an average precision of 0.407 against a no-skill baseline of 0.024 (dashed). The right-hand panel is the honest summary for a rare-class problem."
#| fig-width: 8
#| fig-height: 3.4

precision_curve, recall_curve, _ = precision_recall_curve(y_test, scores_test)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 3.4))

axes[0].plot(false_pos_rate, true_pos_rate, color=PALETTE[0], linewidth=1.6)
axes[0].plot([0, 1], [0, 1], "--", color="grey", linewidth=1)
axes[0].set_xlabel("False positive rate"); axes[0].set_ylabel("True positive rate")
axes[0].set_title(f"ROC (AUC = {roc_auc_score(y_test, scores_test):.3f})")

axes[1].plot(recall_curve, precision_curve, color=PALETTE[3], linewidth=1.6)
axes[1].axhline(y_test.mean(), linestyle="--", color="grey", linewidth=1)
axes[1].set_xlabel("Recall"); axes[1].set_ylabel("Precision")
axes[1].set_title(f"Precision-recall (AP = "
                  f"{average_precision_score(y_test, scores_test):.3f})")

fig.tight_layout()
```

Two further habits. Report a confusion matrix, not a single number, so a reader
can see which errors the model makes. And when classes are imbalanced, say what
you did about it: class weighting, threshold adjustment or resampling are all
defensible, but resampling must happen inside the cross-validation loop, for the
reason established in the previous section.

## Deep learning in genomics {#sec-12-deep-learning}

Deep learning is a family of models built from many layers of learned
transformations, trained end to end by gradient descent [@lecun2015]. In genomics
it has delivered genuinely and narrowly.

Where it has worked, three conditions hold: abundant labelled training data, a
sequence-to-function mapping with local structure that convolutional or attention
architectures capture naturally, and a well-posed target. Predicting regulatory
activity, chromatin accessibility and expression from DNA sequence is the clearest
success, with models that integrate information across long genomic distances
[@avsec2021]. Protein structure prediction is the other, and is discussed in the
structural bioinformatics chapter. Variant effect prediction and base calling
from raw signal are now routinely neural.

Where it has not worked is equally clear. On tabular omics data with a few
hundred samples and tens of thousands of features, deep networks rarely beat
regularised linear models or gradient-boosted trees, and the comparison is often
not reported [@libbrecht2015]. Clinical outcome prediction from expression data
has repeatedly produced models that fail on external cohorts. Interpretability
remains genuinely hard: attribution methods indicate which inputs a model used,
not which are causal, and a model can be accurate for reasons that do not
generalise.

The practical advice is unglamorous. Establish a simple baseline — logistic
regression with regularisation, or a random forest — and report it alongside any
deep model. Hold out an entire cohort, not a random subset, because random splits
of a single cohort overstate generalisation. And apply the same leakage
discipline: a neural network trained on leaked features is simply a more
expensive way of getting the wrong answer [@zou2019; @eraslan2019].

## Summary {#sec-12-summary}

- Biological replicates license claims about populations; technical replicates
  measure the instrument. No analysis converts one into the other.
- An omics matrix has far more features than samples, which guarantees that some
  feature separates any two groups, makes distances unreliable, and forces
  external validation.
- Raw counts are not comparable across libraries. Before normalisation the median
  log ratio between two control samples in this dataset was 1.443, the log ratio
  of their library sizes; after CPM it was 0.056.
- Batch effects are reproducible and directional, so they imitate biology. In a
  deliberately confounded contrast, 22.3% of the significant genes were also
  significant in a pure library-protocol comparison, against 7.8% expected.
- Multiple testing correction is not optional: 10,000 null tests produced 501
  results at *p* < 0.05 and none after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment.
- PCA on these samples put treatment on PC1 (39.7%) and library protocol on PC2
  (31.8%). Always colour the PCA by every recorded technical factor.
- t-SNE and UMAP preserve local neighbourhoods only; distances between clusters,
  cluster sizes and apparent gaps carry no reliable information.
- A stable cluster is not necessarily a biological one — the stable third cluster
  here was the single-read library.
- Selecting features outside the cross-validation loop reported perfect accuracy
  on randomly permuted labels in 100 of 100 permutations. Put every label-aware
  step inside the loop, and permute the labels to check.
- Accuracy measures class balance when classes are imbalanced. Report precision,
  recall and the precision–recall curve instead.

## Solutions {#sec-12-solutions}

::: {.callout-caution collapse="true" title="Solutions"}

**1.** A stricter filter keeps fewer genes and so reduces the multiple-testing
burden proportionally.

```{python}
#| label: soln-12-1
strict = counts[(counts > 10).sum(axis=1) >= 4]
print(f"retained: {strict.shape[0]:,} (was {expressed.shape[0]:,})")
print(f"expected false positives at p<0.05: {0.05 * strict.shape[0]:.0f} "
      f"(was {0.05 * expressed.shape[0]:.0f})")
```

**2.** The extremes are the deepest and shallowest libraries, differing 2.6-fold.

```{python}
#| label: soln-12-2
sizes = counts.sum() / 1e6
deepest, shallowest = sizes.idxmax(), sizes.idxmin()
print(f"{deepest}: {sizes.max():.2f}M   {shallowest}: {sizes.min():.2f}M   "
      f"ratio {sizes.max() / sizes.min():.2f}")
cpm_all = counts / counts.sum() * 1e6
print("raw ratio:", round(counts.loc["FBgn0000008", deepest] /
                          counts.loc["FBgn0000008", shallowest], 2),
      " CPM ratio:", round(cpm_all.loc["FBgn0000008", deepest] /
                           cpm_all.loc["FBgn0000008", shallowest], 2))
```

**3.** Scaling gives every gene equal weight, including low-expression genes whose
variance is mostly Poisson noise, so the leading components explain less and the
structure is diluted.

```{python}
#| label: soln-12-3
scaled = centred / centred.std(axis=0, ddof=1)
scaled_pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit(scaled)
print("scaled:   ", (100 * scaled_pca.explained_variance_ratio_).round(1))
print("unscaled: ", variance_explained[:2].round(1))
```

**4.** The number of hits barely changes — 1,539 against 1,533 — because a two
versus two comparison has the same meagre power either way. What changes is their
composition: only 12.1% of the library-type-matched hits appear in the pure
protocol list, against 22.3% for the confounded contrast. Counting significant
genes tells you almost nothing about whether a design is sound; asking what
fraction of them track a known technical factor tells you a great deal.

```{python}
#| label: soln-12-4
matched = two_group_p(["treated2", "treated3"], ["untreated3", "untreated4"])
matched_hits = set(matched[matched < 0.05].index)
print(f"library-type-matched contrast: {len(matched_hits):,} genes at p < 0.05 "
      f"(confounded: {len(conf_hits):,})")
print(f"overlap with the pure library-type list: "
      f"{100 * len(matched_hits & batch_hits) / len(matched_hits):.1f}%")
```

**5.** Accuracy falls slightly, to 0.907, but remains far above the 0.571
majority-class baseline on data with no signal at all. Selecting 500 genes
dilutes the individually most over-fitted ones, yet the selection step has still
seen every label, so the leak persists. The size of *k* is a second-order detail;
the position of the selection step is the problem.

```{python}
#| label: soln-12-5
rng_five = np.random.default_rng(7)
leaky_500 = [leaky_accuracy(X, rng_five.permutation(y), k=500) for _ in range(20)]
print(f"k=500, selection outside CV, 20 permutations: "
      f"mean accuracy {np.mean(leaky_500):.3f}")
```

**6.** Recall rises and precision falls. In screening, a missed case is usually
costlier than a false alarm that a confirmatory test will resolve, so trading
precision for recall is defensible — provided the confirmatory test exists and
the false-alarm burden is affordable.

```{python}
#| label: soln-12-6
for threshold in (0.5, 0.1):
    called = (scores_test >= threshold).astype(int)
    print(f"threshold {threshold}: precision "
          f"{precision_score(y_test, called, zero_division=0):.3f}  "
          f"recall {recall_score(y_test, called):.3f}  "
          f"positives called {called.sum()}")
```

**7.** The class balance is 55:5, so predicting "responder" for everyone scores
91.7% — almost the reported figure. The questions: what is the majority-class
baseline; were the 40 genes selected inside or outside the cross-validation loop;
was there an independent cohort; what are precision and recall for the minority
class; how were the five non-responders distributed across folds; and was any
normalisation or batch correction applied before splitting. The single most
diagnostic piece of information is the performance of the whole pipeline on
permuted labels.

```{python}
#| label: soln-12-7
print(f"majority-class baseline for 55 responders of 60: {55 / 60:.3f}")
```

**8.** With seven samples, a bootstrap resample of samples omits roughly a third
of them and duplicates others, so the clustering is computed on two or three
distinct profiles and the result reflects which samples were drawn rather than
the stability of the structure. Bootstrapping genes perturbs the distance
estimates while keeping all samples present, which is the relevant question here.
With 200 samples, resampling samples becomes the more informative test, because
it asks whether the cluster structure would survive in a different draw from the
same population — the question that matters for generalisation.
